Why Analytical TP-Decode Latency Models Fail in Eager LLM Serving

To predict one decode step under tensor parallelism, the natural model shards the single-GPU compute so it falls as one over k and adds the all-reduce cost the shards must pay. The all-reduce term, calibrated in isolation, is faithful. The composition is not: it is accurate at TP-2 and fails at TP-4, because the measured step barely shrinks as GPUs are added. The paper presents that negative result as the contribution and localizes the missing term.

1. Why This Test, Why Now

This section walks a reader who has never seen the model into why the test is both necessary and decisive, before any of the paper's own findings appear. The argument is built from the deployment reality of serving 70B-class models in 2025, one concrete way the untested composition misleads an operator, the size of the error the status quo leaves open, the obvious fix that does not settle the question, and the structural reason a simple subtraction is the right instrument.

1.1 Why now: tensor parallelism is the precondition for serving 70B at all

The largest open models that operators actually deploy no longer fit on a single GPU. A BF16 Llama-3.1-70B checkpoint is roughly 140 GB of weights, well past the 80 GB of an H100 and the 141 GB of an H200 once activations, the KV cache, and CUDA workspace are accounted for. Tensor parallelism (TP) is therefore not an optional tuning knob for 70B-class serving; it is the precondition for serving the model at all, sharding each layer across $k$ GPUs and recombining the shards with an all-reduce after the attention block and again after the MLP block. Yet the per-step decode latency models operators rely on for capacity planning were built and validated entirely at the single-GPU case. The question of how a step costs at TP-2 versus TP-4, and how much of it shrinks with $k$, is exactly the question those single-GPU models cannot answer, and it is the question that arises the moment a 70B model is deployed.

1.2 A concrete failure: the model under-budgets a TP-4 fleet

Consider a capacity planner responsible for a chat fleet whose admission controller budgets one 33-token decode trace at context 2048 by subtracting a 1-token prefill wall and dividing the remaining wall time over 32 decode steps. On H100 TP-2 the planner measures a 27.8 ms decode step for Llama-3.1-70B and asks whether TP-4 can cut that per-token service time enough to admit the next batch without missing the budget. The additive composition encourages exactly this expectation: it shards the compute as one over k, so it predicts a TP-4 step near 14.5 ms, almost a factor-of-two reduction as GPU count doubles. The measurement contradicts this directly: the trusted H100 TP-4 grid measures 25.6 to 26.7 ms per step, and the model under-predicts every TP-4 cell by 32 to 45 percent. A planner who sizes replicas from the model would allocate twice the GPUs per replica, admit traffic against nonexistent headroom, and discover the shortfall only after deployment.

1.3 The baseline pain: a 42% under-prediction at TP-4, and a step that does not shrink

Before any improvement is introduced, the status-quo error is worth stating plainly. On the trusted H100 decode grid the additive composition reaches a mean absolute percentage error of 2.86 percent at TP-2, which would read as a working model in isolation, but a mean absolute percentage error of 42.3 percent at TP-4, where it under-predicts all twelve cells. The single number behind that failure is the step-shrink ratio: from TP-2 to TP-4 the measured decode step contracts by only about 1.12 times, against the factor of two the one-over-k compute term assumes. Roughly fourteen milliseconds of compute that the model says TP-4 should save never appears in the wall clock. A 42 percent under-prediction on the higher-GPU configuration is large enough that the composition cannot be used for the cross-TP planning question it was meant to answer.

1.4 The strawman: just re-fit a per-step constant at TP-4

An obvious response is to add a free per-step constant and re-fit it directly against the TP-4 measurements, then declare the model repaired. This does not settle the scientific question the paper poses. The composition's structural claim is that the non-communication remainder of a decode step shrinks as one over k; a per-step constant calibrated at TP-2 is the model's own probe of that claim, and the paper shows it does not transfer. The implied per-step residual is about minus 2.5 milliseconds at TP-2 but an implied plus 8.0 milliseconds at TP-4, a shift of roughly ten milliseconds as k grows. Re-fitting the constant separately at each TP degree would absorb that shift into a free parameter and hide precisely the structural failure the test is meant to expose, turning a falsified mechanism into a calibration table with no predictive reach across the configurations an operator has not yet measured.

1.5 The fundamental obstacle: a correct communication term is not sufficient

The structural difficulty is that getting the network model right does not rescue the composition. It would be natural to suspect that the failure lives in the all-reduce term, since communication is the visibly new cost that tensor parallelism introduces. The paper closes that escape route by calibrating the all-reduce cost in isolation from a standalone NCCL microbenchmark and confirming it is faithful: the closed form fits the message-size sweep to a high global $R^2$ above 0.999, and at TP-2 the communication accounts for only about 12 percent of the measured step. With the network term measured and small, the 42 percent shortfall at TP-4 cannot be a communication error. The missing physics is therefore on the compute-or-host side of the step, the part the composition assumed would shrink with k, which means the test must isolate that remainder rather than refine the network model.

1.6 Position vs prior work

The page sits among four concrete prior works. Megatron-LM introduced the tensor-parallel sharding and the two-all-reduce-per-layer pattern this paper times, but it describes the mechanism without a closed-form per-step latency prediction. Vidur handles tensor parallelism by fitting per-operator random-forest runtime regressors that must be re-fit for each configuration, so it can predict TP-4 only after TP-4 has been measured, which is the opposite of the refit-free composition under test here. LLMCompass and the analytical communication frameworks ASTRA-sim and Calculon model the collective cost analytically inside a design-space simulator, but they treat the network in detail while leaving the eager per-step host-and-dispatch remainder out of the predicted serving step. None of the four reports, on measured eager-serving decode steps, that the natural additive composition fails and localizes why. What they leave on the table is an honest confrontation between the seemingly obvious one-over-k composition and real TP-decode measurements, which is what this paper supplies.

2. The Whole Story in One Minute

With the motivation established, the result can be summarized in one pass. To serve a 70B model, tensor parallelism splits each layer across $k$ GPUs, and after each attention and MLP block the shards exchange their partial results in a collective all-reduce. The natural latency model adds two terms: the single-GPU compute divided by $k$, because each GPU now does a fraction of the work, plus the all-reduce cost the recombination requires. The paper calibrates the all-reduce term on its own and confirms it is faithful, then tests the full composition against real eager-mode vLLM decode measurements on H100 and H200. The consequence is a primarily negative result alongside one solid positive component, summarized below.

The four cards below separate the result into the validated communication term, the falsified additive composition, the localized per-step residual that explains the failure, and the same-node control that rules out the obvious alternative reading.

Positive: a refit-free all-reduce model

The closed form $\lambda + \tfrac{2(k-1)}{k}\,b/\beta$ fits a standalone NCCL message-size sweep to a global $R^2$ above 0.999 on all three configurations, recovering a per-call latency of 21 to 25 microseconds and an effective ring bandwidth of 307 to 335 GB/s, with no fit to any serving trace. This term is the part the paper recommends reusing.

Negative (headline): the composition does not predict TP-decode latency

On the trusted H100 grid the additive composition is accurate at TP-2 (2.86% MAPE) but fails its pre-registered 15% kill-switch at TP-4 (42.3% MAPE), under-predicting all twelve TP-4 cells by 32 to 45 percent. The paper does not retro-fit a correction and does not present the TP-2 pass as a working model.

The diagnosis: a per-step term that does not shrink with k

Subtracting the measured communication term and the predicted one-over-k compute leaves a per-step residual that is not constant in $k$: about minus 2.5 milliseconds at TP-2 but an implied plus 8.0 milliseconds at TP-4, a roughly ten-millisecond upward shift. This is presented as evidence, consistent with a non-shrinking eager host or dispatch cost, not as a proven mechanism.

De-confounded: it is TP scaling, not node variance

Because the original H100 TP-2 and TP-4 grids ran on different nodes, a dedicated single-node control measured both degrees back-to-back on one H100 node. The failure reproduces, with a step-shrink of 1.08 times and a TP-4 MAPE of 44 percent, so the negative result is a genuine TP-scaling effect and not a slow or co-tenanted TP-4 node.

Pre-registered kill-switch — fired. The test asked, before measurement, whether the additive form matches the measured TP scaling within 15 percent. It does not: the form misses H100 TP-4 by 42.3 percent MAPE, passing only at TP-2. As pre-specified, the paper reports the negative outcome as the result rather than tuning a correction until the form passes.

3. What Happens in One TP Decode Step

To read the result, one needs a clear picture of where time goes in a single tensor-parallel decode step. This section builds that picture in plain terms before any equation: what tensor parallelism does to the work, what the all-reduce is for, and why one might reasonably expect the step to shrink as GPUs are added. The expectation built here is exactly the one the measurement later overturns.

3.1 Sharding the layer, then recombining with an all-reduce

In tensor parallelism each transformer layer's projection matrices and attention heads are split column- or row-wise across the $k$ GPUs, so every GPU holds a slice of the weights and computes a slice of the result. Because the slices are partial, they must be summed back together before the next sub-layer can proceed, and that summation is a collective all-reduce over the $k$ ranks. A Llama-3.1-70B layer performs two such all-reduces, one after the attention block and one after the MLP block, and the model has 80 layers, so a single decode step issues 160 all-reduce collectives on the critical path. The intuition the additive model encodes is that splitting the weights across twice as many GPUs should halve the per-GPU compute, while the all-reduce adds a comparatively small recombination cost on top.

1. Sharded compute (the model expects this to shrink as 1/k)

Each GPU computes its slice of the attention and MLP projections. With twice the GPUs, the model assumes each does half the work.
↓

2. All-reduce after attention (160 collectives per step at 80 layers)

The partial slices are summed across ranks over NVLink. This is the new cost tensor parallelism adds, and the paper measures it directly.
↓

3. All-reduce after MLP

A second collective recombines the MLP slices, completing one layer; this repeats 80 times per decode step.
↓

4. Host and dispatch work (assumed negligible or shrinking)

In eager mode the Python runtime launches every kernel and collective each step. The additive model has no growing term here; the measurement suggests this is where the missing time lives.

3.2 Eager mode keeps the host on the critical path

The measurements are taken in eager mode, the execution mode in which the Python runtime issues each kernel and each collective launch individually on every step rather than replaying a captured CUDA graph. This matters because in eager mode the kernel-dispatch and collective-launch work sits on the critical path and is paid afresh on every decode step. When the GPU count doubles, the compute slice each GPU owns may halve, but the number of launches the host must issue does not fall, and the 160 collective launches per step are if anything more numerous to coordinate. The paper does not assert in advance which component dominates; it builds the additive model on the assumption that the non-communication remainder shrinks with $k$ and then asks the measurement whether that assumption holds.

The eager-mode choice is deliberate and is also the boundary of the claim. A serving stack that captures CUDA graphs would fold most of the per-step host launches into a single replay, which could change both the level and the scaling of the residual the paper isolates. The negative result here is a statement about eager-mode TP decode, the mode in which the host stays on the critical path.

4. The Composition Under Test, and How It Is Measured

The method has two halves that mirror the two terms of the model. The first is the closed-form all-reduce cost, calibrated in isolation so the communication term is never tuned against serving data. The second is the full additive composition, confronted with a measured eager-mode decode grid through a pre-registered kill-switch. Each half is described in concept before its equation appears.

4.1 The all-reduce term: a textbook closed form, calibrated alone

The communication cost of a ring all-reduce over $k$ ranks is captured by a textbook model with two physical constants: a fixed per-call latency $\lambda$ that every collective pays once, and a bandwidth-limited transfer time that depends on the message size $b$ and the effective ring bandwidth $\beta$. The leading factor reflects that a ring all-reduce moves roughly twice the message volume across the link as the rank count grows. The constants are recovered by a standalone NCCL microbenchmark that sweeps the message size from 32 KiB to 256 MiB on the same NVLink allocation the 70B run uses, so the bandwidth that calibrates the term is the bandwidth the serving step actually sees.

(1) $$T_{\mathrm{allreduce}}(b,k) \;=\; \lambda \;+\; \frac{2(k-1)}{k}\,\frac{b}{\beta}$$

Fitting Equation (1) to the message-size sweep recovers a per-call latency $\lambda$ of 21 to 25 microseconds and an effective ring bandwidth $\beta$ of 307 to 335 GB/s, with a global $R^2$ above 0.999 on all three configurations. One honest caveat applies and the paper discloses it: the high global $R^2$ is dominated by the large messages in the sweep, and at the 32 to 256 KiB payloads a 70B decode step actually produces, the linear fit over-predicts the raw per-call NCCL time by about 7 to 10 percent on H100 TP-2 and by about 22 to 27 percent on H100 TP-4. The term is therefore a faithful first-order standalone description of the collective, with a known small-message bias rather than a perfect match in the decode-relevant regime.

4.2 The full composition and the pre-registered kill-switch

The composition under test takes the single-GPU compute model, divides the sharded shapes by $k$ so the compute term falls as one over $k$, and adds the two all-reduces per layer across all 80 layers. A single per-step constant $R_0$, calibrated from one TP-2 cell, stands in for any fixed dispatch floor. The kill-switch was specified before the decode measurements were read: the composition passes if its predicted TP scaling matches the measured step within 15 percent mean absolute percentage error, and otherwise the paper reports the negative outcome without retro-fitting a correction.

(2) $$T_{\mathrm{step}}(k) \;\stackrel{?}{=}\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{k}\,T_{\mathrm{compute}}(1)}_{\text{assumed to shrink as }1/k} \;+\; \underbrace{2\,N_{\mathrm{layers}}\,T_{\mathrm{allreduce}}(b,k)}_{\text{added, does not shrink}} \;+\; R_0$$

The question mark over the equality is the whole paper. Equation (2) is the hypothesis, not a result, and the kill-switch is the instrument that decides it. Because the all-reduce term is calibrated independently and the per-step constant is fit from a single cell rather than tuned per configuration, a failure cannot be blamed on an over-flexible network model or on hidden free parameters; it has to come from the structural assumption that the one-over-$k$ compute term carries the step.

4.3 The decode-subtraction instrument and its fragility

The per-decode-step latency is obtained by a difference method: the wall-clock time of a 33-token generation minus the wall-clock time of a 1-token generation, divided by 32. The 1-token wall is meant to capture exactly the prefill, so that subtracting it leaves a clean steady-state decode step. The paper treats this as a first-class measurement threat rather than a detail, because when the 1-token wall instead spills extra time, from a long prefill, scheduler warm-up, or KV allocation that does not amortize the same way, the subtraction is contaminated and the per-step value is wrong. The grid is therefore gated on this fragility, and the negative result is read only off cells where the 1-token wall is small and the per-step values are tight across the twelve timed repetitions.

5. What the Measurements Show

Three pre-specified claims resolve in this section: one passes, one fails as the headline, and the third explains the failure. They are reported in that order, with the same-node control closing off the obvious alternative reading of the headline. Every number below is a landed PACE measurement, and predictions are labelled as predictions throughout.

5.1 The communication term is validated as a first-order standalone term

The residual of the measured H100 TP-2 step over the predicted sharded compute is small and positive, in the range of 0.14 to 1.87 milliseconds across batch sizes at context 2048, and it is the same order as the microbenchmark-predicted communication time of about 3.45 to 3.51 milliseconds. At TP-2 the communication term is about 11.7 to 12.5 percent of the measured step. The all-reduce fit is a faithful, serving-trace-free description of the collective cost, validated as a first-order standalone term and recommended for reuse, with the disclosed small-message over-prediction as its one known limitation.

5.2 The composition fails the kill-switch at TP-4 (the headline)

On the clean H100 grid the composed model achieves a mean absolute percentage error of 2.86 percent at TP-2 but 42.3 percent at TP-4, where it under-predicts every one of the twelve cells by 32 to 45 percent. 5.3 Why it fails: the step does not shrink with k

The mechanism model predicts the per-GPU compute halves from TP-2 to TP-4, falling from about 27.6 to about 13.8 milliseconds. The measured step does not follow: the raw H100 TP-2 to TP-4 step ratio is only about 1.12, far from the ideal 2.00. Subtracting the predicted communication term and the one-parameter per-step constant exposes the shape of the gap. The implied per-step residual is not constant in $k$: it is about minus 2.5 milliseconds at TP-2 but an implied plus 8.0 milliseconds at TP-4, so the constant that fits TP-2 does not transfer to TP-4. The additive decomposition is structurally insufficient because it assumes a per-step term fixed in $k$, while the data say that term shifts by roughly ten milliseconds as $k$ grows. This non-transfer, and not the communication term, is the negative result.

5.4 The same-node control: the failure is not node variance

The original H100 TP-2 and TP-4 grids ran on different nodes, which leaves one alternative reading open: the TP-4 under-prediction could be a slow or co-tenanted TP-4 node rather than a TP-scaling effect, exactly the kind of node pathology the paper had to exclude for H200. To settle this, a dedicated same-node control measured TP-2 and TP-4 back-to-back on the single H100 node, with the identical NCCL sweep and 70B decode grid. The same-node NCCL fit is internally consistent with the main runs, recovering an effective bandwidth of 308 and 339 GB/s at TP-2 and TP-4, though its TP-4 fit is slightly looser at $R^2$ of 0.9969, just below the main fits' 0.999, and is not implied to match them exactly.

The original failure reproduces on the single node. The composed model again tracks TP-2 to a mean absolute percentage error of 2.2 percent and again fails hard at TP-4 with a mean absolute percentage error of 44.4 percent, under-predicting every clean cell by 42 to 46 percent. The mechanism is identical: across the ten clean same-node cells the measured TP-2 to TP-4 step-shrink is only about 1.08 times, against the ideal 2.00, and the implied per-step residual makes the same shift it does cross-node, from about minus 2.6 milliseconds at TP-2 to about plus 8.7 milliseconds at TP-4. Because there is only one node, the TP-4 failure cannot be cross-node variance, so the failure of the composition is a genuine TP-scaling effect.

6. Why a Negative Result Is Worth Reading

The value of this paper is that it is a faithful negative characterization rather than a tuned model. It tells the next person where not to spend effort, hands them one reusable term, and localizes the missing physics precisely enough to aim the follow-up experiment. The three subsections below state what the reader should take away, what remains an inference rather than a proof, and what the follow-up study should measure.

6.1 The takeaway: aim at the per-step term, not the network

The single most useful consequence is a redirection of effort. A practitioner extending a single-GPU decode model to tensor parallelism would naturally invest in a more detailed network model, because communication is the visibly new cost. This paper shows that investment is misplaced for eager TP decode: the all-reduce term is already faithful and accounts for only about an eighth of the step at TP-2, while the 42 percent error at TP-4 comes entirely from a non-communication per-step term that the additive form assumed would shrink with $k$ and does not. The reusable artifact is the refit-free all-reduce model, which a follow-up can adopt as-is while concentrating its modeling effort on the per-step remainder.

6.2 What stays an inference: the cause is a candidate, not a proof

The roughly ten-millisecond upward shift of the per-step residual is the evidence the paper presents, and it is reported as evidence rather than as a proven mechanism. The shift is consistent with a non-shrinking per-step host or dispatch cost, since in eager mode with no CUDA-graph capture the 160 collective launches and the kernel dispatch sit on the critical path and do not fold away when ranks are added. It is, however, only one explanation among several: the vLLM tensor-parallel implementation, the in-model NCCL protocol assumption, and scheduling effects are each plausible contributors. No Nsight Systems traces were collected, so the paper localizes the residual and names the candidates without claiming a single proven cause.

6.3 What the follow-up should measure

The characterization sets up a concrete follow-up. A CUDA-event or Nsight-timed per-step cross-check at both TP degrees would confirm which candidate dominates the residual shift, a measurement neither the original runs nor the same-node control provide. A fresh-node H200 rerun would extend the conclusion beyond the single H100 SKU the decode grid currently trusts, since the original H200 node was excluded as pathological. Together these would convert the present inference into a mechanism, but they are deliberately framed as future work; the paper reports the negative outcome as the result, not as a step toward a model it has not yet validated.

7. Limitations — Read Before Citing

The conclusions are deliberately bounded, and the boundaries are part of the contribution. The strengths and limitations below should be read together: the negative result is robust within its scope, and the scope is stated plainly so the claim is neither over- nor under-read.

Strengths

  • The kill-switch was pre-registered, and the negative outcome is reported without retro-fitting a correction.
  • The communication term is calibrated in isolation, so a failure cannot be hidden in an over-flexible network model.
  • A same-node control reproduces the failure on one node, ruling out cross-node variance as the cause.
  • The NCCL benchmark, decode harness, analysis, and sbatch scripts are released with the paper.

Limitations

  • The decode conclusion is H100-only. The H200 decode grid is excluded as pathological, with the node running identical prefill 6 to 12 times slower than H100; only its NCCL fit is retained.
  • Only two TP degrees were measured, so the TP-2 pass is one data point and is not presented as a working model.
  • The cause is an inference. No Nsight traces were collected; the non-shrinking host cost is one candidate among several.
  • The result is for eager mode; a CUDA-graph serving stack could change both the level and the scaling of the residual.
The all-reduce fit's headline $R^2$ above 0.999 is a global figure dominated by the large messages in the sweep. At the 32 to 256 KiB payloads a decode step actually produces, the linear fit over-predicts the raw collective by about 7 to 27 percent. The communication model should be read as a faithful first-order term with this disclosed small-message bias, not as an exact match in the decode regime.
