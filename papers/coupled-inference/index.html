Characterizing & Optimizing LLM Inference on Coupled CPU-GPU Architectures

A trace-driven study of how the choice between PCIe-discrete (LC), NVLink-C2C (CC), and on-package (TC) CPU-GPU coupling shifts the inference bottleneck — with TKLQT as the diagnostic metric and proximity-score kernel fusion as the remedy

arXiv:2504.11750 Preprint 2025 A100 · H100 · GH200 4 LLM workloads Prefill latency PyTorch 2.4.1 · nsys

Prabhu Vellaisamy, Thomas Labonte, Sourav Chakraborty, Matt Turner, Samantika Sury, John Paul Shen

Carnegie Mellon University · Samsung Semiconductor, Inc. · Hewlett Packard Enterprise

1. Problem: Faster Interconnects Move the Bottleneck, They Do Not Remove It

Data-centre LLM inference is in the middle of a hardware transition: the PCIe-connected discrete-CPU-discrete-GPU machine that defined the 2022 generation is giving way to closely-coupled designs in which a Grace-class Arm CPU sits on the same board as a Hopper-class GPU, and ultimately to tightly-coupled APUs such as AMD MI300A that put the CPU and GPU on the same die. Each step in this progression promises higher interconnect bandwidth — from PCIe Gen5's roughly 128 GB/s lanes to NVLink-C2C's 900 GB/s — and the marketing case for the upgrade is that LLM inference latency will fall by the same multiple. The data in this paper shows that the truth is more nuanced. Tighter coupling does win at large batch sizes, but it shifts the inference bottleneck onto a different resource, and at the latency-sensitive batch sizes that real interactive applications use, the upgrade can leave throughput unchanged or even invert the ranking.

Figure 1 (from paper): Evolution of the CPU-GPU coupling paradigm from discrete PCIe interconnect (LC, left), through on-board NVLink-C2C with a unified virtual address space (CC, centre), to on-package integration with a shared physical memory pool (TC, right). The interconnect tightens monotonically; the data in this paper shows that the inference bottleneck does not.

1.1 Why Now: Three Generations of Coupling Stacked in One Fleet

The execution profile of a 2025 inference deployment is no longer the one a 2022 GPU-centric study calibrated against. Three changes matter together. First, the dominant model class on inference servers is no longer the dense encoder-only network that fit cleanly into a compute-bound regime; production fleets now mix encoder-only models such as BERT-Base and XLM-RoBERTa-Base with decoder-only LLMs such as GPT-2 and Llama-3.2-1B, and the small decoder workloads in particular dispatch many small kernels per output token. Second, the serving stack itself has grown a high-level runtime layer; vLLM and Orca now own batching and KV-cache state on top of PyTorch 2.x, and dynamic shapes keep the runtime in eager mode for most production paths. Third, NVIDIA's GH200 superchip pairs a 72-core Arm Neoverse V2 Grace CPU with a Hopper GPU over a 900 GB/s NVLink-C2C link — a roughly seven-fold step over PCIe Gen5 — and is being deployed alongside the older LC machines, so the question of which platform to send a given workload to is now a live operational decision rather than a procurement abstraction.

1.2 A Concrete Bad Scenario: BERT-Base-Uncased at Small Batch on GH200

Consider an operator who has acquired a fleet of GH200 superchips with the goal of accelerating an interactive embedding service backed by BERT-Base-Uncased at sequence length 512. The marketing material describes a 7$\times$ bandwidth improvement over PCIe Gen5, and the operator expects to see a corresponding latency reduction across all batch sizes. The measured result inverts that expectation at the operating point that matters. At batch size one — the latency-critical configuration benchmarked by MLPerf Inference SingleStream as representative of chatbots and conversational AI agents — the GH200 platform delivers a prefill latency of roughly 18 ms, while the Intel + H100 LC platform delivers about 11 ms. The GH200 is 1.7$\times$ slower at this batch size, and the cross-over point at which it begins to outperform the LC platform is delayed all the way to batch size 16. For the first sixteen requests of every interactive burst, the supposedly faster machine is the slower one, and the operator's interactive SLO is no longer met.

1.3 The Quantified Baseline Pain

The paper supplies the measurements that justify treating this cross-over point as a first-order operational problem. Across the four evaluation workloads — BERT-Base-Uncased, XLM-RoBERTa-Base, GPT-2, and Llama-3.2-1B — the GH200 remains CPU-bound up to a 4$\times$ larger batch size than the LC systems before transitioning to a GPU-bound regime. For encoder-only models specifically, the CPU-bound to GPU-bound transition occurs around batch size 8 on Intel+H100 and AMD+A100, but is delayed to roughly batch size 32 on GH200 — so over the entire small-batch interactive operating range the GH200 spends its wall-clock time waiting for kernel launches rather than executing them. At batch size 64 the picture inverts and the GH200 delivers 1.6$\times$ to 2.4$\times$ lower prefill latency for BERT-Base-Uncased; for Llama-3.2-1B the inversion is even sharper, with 1.9$\times$ to 2.7$\times$ lower prefill latency at batch size 16. A workload that benefits from a tighter coupling at batch size 64 and is punished by it at batch size 1 is one whose placement decision cannot be made on coupling alone.

1.4 Strawman: Trust the End-to-End Latency Curve

A reasonable first response is to pick the platform by inspecting the end-to-end latency curve at the target batch size and leaving the diagnosis at that. The difficulty is that the end-to-end curve hides the structural cause of the cross-over: when GH200 is the slower platform at batch size one, the curve cannot tell the operator whether the gap is because the GPU itself is slower, because the Arm CPU is slower per single-thread, because the runtime stack on Arm is less mature, or because the higher unified-memory bandwidth is irrelevant in this regime. Each of these hypotheses points to a different remediation — a kernel-fusion pass, a CPU upgrade, a software-stack tuning effort, or a runtime swap to torch.compile — and committing engineering effort to any of them without an underlying diagnosis has an uncontrolled expected cost because the chosen intervention may target the wrong subsystem. The end-to-end curve flags the problem; it does not name it.

1.5 The Fundamental Obstacle: Existing Profilers See Either Kernels or Operators, Not Both

The reason the end-to-end curve cannot be split into named contributions is structural and not a matter of more careful logging. Nsight Systems exposes per-kernel timestamps on the GPU side but cannot resolve which PyTorch ATen operator on the CPU side issued each kernel; the PyTorch Profiler exposes per-operator timings but reports CUDA kernels only in aggregate. Nsight Compute provides intra-kernel detail but is by design GPU-only, so the interaction between CPU dispatch and GPU execution is recorded in two separate views that share no identifier. A diagnosis of CPU-vs-GPU boundedness therefore cannot be assembled from existing tools without bridging the two streams kernel-by-kernel — and that bridge is precisely what no shipped profiler builds for the operator. The paper's response is to construct that bridge as a profiling artifact, SKIP, and to derive from it a metric — Total Kernel Launch and Queuing Time (TKLQT) — that resolves PU-boundedness directly from per-kernel attribution.

2. Contributions

The five contribution cards below summarise what this paper adds on top of the gap identified in §1. The first card is the experimental scaffold; the next three are the methodological artifacts (a profiler, a metric, and a recommender) that the scaffold makes possible; the last is the surprising measurement finding that the artifacts surface. The headline insight that follows the cards restates the operational consequence in one sentence, so a reader who skips the rest of the page leaves with the load-bearing claim of the work.

Contribution 1: Three-Paradigm Inference Benchmark

Apples-to-apples prefill-latency measurements across the LC (AMD+A100, Intel+H100) and CC (GH200) paradigms on four LLM workloads — BERT-Base-Uncased, XLM-RoBERTa-Base, GPT-2, Llama-3.2-1B — over batch sizes from 1 to 64.

Contribution 2: TKLQT Metric

Total Kernel Launch and Queuing Time, the sum over all kernels of the interval from cudaLaunchKernel issuance to GPU execution start, serves as a fine-grained PU-boundedness classifier that locates the CPU-bound to GPU-bound transition.

Contribution 3: SKIP Profiler

A PyTorch-based operator-kernel inference profiler that constructs a dependency graph spanning ATen operators and CUDA kernels using CUPTI correlation identifiers, exposing the per-kernel launch latency $t_l$ that TKLQT aggregates.

Contribution 4: Proximity-Score Fusion Recommender

A kernel-fusion recommendation method that scores candidate chains by their proximity score $PS(C) = f(C)/f(k_i) \in (0,1]$ and recommends a chain only when $PS(C) = 1$, isolating deterministic sequences that can be safely fused.

Contribution 5: Grace CPU Single-Thread Finding

A measurement that isolates the Grace CPU's single-thread performance as the cause of GH200's elevated small-batch latency: encoder-only models are 4$\times$ more CPU-bound on GH200 than on LC platforms, with the bottleneck attributable to the Arm Neoverse V2 CPU rather than the Hopper GPU.

Key insight: Tighter CPU-GPU coupling is a necessary but not sufficient condition for lower inference latency. At large batches, where the GPU is the bottleneck, tighter coupling delivers the expected 1.9–2.7$\times$ latency improvement for decoder LLMs. At small batches, where the launch path is the bottleneck, the same coupling can shift the bottleneck onto the host CPU and erase the benefit. The decisive question for any given workload is which side of the CPU-bound to GPU-bound transition the operating point sits on, and TKLQT is the metric that answers that question per kernel.

3. Three CPU-GPU Coupling Paradigms

Before the measurement results can be read meaningfully, the three coupling paradigms have to be distinguished by what they actually share. The classification used throughout the paper turns on the physical proximity of the CPU and GPU and on the structure of the memory pool they reach. Each paradigm pays a different orchestration price, and each unlocks a different class of optimisation; the inference workload's batch-size operating point then determines which price is dominant and which optimisation is on the table.

2.1 Loosely-Coupled (LC) — Discrete PCIe-Connected PUs

LC machines pair a discrete x86 CPU with a discrete NVIDIA GPU over a PCIe Gen5 link. Each side has its own memory pool; data movement between them is explicit and bounded by roughly 128 GB/s of interconnect bandwidth. The paper evaluates two LC platforms: an AMD EPYC 7313 16-core CPU paired with an A100-SXM4-80GB GPU, and a 2-socket Intel Xeon Platinum 8468V (48-core) CPU paired with an H100 over PCIe Gen5. Both LC platforms run with the same evaluation protocol — PyTorch 2.4.1, CUDA 12.6, NVIDIA driver 560.35.03 — so that any latency difference observed across them attributes to CPU or GPU hardware rather than to software configuration drift.

2.2 Closely-Coupled (CC) — NVIDIA GH200 with NVLink-C2C

The CC paradigm is represented by the NVIDIA GH200 superchip, which integrates a 72-core Arm Neoverse V2 Grace CPU with a Hopper H100 GPU on the same board. The two PUs communicate over the NVLink-C2C interconnect, which provides up to 900 GB/s of bidirectional bandwidth — approximately seven times the PCIe Gen5 link used in the LC platforms. The Grace CPU is paired with 480 GB of LPDDR5X memory, the Hopper GPU with 96 GB of HBM3, and NVLink-C2C exposes both memory pools to both PUs under a single unified virtual address space. The two memory pools remain physically distinct, so each forms a non-uniform-memory-access (NUMA) domain, but the dispatch path no longer needs to mediate explicit copies: a kernel reading host memory or a CPU operator touching device memory both incur an NVLink-C2C traversal rather than a PCIe DMA.

2.3 Tightly-Coupled (TC) — AMD MI300A and the On-Package Limit

The TC paradigm represents the further step of putting both processing units on the same package. The AMD Instinct MI300A APU combines AMD Zen4 x86 CPU cores with CDNA3 GPU units, stacked on a single substrate and backed by a unified pool of HBM3 shared coherently across both PU classes. There is no explicit CPU-to-GPU data transfer; both PU classes resolve every memory access against the same physical pool through eight on-package Infinity Fabric interfaces. TC is not benchmarked in this paper — the authors do not have an MI300A platform available — and it is included on the spectrum as the asymptote that LC and CC are converging towards rather than as a direct evaluation point.

2.4 What Each Coupling Paradigm Pays For

Each paradigm reshapes the cost structure of inference orchestration in a way that the operator must understand before reading the latency curves. The three columns below pin the resource each paradigm spends most on, the regime in which the spending is worthwhile, and the optimisation class that this paper's measurements show is the right response.

4. SKIP — A System-Aware Kernel Inference Profiler

Existing profilers split the inference timeline into two streams — operators on the CPU and kernels on the GPU — that share no first-class identifier. To produce the kernel-level metrics required by the cross-paradigm study, the authors build SKIP, a PyTorch-based profiler whose output is an operator-kernel dependency graph rather than two parallel timelines. The graph makes every CPU-side launch call directly addressable from the GPU-side kernel start, so the launch interval $t_l$ and the queuing interval that compose TKLQT can be measured per kernel rather than inferred from aggregate residuals.

Figure 4 (from paper): Operator-kernel execution timing. A parent ATen operator $p_i$ on the CPU thread emits child operators $c_{i1}, c_{i2}, \ldots$ that each issue a cudaLaunchKernel call $l_i$. The launch latency $t_{l_i}$ is the interval from the start of $l_i$ to the start of the corresponding kernel $k_i$ on the GPU stream. SKIP threads CPU operators, launch calls, and GPU kernels into a single dependency graph via CUPTI correlation identifiers, so $t_{l_i}$ is measured directly rather than estimated as a residual.

3.1 Building the Operator-Kernel Dependency Graph

SKIP runs the model under the PyTorch Profiler with CUPTI enabled, captures timestamped CPU operators, CUDA runtime calls, and GPU kernels, and processes the resulting trace to build a directed graph. Every parent ATen operator $p$ becomes a node whose start timestamp $ts_b(p)$ and end timestamp $ts_e(p)$ bracket its host-side duration. Each child operator $c$ generated by $p$ is attached as a descendant; if $c$ issued a CUDA runtime call $l$, the call becomes a further node, and the GPU kernel $k$ launched by $l$ is linked through the CUPTI correlation identifier rather than by timestamp proximity. The correlation identifier is what makes the graph durable under autotuning: even if cuBLAS chooses a different SM90 GEMM variant on a re-run, the launch call and the kernel still share the same correlation identifier, so the edge from $l$ to $k$ stays correct.

The pseudocode below paraphrases the construction algorithm described in Section IV of the paper rather than reproducing a source file; the paper does not link a public SKIP repository. It is given here so the reader can follow the structural relationship between the three trace streams (operators, CUDA calls, kernels) without re-reading the paper, and so the next subsection's discussion of correlation identifiers has a concrete reference point. Variable names are chosen to match the paper's notation.

# SKIP graph construction — pseudocode paraphrased from §IV of the paper (no public repo)
graph = DependencyGraph()
for p in aten_ops(trace):                # parent ATen ops on the CPU thread
    graph.add_node(p, ts_b(p), ts_e(p))
    for c in children_of(p):            # child operators
        graph.add_edge(p, c)
        for l in cuda_runtime_calls(c):  # every cudaLaunchKernel
            graph.add_node(l, ts_b(l), ts_e(l))
            graph.add_edge(c, l)
            k = kernel_by_correlation_id(l.cid)  # CUPTI link
            if k is not None:
                graph.add_edge(l, k)
                # Per-kernel launch latency for TKLQT (Eq. 1)
                t_l = ts_b(k) - ts_b(l)
                graph.attribute[k]["t_l"] = t_l
return graph

The output is a graph in which one full forward step appears as a tree rooted at the top-level ATen call, with the leaves being GPU kernels and the edges carrying both timing and correlation metadata. The per-kernel launch latency $t_{l_i} = ts_b(k_i) - ts_b(l_i)$ falls out of the graph as a direct measurement, not as the residual of an end-to-end subtraction. This is the data shape that the subsequent metrics consume, and it is the practical difference between SKIP and the Nsight-plus-PyTorch-Profiler combination that an operator can assemble by hand: Nsight reports $ts_b(k_i)$, PyTorch Profiler reports $ts_b(l_i)$, but no shipped tool reports the join of the two streams in a structure that survives autotuning re-runs.

3.2 Why a Graph and Not a Table

A graph-shaped data product is what makes the subsequent fusion-recommendation step possible. A flat table of $(operator, kernel, latency)$ rows is sufficient for reporting top-$k$ slow kernels, but it cannot answer the question that the fusion recommender needs: which sequences of kernels co-occur deterministically every time a given parent operator is invoked? That question is a sub-graph isomorphism query over the operator-kernel dependency graph, and it is what the proximity score introduced in section 5 actually computes. By building SKIP as a graph rather than as a profiling extension, the authors retain the structural information that table-shaped profilers throw away — and that information is what unlocks the kernel-fusion remedy on top of the diagnostic.

5. From Launch Latency to PU-Boundedness — The TKLQT Metric

The dependency graph above exposes per-kernel timestamps, but it is a sequence of numbers, not a diagnosis. To turn the trace into an actionable verdict, the paper defines four derived metrics on top of the graph and singles out one of them — Total Kernel Launch and Queuing Time — as the classifier that names whether a workload is CPU-bound or GPU-bound at a given operating point. The remaining three metrics serve as the calibration around it.

4.1 Per-Kernel Launch Latency $t_{l_i}$ (Equation 1)

The kernel launch overhead of a single invocation is defined as the interval from the start of the CPU-side launch call $l_i$ to the start of the corresponding GPU kernel $k_i$. This window includes the CPU time spent inside cudaLaunchKernel, any driver overhead, and the data-transfer time when an implicit copy is required, but excludes the kernel's own execution time. The graph built by SKIP joins the two endpoints by correlation identifier rather than by timestamp proximity, so the interval is well-defined even when many kernels are in-flight on the GPU stream simultaneously.

(1) $$t_{l_i} \;=\; ts_b(k_i) - ts_b(l_i)$$

4.2 Total Kernel Launch and Queuing Time (Equation 2)

The workload-level metric is the sum of $t_{l_i}$ across all $n$ kernels in the inference step. This summation has two structural properties that distinguish TKLQT from the framework-bound vs compute-bound residual reported in prior overhead-attribution work. First, TKLQT captures both the launch cost — the CPU time inside the runtime call — and the queuing time — the time spent waiting for the GPU stream to admit the kernel — and therefore varies in a measurable way with both CPU and GPU pressure. Second, because $t_{l_i}$ is read off the graph rather than estimated from end-to-end residuals, TKLQT scales with kernel count rather than with workload runtime, so the metric remains stable when the same model is run at different batch sizes.

(2) $$\mathrm{TKLQT} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} t_{l_i}$$

4.3 Reading TKLQT — Two Regimes Separated by an Inflection Point

The diagnostic value of TKLQT comes from the shape of its curve as batch size increases on a fixed platform. At small batch sizes the GPU stream is under-subscribed; each kernel is admitted immediately on arrival, so $t_{l_i}$ reflects only the launch overhead and TKLQT remains approximately constant as batch size grows. This is the CPU-bound region: the wall-clock time of the inference step is set by the CPU's ability to issue cudaLaunchKernel calls, not by the GPU's ability to execute them. Above a workload-specific inflection point the GPU's execution rate falls behind the CPU's issue rate, queuing time starts to accumulate, and TKLQT grows roughly linearly with batch size. This is the GPU-bound region: the wall-clock time is now set by the GPU. The TKLQT curve therefore makes the CPU-bound to GPU-bound transition visible as a discrete inflection rather than as a smooth blend.

Figure 6 (from paper): TKLQT for the encoder models BERT-Base-Uncased and XLM-RoBERTa-Base on the three evaluation platforms, with input sequence length fixed at 512 and batch size swept from 1 to 64. The star markers indicate the inflection point at which the workload transitions from the CPU-bound (launch-dominated) region to the GPU-bound (queue-dominated) region. The transition occurs around batch size 8 for the LC systems (Intel+H100 and AMD+A100) but is delayed to roughly batch size 32 on the GH200 — a 4$\times$ wider CPU-bound region driven by the Grace CPU's single-thread performance.

4.4 Why TKLQT Improves on Framework-Tax Residual

Earlier overhead-attribution work classifies a workload as framework-bound or compute-bound by observing how end-to-end inference latency scales with batch size: a flat latency curve at small batch sizes is read as framework-bound. The construction is correct as a qualitative signal but quantitatively poor because it cannot say how much of the residual is launch overhead, how much is queuing, and how much is irreducible CPU dispatch. TKLQT improves on the residual in three ways. It is measured kernel-by-kernel from CUPTI correlation identifiers rather than estimated as a latency residual, so the value is not contaminated by GPU-side kernel-execution variance. It is structurally decomposable: launch cost and queuing time are two distinct named contributors that move in opposite directions across the inflection point, so a workload's regime is named rather than inferred. And it scales with kernel count rather than with workload runtime, so encoder-only vs decoder-only and dense vs MoE workloads can be compared on the same axis.

4.5 Companion Metrics — AKD, IL, and GPU Idle Time

Three companion metrics calibrate the regime named by TKLQT and are used together with it throughout the paper. The Average Kernel Duration (AKD), the mean GPU execution time per kernel, indicates whether kernels are lightweight (small AKD, encoder-like) or computationally intensive (large AKD, decoder-like). The Inference Latency (IL) measures the wall-clock interval from the first parent operator's start to the last kernel's end. The GPU Idle Time is the gap between IL and the sum of all kernel durations and is the cleanest direct evidence of CPU-bound operation — if the GPU is idle, no amount of additional GPU capacity will lower latency. These three metrics together pin the regime to a specific cause: a CPU-bound workload has low AKD, large GPU idle time, and TKLQT below the inflection point; a GPU-bound workload has high AKD, low GPU idle time, and TKLQT above the inflection point.

(3)–(5) $$\mathrm{AKD} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n} t_{k_j}}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{IL} = ts_e(k_n) - ts_b(p_1), \qquad \mathrm{GPU\ Idle\ Time} = \mathrm{IL} - \sum_{j=1}^{n} t_{k_j}$$

6. Proximity-Score Kernel Fusion Recommendation

The diagnostic above identifies CPU-bound region as the target for optimisation; the remedy is to reduce kernel count, and the safest reduction is kernel fusion. Existing fusion techniques are either domain-specific (FlashAttention-2 fuses self-attention kernels), or graph-level (torch.compile / CUDA Graphs require pre-specified static shapes and complete graph capture). Both classes leave a gap: the dispatcher in eager-mode PyTorch issues many small kernel chains that are deterministic at the operator level but invisible to either fusion strategy. The paper closes this gap by extracting deterministic kernel chains from the SKIP dependency graph, scoring each by a proximity score in $(0,1]$, and recommending fusion only when the chain is provably deterministic ($PS(C) = 1$).

Figure 2 (from paper): Three classes of kernel fusion granularity. (1) Kernel-to-kernel offload — the eager-mode default, no fusion. (2) Domain-specific operator fusion — for example FlashAttention fusing self-attention operators. (3) Entire graph offload — torch.compile or CUDA Graphs fusing larger subgraphs or the whole forward graph. Proximity-score fusion targets the middle of this spectrum: deterministic chains that are too short for whole-graph compilation but too repetitive to leave to the dispatcher.

5.1 Defining Kernel Sequences and Chains

A kernel sequence $S_j$ is a maximal run of GPU kernels emitted from the same CPU thread without an intervening dependency on a different operator's output. The dependency graph built by SKIP segments the trace into a collection of such sequences $S_1, S_2, \ldots, S_N$, each terminated by an explicit synchronisation barrier or by a control-flow point that the dispatcher cannot collapse. Within a sequence $S_j = [k_{j_1}, k_{j_2}, \ldots, k_{j_{n_j}}]$, every kernel chain $C$ of length $L$ is a contiguous sub-sequence $(k_i, k_{i+1}, \ldots, k_{i+L-1})$ that begins at some position $i$ inside the sequence. The fusion question is then: for a given chain $C$, how often does $C$ appear in the trace every time its leading kernel $k_i$ is launched?

5.2 The Proximity Score (Equation 6)

The proximity score formalises the question. For a chain $C = (k_i, k_{i+1}, \ldots, k_{i+L-1})$, let $f(C)$ denote the number of trace positions at which the chain occurs exactly in that order, and let $f(k_i)$ denote the number of trace positions at which the leading kernel $k_i$ is launched. The proximity score is the ratio.

(6) $$PS(C) \;=\; \frac{f(C)}{f(k_i)} \;\in\; (0, 1]$$

A proximity score of one indicates that the chain is deterministic: every time the leading kernel is launched, the remaining $L-1$ kernels follow in the same order without any branch or control-flow divergence. A proximity score of, say, 0.6 indicates that the chain is followed 60% of the time the leading kernel runs, and that the other 40% of invocations branch into a different sub-tree. The fusion recommender accepts only chains with $PS(C) = 1$, because only deterministic chains can be safely collapsed into a single kernel: a chain with branching would require the fused kernel to encode the control flow internally, which negates the launch-overhead saving.

5.3 Idealised Launch-Count Savings (Equations 7–8)

The fusion candidate set is then the set of chains with $PS(C) = 1$ that are mutually non-overlapping — that is, no two recommended chains share any kernel position. Let $K_{eager}$ denote the total number of kernel launches under eager execution, $C_{fused}$ the number of accepted chains of length $L$, and $K_{fused}$ the launch count after fusion. The fusion of $C_{fused}$ chains of length $L$ saves $C_{fused}(L-1)$ launches, because each fused chain collapses $L$ kernels into one, and the post-fusion launch count and ideal speedup are given by equations 7 and 8.

(7) $$K_{fused} \;=\; K_{eager} \;-\; C_{fused} \cdot (L - 1)$$
(8) $$\mathrm{Speedup} \;=\; \frac{K_{eager}}{K_{fused}}$$

The speedup expression is intentionally idealised: it assumes that the launch overhead per kernel is constant, that fusing $L$ kernels into one introduces no additional per-kernel cost, and that the GPU still has free stream capacity to admit the reduced kernel stream without queuing. The first two assumptions are the standard kernel-fusion accounting; the third is what the diagnostic above is good for. Fusion is recommended only in the CPU-bound region — where the GPU has free capacity and the launch path is the actual bottleneck — so the third assumption holds by construction. In the GPU-bound region the recommendation is suppressed, because reducing launch count would not shorten the wall-clock of an already saturated GPU stream.

5.4 What Proximity-Score Fusion Adds Over Domain-Specific Fusion

The position of proximity-score fusion against the two adjacent techniques — FlashAttention-2 on one side, torch.compile on the other — is what gives the method its operational value. Domain-specific fusion is pre-specified per operator class: a developer has to recognise the self-attention pattern, write a hand-tuned fused kernel, and accept that the fusion will only apply to that one pattern. Whole-graph fusion is general but requires the model to be captured under torch.compile, which sacrifices the dynamic shapes and dynamic control flow that production serving stacks rely on. Proximity-score fusion sits in the gap: it does not require a human to identify the pattern, does not require static graph capture, operates per-kernel-chain at the trace level, and reuses the existing eager dispatcher for everything outside the recommended chains. Its scope is narrower than whole-graph compilation, but it is the only fusion technique in the three that fires automatically on eager-mode traces and therefore on the dynamic-shape paths that dominate production.

For both models the speedup curve climbs through chain lengths up to roughly 256 — peaking at 2.7$\times$ for GPT-2 and 6.8$\times$ for XLM-RoBERTa-Base — before plateauing as the long-chain candidates become rare and the total kernel-launch budget is depleted.

Decoder-only inference dispatches more kernels per output token than encoder-only inference of comparable parameter count — the autoregressive structure introduces additional small kernels around KV-cache reads and writes, and the per-kernel arithmetic intensity is therefore lower. 6.4 Idealised Fusion Speedups in the CPU-Bound Region

When the proximity-score recommender is applied to the eager-mode traces in the CPU-bound region, the idealised speedups computed from equations 7 and 8 reach up to 2.7$\times$ for GPT-2 prefill at batch size 1 and 6.8$\times$ for XLM-RoBERTa-Base under the same conditions. These numbers are not realised speedups — they are the theoretical maximum that pure launch-count reduction would deliver if every accepted chain were collapsed into a single kernel without additional per-kernel cost. For comparison, the same XLM-RoBERTa-Base configuration under torch.compile achieves a 1.34$\times$ to 1.56$\times$ realised speedup over eager mode, but the compilation step requires up to 944.6$\times$ more compile time and disallows the dynamic shapes and dynamic KV-cache sizes that production serving relies on; the proximity-score path retains those degrees of freedom while delivering a comparable upper bound.

6.5 nullKernel Launch Floor — Why Hopper Pays a Small Tax

To separate the platform-level launch tax from workload-driven queuing, the paper measures the nullKernel launch overhead on each platform: the mean raw launch interval of an empty CUDA kernel, profiled under the same protocol. The result calibrates the lower bound of $t_{l_i}$ that no workload can fall below. On AMD+A100 the nullKernel overhead is 2260.5 ns; on Intel+H100 it is 2374.6 ns; on GH200 it is 2771.6 ns — a roughly 400–500 ns surcharge over the LC platforms. The surcharge is small in absolute terms, but it multiplies by the kernel count and is therefore the structural reason that small-batch encoder workloads on GH200, which dispatch hundreds of kernels per step, accumulate a launch tax that LC platforms do not pay. The Grace CPU's single-thread performance widens this tax further, but even with a perfect Grace CPU the platform-level floor would already be 17–22% higher than on the LC machines.

8. The Grace CPU as the Latency-Sensitive Bottleneck

The most operationally surprising finding in the paper is not that GH200 has lower latency at large batch sizes; that follows from the 7$\times$ NVLink-C2C bandwidth improvement and the on-board HBM3. The surprising finding is that the same machine has higher latency at small batch sizes because the Grace CPU's per-thread launch dispatch rate is slower than the x86 CPUs in the LC platforms. Section 7 isolates this finding through a controlled comparison and explains why latency-sensitive deployments cannot rely on the headline batch-64 number.

7.1 Why Grace's Single-Thread Performance Matters at All

The Grace CPU integrates 72 Arm Neoverse V2 cores, which is more cores than the 16-core AMD EPYC 7313 used in the AMD+A100 LC platform. By a naive core count, Grace should be the faster CPU for any dispatch-bound workload. The eager-mode PyTorch dispatcher does not parallelise across cores: each forward step issues its kernels serially from a single CPU thread, so the relevant performance number is per-thread issue rate, not total thread count. Per single-thread metric, the Arm Neoverse V2 core runs at a lower frequency than the AMD EPYC 7313's Zen3 cores and lacks the per-clock dispatch optimisations that the Intel Xeon Platinum 8468V brings to bear; the Arm software stack also has fewer years of fine-grained optimisation behind the PyTorch ATen path. The result is that a single forward step on GH200 issues kernels at a measurably slower rate than the same step on an LC platform, even though the GH200 has more cores in total.

7.2 The 4$\times$ Wider CPU-Bound Region

The TKLQT inflection-point data above quantifies the consequence of this single-thread gap. For encoder-only workloads the LC inflection occurs near batch size 8 and the GH200 inflection occurs near batch size 32; the GH200 therefore stays CPU-bound across a 4$\times$ wider batch-size interval. For decoder-only workloads the multiplier is smaller (the CPU-bound region on LC is itself narrower because decoder steps dispatch more kernels per token), but the same direction holds: the GH200 transitions later than the LC platforms. The implication is direct — any deployment whose latency-critical operating point sits at a batch size below the GH200 inflection will not benefit from a GH200 upgrade and may actively regress, regardless of the NVLink-C2C bandwidth advantage on paper.

7.3 Operational Recommendations

The paper closes with three operational recommendations that follow from the measurements. Each recommendation pairs a workload profile with the placement and optimisation policy that the diagnostic implies, so that an operator who has read the TKLQT curve and the GPU idle time can act on it directly.

(a) Latency-sensitive small-batch decoder LLM

Send to LC platforms (Intel+H100 preferred) until the application can amortise across batch size 8 or larger. The x86 single-thread dispatch rate dominates wall-clock at small batches, and the LC platform's interactive latency is between 1.5$\times$ and 1.9$\times$ better than GH200 in this region.

(b) Throughput-oriented large-batch LLM

Send to GH200. Once past the inflection point the higher HBM bandwidth and unified-memory architecture deliver the 1.9–2.7$\times$ prefill speedup on decoder workloads and the 1.6–2.4$\times$ on encoder workloads. The Grace CPU's idle time at this regime is operationally irrelevant because the GPU is fully utilised.

(c) Mid-range, dynamic-shape, eager-mode workloads

Apply proximity-score kernel fusion before deciding platform. The fusion remedy fires precisely in the CPU-bound region where Grace's single-thread cost dominates; on the LC platforms it has less margin to exploit but still amortises the launch tax. Once the fusion-recommended chains are collapsed, re-measure TKLQT and re-evaluate placement.

Operational caveat: The numbers reported in this paper are obtained with PyTorch 2.4.1, CUDA 12.6, and NVIDIA driver 560.35.03. As the Arm software stack on Grace matures, the single-thread dispatch rate on GH200 is expected to narrow the gap to the x86 platforms, and the inflection-point batch sizes will likely shift left. The diagnostic methodology — TKLQT plus the proximity-score fusion recommender — does not depend on the absolute numbers; it depends on the per-kernel attribution structure, which remains valid across software stack revisions.

8.1 Scope and Limitations

The paper is explicit about the boundaries of its conclusions. The benchmarked workloads are dense LLMs at small to moderate parameter counts (110M to 1.24B), so the findings on encoder-only vs decoder-only kernel intensity do not directly extend to MoE workloads such as DeepSeek-V3 or to dense models above 7B parameters. The TC paradigm is included on the spectrum but not evaluated, because the authors do not have an MI300A platform; the LC vs CC contrast is therefore the empirical core of the paper. The proximity-score fusion speedups reported in section 6.4 are idealised maxima from equations 7 and 8, not realised speedups from an implemented fusion compiler — the paper notes that implementation via kernel compilers such as Triton or manual coding is planned future work. Finally, the cross-platform measurements share the same software stack, but the underlying Arm vs x86 PyTorch dispatch path has different absolute maturity, so a portion of the GH200's small-batch tax is likely to close as the Arm stack hardens.

8.2 Relationship to the Precursor Work — TaxBreak

The first author and the senior author of this paper (Vellaisamy and Shen, both at the CMU NCAL group) extend this line of work in a 2026 preprint, TaxBreak (arXiv:2603.12465), which inherits the per-kernel attribution structure and the trace-driven posture established here but adds a mutually exclusive three-stage decomposition of host-side overhead into framework, library, and launch components, alongside a Host-Device Balance Index that summarises host-vs-device boundedness in a single ratio. This paper is the empirical bedrock that motivates TaxBreak's finer-grained decomposition; TaxBreak in turn refines the launch-and-queue measurement reported here into a layer-resolved attribution. The two papers are read most productively together — this one establishes that the CPU-GPU coupling decision is non-trivial, and TaxBreak then names which sub-layer of the host stack contributes most of the cost.
