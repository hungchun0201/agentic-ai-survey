Dissecting the NVIDIA Turing T4 GPU

A microbenchmark dissection of the Turing T4 that documents, instruction by instruction and cache by cache, how a small-form-factor inference GPU behaves under load — and shows the SM clock falling as cuBLAS matrix size grows under a 70 W power cap.

arXiv:1903.07486 GTC 2019 Microbenchmarking Power-Limit Throttling Tensor Cores

Zhe Jia, Marco Maggioni, Jeffrey Smith, Daniele Paolo Scarpazza

Citadel, High Performance Computing Group

1. Why Dissect a GPU at All?

When NVIDIA introduced the Turing architecture in 2018, it paired the familiar full-height training boards with a new device class aimed squarely at inference: the small-form-factor, single-slot, passively cooled T4, rated at 70 W rather than the 250–300 W of a full-height datacenter card. The architectural reference manuals that ship with such a device disclose almost none of the quantities a performance engineer actually needs — the true latency of an instruction, the size and associativity of each cache, the precise conditions under which the advertised boost clock is sustained. This paper exists because that gap between the published datasheet and the silicon's measured behavior had widened with every generation, and the gap is precisely where deployed inference throughput is won or lost.

1.1 A concrete failure: the datasheet TFLOPS that never arrive

Consider an engineer who provisions a T4 inference fleet against NVIDIA's datasheet Tensor-Core peak of 65 TFLOPS FP16 (the vendor's published mixed-precision ceiling, external to this paper) and then runs the obvious workload — a stream of half-precision cuBLAS matrix multiplications, the kernel underneath nearly every transformer layer. The application clock is explicitly pinned to the maximum 1,590 MHz. As the matrices grow, the measured cuBLAS throughput climbs to a mid-size peak and then, instead of holding there, turns over and falls — and it falls because the silicon has silently lowered its own clock from 1,590 MHz toward roughly 1,330 MHz to stay inside the 70 W envelope (Figure 4.3). The engineer never issued a clock-change command and the profiler reports no error; the only symptom is that the largest, most compute-bound layers run measurably slower than mid-size ones, the opposite of what a constant-clock roofline model predicts.

This abstraction has a sharp present-day edge. The T4's 70 W envelope still makes it one of the cheapest rentable inference accelerators per hour, so the throttling magnitudes the paper measures — the clock falling from 1,590 MHz toward 1,330 MHz, then below 800 MHz at 85 °C — propagate directly into the latency budget of any modern LLM-serving deployment. The following illustrative trace is not from the paper; it is a worked example that applies the paper's measured 16% power-limit clock reduction and its thermal step-down to a typical serving SLO, to show why the effect matters at the application level. Suppose a chat-completion service commits to a 200 ms time-to-first-token target and, provisioning against the constant-1,590 MHz assumption, budgets its prefill GEMM at roughly 150 ms. Applying the paper's 16% clock reduction stretches that same prefill to about 175 ms; applying the further thermal step-down below 800 MHz more than doubles it, so the first token arrives only after the target has already been missed. The end user sees a chat box that stalls before any text appears, and the operator sees a latency-target violation rate that climbs with operand size for reasons no profiler counter explains — which is precisely the gap the paper's measurements close.

1.2 The baseline pain: how far the achieved clock falls

The magnitude is not a rounding error. In the paper's own measurement, the sustained graphics clock under a continuous large-matrix cuBLAS load drops from the pinned 1,590 MHz to roughly 1,330 MHz — a reduction on the order of 16% from power-limit throttling alone, before any thermal effect is involved. Once the die also reaches its 85 °C limit, a second, sharper step-down follows, and the clock dips intermittently below 800 MHz, which is less than half of the nominal rate. Any capacity plan, latency SLO, or cost-per-token estimate built on the constant-clock assumption is therefore wrong by a margin large enough to violate a service contract.

1.3 The strawman: just read the datasheet, or just set the application clock

The obvious response is that NVIDIA already publishes peak FLOPS, cache sizes, and a base and boost clock, so a careful reader needs no microbenchmarks. This fails for two structural reasons. First, the datasheet peak is a ceiling that assumes a clock the device will not sustain under a sustained compute-bound load on a 70 W board — the very condition an inference server operates in. Second, the natural mitigation, pinning the application clock to its maximum with the management API, does not prevent throttling at all; the paper pins the clock to 1,590 MHz and the hardware overrides it downward anyway, because power-limit and thermal protection sit below the application-clock setting in the device's control hierarchy. The published numbers describe a regime the production workload never enters.

1.4 The fundamental obstacle: the control loop is undocumented and closed

The structural difficulty is that the relationship between an instruction stream and the clock the hardware will grant it is governed by a closed, on-die control loop that NVIDIA does not document and the user cannot inspect. Latency, throughput, cache geometry, and the clock-versus-load curve are not exposed through any interface; they are emergent properties of the silicon that can only be recovered by constructing workloads whose timing isolates one variable at a time. This is why the paper's method is empirical rather than analytical: there is no specification to read, so the behavior must be measured. The clock-throttling curve in particular cannot be derived from first principles, because the power model, the voltage-frequency table, and the throttle thresholds are all proprietary.

Key insight  — On a power-capped inference board, the SM clock is not a constant set by the user but a dependent variable driven by the instantaneous power draw of the workload. 2. The Microbenchmark Method

Because the device exposes no specification of the quantities of interest, every result in the paper is the output of a purpose-built kernel whose timing isolates a single architectural variable. This section describes the two instruments that underlie the whole study — a clock-cycle latency probe and a sustained-throughput sweep — and the principle that ties them together: each measurement perturbs exactly one degree of freedom and reads the silicon's response through the on-chip clock register.

2.1 The latency probe: reading the cycle counter around one dependent chain

To measure the latency of a memory access or an instruction, the kernel constructs a dependent chain so that each operation cannot begin until its predecessor retires, then brackets the chain with reads of the SM's free-running cycle counter via the %clock special register. The elapsed cycle count divided by the chain length yields the per-operation latency in clocks, free of any scheduling overlap. The same probe, applied to a pointer-chasing chain whose stride sweeps through ever-larger working sets, reveals each cache level as a discontinuity where the measured latency jumps.

The probe is shown in its essential form below. The reader should first understand its intent: it is a stopwatch wrapped tightly around a single chain of dependent operations, with nothing else in flight that could hide latency behind concurrency.

// Bracket a dependent chain with the SM cycle counter.
uint32_t start, stop;
asm volatile("mov.u32 %0, %%clock;" : "=r"(start) :: "memory");
// dependent pointer-chase: each load address depends on the prior load
for (int i = 0; i < CHAIN_LEN; ++i) p = *(const void**)p;
asm volatile("mov.u32 %0, %%clock;" : "=r"(stop) :: "memory");
latency_cycles = (stop - start) / CHAIN_LEN;

The subtlety that makes this faithful is the dependency edge between successive loads: without it, the warp scheduler would issue independent loads back to back and the measured interval would report throughput, not latency. The "memory" clobber prevents the compiler from reordering the clock reads across the chain. With this instrument the paper reports, for example, an L1 hit latency of about 32 cycles and a shared-memory latency of about 19 cycles on the T4.

2.2 The throughput sweep: driving power with operand size

The clock-throttling result requires a different instrument: a sustained-load sweep rather than a single-shot latency probe. The kernel repeatedly invokes cublas<t>gemm with input matrices of monotonically growing size while a companion host thread polls the device's clock and power telemetry. Because the application clock is pinned to its maximum, any observed clock reduction can be attributed to the hardware's own protection loop rather than to a user command, which is exactly the property needed to isolate involuntary throttling.

1 · Pin the application clock

Set the graphics clock to its maximum (1,590 MHz) so that any later reduction is involuntary, not commanded.
↓

2 · Sweep operand size upward

Issue cuBLAS GEMM on matrices whose per-operand footprint grows from kilobytes to several megabytes, holding precision fixed.
↓

3 · Read back the granted clock

Poll the actual SM clock and instantaneous power per step; record the achieved cuBLAS throughput alongside the clock.
↓

4 · Recover the clock-vs-size curve

The granted clock plotted against operand size is the involuntary throttling response the device never documents.

The reason this sweep recovers a meaningful curve, and not noise, is that operand size is a clean proxy for sustained power: larger matrices keep more functional units busy for longer, raising average draw toward the 70 W ceiling until the protection loop intervenes. The experiment therefore turns an inaccessible internal control loop into an observable input–output relation, where the input is operand size and the output is the clock the hardware is willing to sustain.

2.3 What the method buys: a worked optimization

The paper opens with a worked example that justifies the entire dissection effort, and it is worth restating because it establishes the standard the rest of the measurements are held to. The chosen workload is the BLAS saxpy operation, a scaled element-wise vector sum that underlies countless numerical kernels and is defined below.

(0) $$y_i \leftarrow \alpha \, x_i + y_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n$$

The cuBLAS single-precision implementation of this operation issues memory accesses limited to 64-bit width. Having recovered Turing's instruction encoding through microbenchmarks, the authors substitute vectorized 128-bit-wide global-memory access instructions, which the compiler does not emit on its own for this kernel. The replacement is small, but it raises the achieved performance of the memory-bound saxpy substantially over the stock cuBLAS routine across the swept operand-array sizes.

The wider lesson of the example is that the gap between the library's behavior and the silicon's capability is recoverable only once the encoding and the memory hierarchy are known, and that the same dissection apparatus that exposes this gap is what later exposes the involuntary clock droop. The two findings are products of one method applied to one device under controlled load.

3. Memory Hierarchy and Cores

Before turning to throttling, the paper establishes the static substrate on which all kernels run: the depth and size of the cache hierarchy, and the per-SM resource counts that bound occupancy. These results matter to a serving predictor because they fix the points at which a working set spills from one cache level to the next, and because they explain why the TU104 sustains higher effective bandwidth than its Pascal predecessor.

3.1 The measured hierarchy

The pointer-chasing latency probe, swept across working-set sizes, exposes each cache level as a latency plateau bounded by jumps. The columns below give the level, its measured latency in clock cycles, and the comparison the paper draws against the Pascal GP104.

3.2 Per-SM resources and the scheduler-to-core ratio

The paper situates the T4's 40 SMs and 1,590 MHz maximum clock within a generational table, and observes a long-running trend: the ratio of schedulers to cores grew from 1:48 in Kepler to 1:16 in Turing, which the authors correlate with rising instruction throughput. 4. Tensor Cores and Achieved GEMM Throughput

The Tensor Cores are the part of the T4 that justifies its existence as an inference accelerator, and the paper measures what they actually deliver rather than what the whitepaper promises. The central finding of this section is that achieved throughput depends jointly on the precision, the library, and — critically — the clock the device is willing to sustain, which connects this section directly to the throttling analysis that follows.

4.1 New Turing Tensor-Core instructions

Where Volta's Tensor Cores accepted only half-precision matrix operands, Turing widens the supported operand set, and the SASS-level mapping shows the device gaining dedicated instructions that express a matrix-multiply-accumulate step in fewer issued operations. The practical consequence is that the nvcc compiler renders a given matrix-math fragment more compactly on Turing than on Volta, reducing instruction-fetch pressure for the same arithmetic.

The encoding survey itself is a foundational result that the throttling and throughput findings rest on, because it lets the authors read the actual machine instructions a kernel issues rather than guessing from the source. The study confirms that Turing reuses Volta's instruction-encoding scheme, adds a new uniform-register class for values shared across a warp, and introduces the matrix instructions that the schematic SASS fragment below illustrates in condensed form.

; Schematic SASS: a half-precision matrix-multiply-accumulate step.
; Uniform registers (URx) hold warp-shared operands on Turing.
S2UR     UR4, SR_CLOCKLO        ; read warp-uniform clock-low into a uniform reg
HMMA.884.F16 R12, R8, R10, R12  ; Turing matrix-multiply-accumulate (condensed)
; ... remaining accumulate fragments omitted ...

The fragment is schematic rather than a verbatim disassembly, but it captures the two structural changes that matter for a performance model: the uniform-register operand, which removes redundant per-thread copies of a shared value, and the matrix-multiply-accumulate instruction, which folds a tile of the GEMM into a single issued operation. Both reduce the number of instructions standing between the source and the silicon, which is precisely the quantity the saxpy example of Section 2.3 showed to be optimizable once the encoding is known.

4.2 Achieved throughput across precisions

The figure below (Figure 4.3 in the paper) plots the achieved cuBLAS half-precision throughput together with the SM clock the device actually grants, both as a function of array size, with the application clock pinned to 1,590 MHz. It is the paper's clearest single picture of how achieved performance and the granted clock decline together once operands grow large, and therefore of how the gap between advertised and achieved performance depends on operand size.

Figure 4.3 (from paper): Clock frequency (right axis, MHz) and cuBLAS throughput (left axis, TFLOPS) measured while continuously computing cuBLAS half-precision matrix multiplication, with the application clock pinned to 1,590 MHz. As array size grows along the x-axis, the granted SM clock declines from about 1,590 MHz to about 1,330 MHz, and the achieved cuBLAS throughput is pulled down with it — the clearest direct evidence that the clock is a function of operand size under the power cap.

The paper also reports a separate per-precision peak-throughput table (reproduced below), distinct from the array-size sweep plotted above. Its single FP16 entry of 41,616 GFLOPS is the representative cuBLAS rate the authors quote for half precision; the plotted curve, taken on a different array-size sweep, peaks somewhat higher (around 47 TFLOPS) at a mid-size operand before the clock droop pulls it back down. Across the table the achieved matrix-multiply rates rise steeply as precision narrows, and the reduced-precision rows are exactly where the T4 separates from its Pascal predecessor.

These are achieved cuBLAS/CUTLASS rates at the pinned 1,590 MHz clock; once the operand grows large enough to trigger throttling, even these figures are an envelope the sustained workload does not hold.

5. Clock Throttling: Power-Limit and Thermal

This section contains the result most directly relevant to a serving-latency predictor: a direct measurement that the small-form-factor T4 lowers its own clock under a sustained compute load, and that it does so for two distinct reasons with different signatures. The authors show that the T4 and P4 are far more prone to this behavior than full-size boards, because their 70 W cap and their limited passive heat-sinks leave little margin between actual draw and the protection thresholds.

5.1 Power-limit throttling: clock falls as operand size grows

Power-limit throttling triggers when instantaneous power exceeds the 70 W manufacturer limit. The authors confirmed the mechanism with the operand-size sweep of Section 2.2: as the cuBLAS input matrices grew, the T4 exceeded its power limit more and more frequently and lowered its clock more and more, and the reduced clock eventually depressed overall arithmetic throughput. On the full-size V100, P100, M60, and K80 the same kernel barely throttled at all, because the margin between draw and limit is far larger. The behavior is therefore not a property of cuBLAS or of the matrix kernel, but of the power envelope of the small-form-factor board.

5.2 Thermal throttling: a sharper step at 85 °C

The second mechanism is characterized by a benchmark that repeatedly launches a GEMM on a single large matrix while temperature climbs. Below the 85 °C maximum operating temperature, power-limit throttling alone reduces the clock as the die warms; once the temperature reaches 85 °C, thermal throttling engages in addition to power-limit throttling and produces a markedly sharper step-down. The figure below plots both temperature and clock over time and labels the two regimes; the authors note that thermal throttling causes a more severe clock reduction than power-limit throttling alone.

Figure 4.4 (from paper): GPU temperature (left axis, °C) and clock frequency (right axis, MHz) over time while repeatedly computing cublasSgemm, application clock pinned to 1,590 MHz. In the first regime, power-limit throttling alone lowers the clock as the die warms toward 85 °C; once 85 °C is reached, thermal throttling adds a sharper step-down and the clock dips intermittently below 800 MHz. The two labeled regions show that the involuntary clock reduction has two distinct contributors with different magnitudes.

5.3 Throttling across device classes

To separate the device-class effect from the kernel, the authors ran an identical 1024×1024 cublasSgemm in an endless loop on every board and recorded the graphics clock over time, normalizing by each card's TDP. The comparison isolates a clean dichotomy between the inference-class and the full-size parts.

Inference-class: T4 (70 W), P4 (75 W)

Hold the maximum clock for only a few seconds at the start; as temperature rises, throttling intervenes and the clock declines. Throttling is observed on these two boards alone.

Full-size: V100, P100, M60, K80 (250–300 W)

Power draw could not be raised close enough to the limit to trigger throttling; the clock holds steady throughout the same kernel.

The consequence for a performance model is decisive: clock-versus-load is not a universal GPU property but a regime that the small-form-factor inference boards enter and the full-size boards do not. A predictor that targets inference-class hardware must therefore treat the clock as an endogenous, load-dependent quantity, whereas one calibrated on a V100 would never observe the effect at all.

6. Relevance to Our Per-Step Serving Predictor

Our own work builds a per-step latency predictor for vLLM serving that folds the GPU SM clock in as a hidden, involuntary, input-token-driven term. The connection to this paper is direct: where we observe the clock falling from boost toward throttle as the input-token count M of a serving step rises, this paper supplies the microbenchmark-level empirical anchor for the same mechanism, expressed in its classical form as clock-versus-operand-size on a power-capped GEMM (Figure 4.3). We cite it as the closest classical analogue to the effect our predictor models.

6.1 The shared mechanism, two vocabularies

The mapping between the two settings is almost one-to-one. In the paper, the independent variable is the cuBLAS operand size; in our serving predictor, it is the input-token count $M$ of a step, which sets the size of the prefill or compute-bound attention GEMM. In both cases a larger operand raises sustained power, the power-cap protection loop responds by lowering the clock, and the lowered clock degrades the achieved rate. The variable our predictor calls $M$ and the variable the paper calls matrix size are two names for the same driver of sustained power.

(1) $$f_{\text{clock}} = g\!\left(P_{\text{sustained}}(M),\; T_{\text{die}}\right), \qquad \text{latency}_{\text{step}} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{f_{\text{clock}}}$$

Equation (1) is the structural form both works share: the granted clock is a function of the sustained power induced by the operand and of the die temperature, and the step latency varies inversely with that clock. The paper measures the function for cuBLAS GEMM at the microbenchmark level; our predictor instantiates the same function with $M$ as the operand driver inside a general serving-latency model, so the per-step latency a scheduler reasons about already absorbs the droop rather than treating the clock as a constant.

6.2 What the paper establishes, and where our gap remains

The honest division of credit is the following. The droop mechanism itself, and the learnability of latency from clock, are both established prior knowledge, and this paper is among the cleanest classical demonstrations that the clock drops as a function of operand size on a power-capped board. What this paper does not do — and what our contribution is — is model that involuntary droop as a function of the serving-step variable $M$ inside a general per-step serving predictor, so that the term feeds directly into scheduling and capacity decisions for a modern decode-and-prefill workload on current SXM-class hardware.

6.3 What carries over to SXM hardware and what does not

The mechanism transfers, but its operating regime must be re-measured for each board, exactly as Section 5.3 warns. On the 70 W T4 the throttling is severe and triggers early; on the SXM-class H100, H200, and A100-80 cards our measurements target, the much larger power and thermal margins shift the throttling onset, so the droop is expected to stay dormant under light decode-only serving and to become material only in the compute-bound, high-$M$, prefill-heavy regime where sustained power approaches the cap — the serving analogue of the large-matrix region of Figure 4.3. Quantifying exactly where each board crosses that onset is itself part of what our measurements recover, rather than something we assume. The lesson we take from this paper is therefore not a constant to copy but a methodology: pin the clock, sweep the operand driver, and read back the granted clock to recover the device-specific droop curve before trusting any constant-clock latency model.

Why we selected this paper  — Among the prior art, this is the microbenchmark study that most cleanly isolates involuntary, operand-size-driven clock reduction under a power cap, with a direct measurement (Figure 4.3) and a mechanism decomposition (Figure 4.4). It is the closest classical analogue to the clock-versus-$M$ term at the centre of our predictor, and the right citation when we argue that the droop mechanism is established while its incorporation into a serving predictor is the open gap.

7. Strengths, Limitations, and Takeaways

The paper's value to a 2025 serving researcher lies in its method and its throttling measurement rather than in the absolute numbers, which are tied to a 2019 device. The following assessment separates what remains durable from what is specific to the T4 and its measurement setup.

Strengths

  • + Direct, reproducible evidence that the SM clock is a function of operand size under a power cap, isolated with a pinned application clock so the reduction is provably involuntary.
  • + Decomposes the involuntary reduction into power-limit and thermal contributors with distinct signatures, which a later model can treat as separate terms.
  • + Cross-device comparison establishes that the effect is a property of the inference-class power envelope, not of the kernel, which scopes where a clock term is needed.

Limitations

  • − The absolute clock and throughput figures are specific to the Turing T4; current SXM-class accelerators have far larger margins and a different operating regime.
  • ~ The throttling experiments ran on a server outside NVIDIA's Tesla-qualified catalog, so the exact thresholds may shift on a qualified host, as the authors themselves caution.
  • − The study characterizes GEMM in isolation, not a full serving step with attention, KV-cache traffic, and a mix of prefill and decode that determines real sustained power.

The durable takeaway is methodological. A latency model for power-capped accelerators cannot assume a constant clock; it must recover a device-specific droop curve by pinning the application clock, sweeping the operand driver, and reading back the clock the hardware grants. This paper is the canonical demonstration of that procedure on an inference GPU, and it is the foundation on which a per-step serving predictor with an explicit clock term is built.
