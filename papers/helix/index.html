Helix: Serving LLMs over Heterogeneous GPUs and Network via Max-Flow

A distributed serving system for heterogeneous and geo-distributed GPU clusters. Helix casts LLM serving as a max-flow problem on a directed, capacity-weighted graph, then co-optimizes model placement and request routing with a mixed-integer linear program.

arXiv:2406.01566 ASPLOS 2025 Heterogeneous Serving Max-Flow / MILP

Yixuan Mei, Yonghao Zhuang, Xupeng Miao, Juncheng Yang, Zhihao Jia, Rashmi Vinayak

Carnegie Mellon University · Purdue University

1. Overview

Production GPU fleets are no longer uniform. The same organization may hold a handful of H100 or A100 accelerators alongside large numbers of cheaper L4 and T4 cards, often spread across several datacenter regions connected by slow wide-area links. Serving a single large language model across that mixed pool is fundamentally harder than serving it on one homogeneous machine, and the serving stacks that defined 2023 — vLLM and Orca among them — were not built for it.

Helix is the first serving system to jointly optimize model placement and request scheduling for heterogeneous GPU clusters. Its central idea is to represent the cluster as a directed graph in which each GPU and each network link becomes an edge whose capacity is its per-second token throughput. Under this representation the maximum serving throughput of the cluster is exactly the max-flow from a source to a sink, which reduces the placement problem to choosing the layer assignment that maximizes that flow.

2. Why Heterogeneous Serving, and Why Now

Before the design can be appreciated, the problem has to be felt. This section builds the case in order: the workload shift that makes heterogeneous serving unavoidable, a concrete failure of the obvious partitioning, how far the status quo falls short in numbers, why the obvious fixes do not work, the structural obstacle underneath them, and where Helix sits relative to the systems that came before it.

2.1 The workload shift

Through 2023 the implicit assumption behind every high-throughput serving system was a rack of identical accelerators sitting on a fast, uniform interconnect. By 2024 that assumption no longer matches how most teams actually obtain compute. The latest accelerators are capacity-constrained and expensive, so organizations accumulate whatever they can secure: a few flagship cards, a long tail of previous-generation and inference-class GPUs, and spare capacity scattered across regions. The practical question is no longer how to schedule one model on one homogeneous cluster, but how to extract throughput from a fleet that is heterogeneous in compute, in memory, and in the bandwidth between its parts.

2.2 A concrete failure: uniform partitioning on a mixed pipeline

Consider a pipeline that places equal numbers of model layers on each GPU, a strategy that is correct on homogeneous hardware. Suppose one A100 stage and one T4 stage each hold the same number of layers. The A100 can process on the order of fifteen hundred tokens per second for its layers, while the T4 manages only a few hundred. Because pipeline throughput is capped by its slowest stage, the entire deployment runs at the T4's rate; the A100 spends most of its time idle, waiting for the weak stage to drain. The compute is present in the cluster, but the partitioning scheme cannot reach it.

The geo-distributed case compounds the problem. When two stages of a pipeline sit in different regions, every token crossing the boundary must traverse a wide-area link of roughly 100 Mb/s with about 50 ms of latency, two orders of magnitude slower than the 10 Gb/s intra-region fabric. A placement that balances compute but ignores where the region boundaries fall will route heavy activation traffic across that slow link and convert the network into the bottleneck.

2.3 How far the status quo falls short

These are not marginal inefficiencies. On the paper's most heterogeneous configuration — 42 nodes spanning seven GPU types — the best baseline (Separate Pipelines) reaches only about a third of Helix's token rate, a gap of 2.72×. On a geo-distributed cluster serving LLaMA-70B, the Swarm baseline incurs roughly 70 seconds of average prompt latency where Helix keeps it far lower, and on the single-cluster LLaMA-30B online setting Swarm shows about 47% higher prompt latency and 13% higher decode latency than Helix under the same load. The status quo leaves a large fraction of purchased compute stranded behind a placement decision.

2.4 The obvious fixes, and why they fail

The first instinct is to balance compute: give each stage a number of layers proportional to its FLOPs, so a strong GPU holds more layers than a weak one (Figure 1c). This removes the compute bottleneck of uniform partitioning but says nothing about the network, so on a geo-distributed cluster it readily places adjacent pipeline stages on opposite sides of a slow inter-region link. The second instinct is to give each machine type its own complete pipeline — the Separate Pipelines baseline. That balances compute within each pipeline but forces every pipeline to hold a full copy of the model, wasting the memory of small GPUs that could otherwise have contributed a few layers to a shared pipeline, and it cannot combine a strong and a weak GPU into one faster path.

2.5 The fundamental obstacle

The reason no simple rule works is that placement and scheduling are entangled. The best layer assignment depends on how requests will be routed, and the best routing depends on where the layers were placed; optimizing either in isolation leaves throughput on the table. Worse, the space of valid placements is combinatorial — each node can hold any contiguous block of layers, and each pair of nodes may or may not form a usable network edge — so an exhaustive search does not scale to clusters of dozens of nodes. Helix's contribution is to find a representation in which this joint, combinatorial problem becomes tractable: the max-flow value gives an exact, linear-time measure of a placement's throughput, and a mixed-integer linear program with only a linear number of variables searches over placements to maximize it.

2.6 Position relative to prior work

Helix sits among four lines of work, each of which left part of this problem unsolved. Homogeneous serving systems such as vLLM and Orca optimize batching and memory on a uniform cluster and simply assume every GPU is identical. Petals serves models over decentralized, heterogeneous GPUs using a greedy rule that hands new devices to the least-efficient pipeline stage, which is reactive rather than globally optimal. Swarm, designed for training over heterogeneous nodes, partitions the model into compute-balanced stages and routes proportionally to throughput, but it never accounts for the network. Separate Pipelines gives each machine type a self-contained pipeline, balancing compute at the cost of replicated weights and idle small GPUs. None of the four jointly optimizes placement and routing while treating compute, memory, and network bandwidth as first-class constraints — which is precisely the gap Helix closes.

2.7 What Helix contributes

With the pain established, the design follows from three contributions, each addressing one facet of the joint problem.

Max-flow representation

Serving throughput is shown to equal the max-flow of a directed graph whose edge capacities encode per-second token rates of GPUs and links, turning an opaque scheduling quantity into a quantity that can be computed exactly.

MILP-based placement

A mixed-integer linear program with a linear number of variables and constraints searches over layer assignments to maximize the max-flow, made practical by pruning, heuristic warm-starts, and an analytical early-stopping bound.

Per-request pipelines

At runtime each request is assigned its own path through the graph by interleaved weighted round-robin, with weights drawn from the optimal flow, so routing tracks the placement instead of being fixed in advance.

3.3 Three coupled challenges

The progression above decomposes into three challenges that a heterogeneous serving system must solve simultaneously rather than one at a time.

Challenge 1 — Model placement

How should model layers be partitioned across GPUs of different compute and memory? Uniform partitioning under-utilizes strong GPUs and lets weak ones bottleneck the pipeline.

Challenge 2 — Request scheduling

How should requests be routed through a pipeline of heterogeneous GPUs? A fixed set of pipelines with round-robin scheduling cannot match each request to the path with spare capacity.

Challenge 3 — Network awareness

Geo-distributed clusters span links that differ by two orders of magnitude (roughly 100 Mb/s inter-region vs. 10 Gb/s intra-region). Placement and routing must be network-aware to avoid congesting the slow links.

Why Joint Optimization of Placement + Scheduling Matters

4. Max-Flow Formulation

The system's foundation is a single equivalence: for a fixed model placement, the maximum serving throughput of the cluster equals the max-flow of a graph derived from it. This turns a question about scheduling — how fast can this placement serve requests? — into a question about flow that a standard algorithm answers in polynomial time.

Helix transforms a cluster of compute nodes with assigned model layers into a directed graph with edge capacities. Each edge capacity is the number of tokens that a compute node or a network link can process or transmit per second, and the max-flow from source to sink equals the cluster's maximum serving throughput.

Figure 2(a) (from paper): A three-node cluster annotated with each node's layer assignment and the bandwidth of the links between nodes.
Figure 2(b): The same cluster as a flow graph — numbers on edges are per-second token capacities, and the max-flow through the graph equals the maximum serving throughput.
Flow Graph Construction for a 3-Node Cluster

4.1 Graph construction rules

Each compute node is split into an in-vertex and an out-vertex joined by an internal edge whose capacity is the smaller of the node's compute throughput and its network throughput — the node can serve no faster than the weaker of the two. Edges between nodes exist only where they correspond to a legal pipeline hand-off:

  1. An edge from the coordinator to node $c_i$ exists if and only if $c_i$ holds the model's first layer.
  2. An edge from node $c_j$ to the coordinator exists if and only if $c_j$ holds the model's last layer.
  3. An edge from $c_i$ to $c_j$ exists if and only if $c_j$ holds the layers that come immediately after the layers on $c_i$.
# simulator/scheduler/global_maxflow/network_flow.py
class FlowGraph:
    def add_compute_node(self, node_uid, inference_throughput,
                          inbound_nic_tp, outbound_nic_tp):
        #      inbound TP          inference TP         outbound TP
        # _in -----------> _start --------------> _end -------------> _out
        self.flow_graph.add_edge(f"{node_uid}_in", f"{node_uid}_start",
                                 capacity=inbound_nic_tp)
        self.flow_graph.add_edge(f"{node_uid}_start", f"{node_uid}_end",
                                 capacity=inference_throughput)
        self.flow_graph.add_edge(f"{node_uid}_end", f"{node_uid}_out",
                                 capacity=outbound_nic_tp)

    def update_flow(self, time_stamp):
        # Build graph from cluster topology, then solve max-flow
        self.flow_value, self.flow_dict = nx.maximum_flow(
            flowG=self.flow_graph, _s="source", _t="sink"
        )

The three-edge split per node is what lets a single internal edge carry the compute capacity while the two NIC edges carry the inbound and outbound network limits; the smallest of the three caps the node, which is exactly the per-node bottleneck the formulation needs. NetworkX's preflow-push solver returns both the scalar flow value (the cluster throughput) and the per-edge flow dictionary that the scheduler later turns into routing weights.

5. MILP-Based Model Placement

The max-flow equivalence measures how good a placement is, but it does not produce one. To find the layer assignment that maximizes the max-flow, Helix encodes the placement decision as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP). The crucial property is that this program has only a linear number of variables and constraints in the number of compute nodes and links, which is what keeps it solvable on real clusters of dozens of nodes.

5.1 Decision variables

The program decides, for each node, which contiguous block of layers it holds, and for each pair of nodes, how much token flow passes between them. The variables below capture exactly that, with counts expressed in the number of nodes $|C|$ and the number of candidate edges $|E|$.

VariableTypeCountDescription
$s_i$int$O(|C|)$Index of $c_i$'s first layer
$b_i^j$binary$O(|C|)$Whether $c_i$ holds $j$ layers
$f_{i,j}$real$O(|E|)$Token flow from $c_i$ to $c_j$
$d_{i,j}$binary$O(|E|)$Whether $(c_i, c_j)$ is a valid connection
$\text{cond}_{i,j}^1,\ \text{cond}_{i,j}^2$binary$O(|E|)$Auxiliary variables that linearize the connection-validity condition

5.2 Constraints and objective

The objective maximizes the total flow leaving the source, which by the equivalence of the previous section is the cluster's serving throughput:

(1) $$\max \;\; \sum_{i \,:\, c_i \text{ holds first layer}} f_{\text{src},\, i}$$

subject to five constraint groups. The first pins down a valid placement, the next three enforce a feasible flow, and the last ties flow to network capacity:

GroupConstraintPurpose
Model placement$\sum_j b_i^j = 1,\ 0 \le s_i < L,\ e_i \le L$Each node holds exactly one contiguous block of layers
Flow conservation$\sum_u f_{u,i} = \sum_v f_{i,v}$Flow in equals flow out at every compute node
Inference throughput$\sum_u f_{u,i} \le \sum_j b_i^j \, T_j$Flow through a node cannot exceed its compute throughput
Connection validity$d_{i,j} \Leftarrow s_j \le e_i < e_j$An edge may carry flow only where its layers are contiguous
Transmission throughput$f_{i,j} \le d_{i,j} \, S_{i,j}$Flow on an edge cannot exceed the link bandwidth

5.3 Making the MILP tractable

Even with a linear variable count, a raw MILP over a large cluster can be slow, so Helix applies three accelerations. It prunes slow network connections before solving to shrink the candidate-edge set, it warm-starts the solver with heuristic placements from Petals and Swarm so the search begins from a feasible point, and it stops early once the incumbent is close to an analytical upper bound.

(2) $$\text{throughput} \;\le\; \frac{\sum_{i} T_i}{L}$$

The bound in equation (2) is the total compute throughput of all nodes divided by the number of layers $L$: no placement can serve faster than if every node's compute were perfectly shared across all layers. The paper gives the solver a 10-minute timeout per cluster and a 4-hour total search budget on a 14-core CPU, and notes that an LP relaxation is not used because relaxed solutions do not convert cleanly into valid integer placements.

6. Runtime Scheduling: Per-Request Pipelines

A good placement still needs a routing policy that realizes the flow it promises. Where prior systems route requests through a small fixed set of pipelines, Helix forms a fresh per-request pipeline for every request, choosing each hop in proportion to the optimal flow on the corresponding edge. The set of possible pipelines is the set of source-to-sink paths in the graph, so the scheduler can spread load exactly as the max-flow solution prescribes.

Figure 3 (from paper): Helix overview — a central coordinator plans model placement and per-request pipelines, while worker nodes run inference and exchange activation/KV traffic.
Figure 4: The request scheduler uses weighted round-robin with the max-flow values as weights, forming an independent pipeline for each request through the cluster.

Three mechanisms make per-request routing work in practice.

Interleaved weighted round-robin (IWRR)

Each node in the topology graph carries an IWRR scheduler that selects the next hop in proportion to the max-flow allocated to each outbound edge. Interleaving the choices follows the optimal flow distribution while avoiding the bursts that plain weighted round-robin would create.

KV-cache estimation

The scheduler tracks expected KV-cache occupancy per node from average output-length estimates. A node whose projected usage crosses a high-water mark is masked out of routing decisions, which prevents out-of-memory failures before they happen.

Best-effort dynamic batching

Each node batches every request that arrived while it was processing the previous batch, without inserting any additional waiting. Batches grow naturally under load and shrink when traffic is light.

6.1 Scheduling flow

On a request's first (prompt) phase the scheduler walks the graph from source to sink, choosing each hop by IWRR, and records the resulting path; subsequent decode steps reuse that same path because the request's KV cache is pinned to those nodes.

# simulator/scheduler/global_maxflow/scheduler_core.py
class SchedulerCore:
    def schedule(self, request: InferenceRequest) -> bool:
        if request.phase == RequestPhase.Initialization:
            # Build a per-request pipeline from source to sink
            pipeline = []
            current_node_uid = self.cluster.source_node.node_uid
            while current_node_uid != -1:
                scheduler_node = self.scheduler_nodes[current_node_uid]
                # IWRR: choose next node proportional to optimal flow
                next_stage = scheduler_node.schedule_initialization(request)
                if next_stage.link_uid == -1:
                    return False   # reject in offline mode, retry later
                pipeline.append(next_stage)
                current_node_uid = next_stage.node_uid
            request.set_pipeline(pipeline)
            self.kv_expectation.add_request(...)   # register KV-cache estimate

        elif request.phase == RequestPhase.Increment:
            # Follow the same pipeline (KV cache is pinned)
            for stage in request.mini_pipeline:
                self.scheduler_nodes[current_uid].schedule_increment(
                    request, stage.link_uid, stage.node_uid
                )

6.2 IWRR with admission masks

Before IWRR picks a hop, each candidate is filtered: edges carrying too little of the total flow are dropped, sequences longer than a node can hold are excluded, and nodes whose KV cache is near full are masked, leaving only viable next hops.

# scheduler_core.py -- SchedulerNode.schedule_initialization
# Filter outbound candidates, then pick one by IWRR:
mask = []
for setting, throughput, node in zip(settings, throughputs, nodes):
    if throughput < 0.05 * total:    mask.append(False)  # too little flow
    elif seq_len > max_tokens:       mask.append(False)  # sequence too long
    elif not kv_check(node):         mask.append(False)  # KV cache full
    else:                            mask.append(True)   # candidate passes

chosen = iwrr.choose_one(workload=seq_len, mask=mask)
bandwidth = min(link_bw, out_nic_limit, next_in_nic_limit)

The 5%-of-total-flow threshold quietly prunes hops that the max-flow solution barely uses, which keeps routing decisions concentrated on the edges the placement actually intended to carry traffic. The final bandwidth is the minimum of the link and the two NICs at either end, mirroring the per-node capacity rule of the graph construction so that runtime routing never promises more than the flow model allowed.

7. Implementation

Helix ships as two artifacts: a real prototype serving system and a high-fidelity discrete-event simulator that lets researchers explore cluster configurations without renting the hardware. The simulator's outputs stay within 5% of the prototype across every reported metric, which is what makes the large simulated studies in the evaluation trustworthy.

Prototype serving system

A multi-replica pipeline-parallel system of roughly 1.5k lines of Python and 1.7k lines of C++. It uses vLLM 0.4.0 as the execution engine, ZeroMQ for inter-node communication, and Gurobi as the MILP solver.

Discrete-event simulator

A 14k-line Python simulator that models heterogeneous GPUs and networks. Its simulation error is under 5% on every metric when checked against the prototype, so it can stand in for hardware in the larger studies.

Unified page pool

A unified KV page pool built atop vLLM supports partial inference, in which a node runs only a subset of the model's layers. This is the mechanism that makes per-request pipelines executable on a single engine.

The printed gap between max compute throughput and max flow is itself diagnostic: when the two are far apart, the network rather than the GPUs is limiting the placement, which is the signal the MILP uses to redistribute layers toward better-connected nodes.

Deploying the real prototype follows a coordinator/worker split: one host process solves the placement and drives scheduling, each worker runs partial inference for its assigned layers, and a final script parses the collected results.

# On the coordinator/host machine:
cd examples/real_sys
python step1_generate_system_config.py
python step2_start_host.py online maxflow

# On each worker machine:
python step3_start_worker.py maxflow

# After the experiment, parse results:
python step4_parse_results.py

Because the simulator is validated to within 5% of this real path, most of the studies above can be reproduced on the simulation track alone, and the real-system deployment is reserved for confirming that the modeled throughput holds on physical hardware.

10. Relevance to Agentic AI Systems

Helix is an infrastructure result rather than an agent system, but it bears directly on how agentic workloads get served. Agent deployments rarely secure a clean homogeneous cluster; they run on whatever GPUs an organization can assemble, often across regions, under request patterns that are bursty and hard to predict. The properties Helix optimizes — squeezing throughput from a mixed fleet, respecting slow inter-region links, and routing each request independently — are the same properties an agent-serving backend needs.

Serving under GPU scarcity

Agent backends often cannot reserve homogeneous clusters. Helix lets an organization aggregate whatever cards it has — H100, A100, L4, T4 — into one serving cluster and still extract near-optimal throughput for agent inference.

Geo-distributed serving

When agents are deployed near users in several regions, the network-aware placement keeps cross-region activation traffic off the slow links, which is the dominant cost in a geo-distributed serving path.

Cost per interaction

Folding cheap L4 and T4 cards into the same pipelines as scarce H100s lowers the effective cost of each agent call, since the MILP maximizes throughput for whatever hardware budget is available.

Bursty request handling

Agent traffic is inherently bursty — tool calls, multi-step reasoning, and fan-out sub-tasks create uneven request patterns. Per-request pipelines absorb this variability without the rigidity of a fixed pipeline set.

Scaling to large deployments
For clusters of hundreds of nodes the paper recommends partitioning the fleet into smaller sub-clusters with a heuristic and applying the MILP independently to each, since a single monolithic MILP over hundreds of nodes exceeds the search budget. This hierarchical approach keeps Helix practical for enterprise-scale serving across diverse hardware.
