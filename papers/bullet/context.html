1. The Puzzle: An Important Problem, Few Citations

Bullet (arXiv:2504.19516, ASPLOS'26) makes a claim that is easy to agree with: chunked-prefill hybrid batching has hit a structural ceiling, because the compute-bound prefill chunk and the memory-bound decode tokens share one kernel launch over the whole GPU, so every step saturates one subsystem — the tensor cores or the HBM bandwidth — and leaves the other idle. Bullet's response is to run prefill and decode concurrently on the same device, partitioning the streaming multiprocessors (SMs) between the two phases at runtime under an SM-scaling roofline model. The premise feels both correct and consequential, which makes the next observation jarring: as of mid-2026, the paper has only a single-digit number of citations.

The puzzle is numerical before it is interpretive: Semantic Scholar shows 8 raw citing records, Google Scholar shows only 2, and de-duplication by hand gives roughly 7 distinct citing papers as of mid-2026. That is a thin count for a paper whose problem framing is already the shared premise of Nexus, MuxWise, TORTA, DuetServe, TokenLake, and ShadowServe — which is precisely the tension this report sets out to resolve.

This companion report treats that mismatch as a research question rather than an anomaly. It answers four questions the reader of the main analysis is likely to ask, with evidence gathered across Semantic Scholar, Google Scholar, arXiv full text, and primary NVIDIA documentation. The conclusion, stated up front: the low citation count is an artifact of timing and indexing, not of weak influence; ‘wave quantization’ is a borrowed GPU-GEMM concept, not a Bullet discovery; the 2025 wave of utilization work was over-determined by five preconditions that only co-occurred recently; and the downstream literature validates Bullet's problem framing almost unanimously while splitting sharply on whether its mechanism is the right solution.

2.3 Why the count is genuinely low — and why that is benign

Three structural forces, none of them about quality, hold the number down. The first is recency compounded by proceedings lag: Bullet was an arXiv preprint for roughly thirteen months and only became an archival ASPLOS'26 paper in 2026, and systems and architecture venues have slow citation velocity because their annual deadlines and twelve-to-eighteen-month review cycles delay the citing wave by design. The second is the shape of the subfield: intra-GPU SM-level prefill/decode sharing is a small, hot cluster in which several works — MuxWise, Nexus, semi-PD, DuetServe, Drift — were written nearly in parallel, and concurrent papers cannot reliably cite one another until a later revision lands. The third is indexer fragmentation: the paper circulates under two titles (‘Bullet’ and ‘Boosting LLM Serving through Spatial-Temporal GPU Resource Sharing’) and across arXiv-versus-ACM clusters, so every aggregator undercounts in a different way.

Taken together, a single-digit count here reflects when the paper landed and how it is indexed, not how much it matters. The strongest evidence for that reading is the identity of the citers: the works that do cite Bullet are precisely the leading systems in its exact niche — the proactive successors and the data-plane neighbours — which is the citation profile of an agenda-setting paper observed too early, not a neglected one.

3. Wave Quantization: A Concept Older Than LLM Serving

Bullet names ‘wave quantization’ as one of two root causes of prefill's suboptimal compute utilization, so it is natural to ask who first discovered it in the history of LLM inference. The honest answer reframes the question, because wave quantization is not an LLM phenomenon at all: it is a property of how any matrix multiplication tiles onto a GPU's streaming multiprocessors, named by NVIDIA years before LLM serving existed. The careful version of the question has three separate answers — who named the term, who first described the underlying effect, and who first carried it into LLM inference — and they are not the same party.

3.1 What wave quantization is

A GPU runs a matrix multiplication by splitting the output into thread-block tiles and executing them in ‘waves’ of roughly one tile per SM. If the total tile count is not an integer multiple of the SM count, the final partial wave occupies only a few SMs yet costs almost a full wave's latency, so the GPU is underutilized during that tail. On a 108-SM A100, 109 tiles take two waves where 108 would take one: a one-tile overflow nearly doubles the wave count. This is wave quantization — work quantized to the granularity of the SM count. Its finer sibling, tile quantization, is the same idea one level down: when a matrix dimension is not a multiple of the tile size, the boundary tiles run a full tile of math for partial useful output.

3.3 The distinction that is easy to blur

Wave quantization is frequently conflated with the broader observation that LLM decode is memory-bound and leaves the GPU's compute idle. The two effects combine to justify spatial-temporal sharing: overlap the memory-bound decode, which leaves compute idle, with the compute-bound prefill, whose tail wave leaves SMs idle, so each fills the other's slack. The precision worth keeping is that wave quantization does not make compute-bound work memory-bound — it merely wastes SM-cycles in the tail wave. Conflating (A) and (B) is a common imprecision; Bullet itself keeps them distinct, attributing prefill waste to (A) and decode waste to (B).

4. Why Utilization Optimization Only Crested in 2025

If decode utilization has always been low — and it has, because decode's arithmetic intensity is fixed and small — the natural question is why a wave of intra-GPU utilization papers appears in 2024–2025 rather than in 2021–2022. The answer is not that anyone overlooked the gap. The gap was visible; what was missing were the preconditions that make attacking it both necessary and possible. Five had to hold at once, and they only co-occurred recently. Importantly, this is not a story of neglect: Orca (2022) and Pope et al. (2022) had already articulated decode underutilization, and SARATHI (2023) and NanoFlow (2024) had already attacked pieces of it. The specific 2025 sub-wave — dynamic, spatial SM partitioning of concurrent prefill and decode — is the part that was gated on tooling and hardware that did not exist earlier.

4.1 The five preconditions

Each card states one precondition, the mechanism by which its absence hid the problem before, and the concrete event that satisfied it. No single card explains the timing on its own; the point is that all five resolved within roughly twelve months.

1 · The objective changed

Pre-2023 serving optimized raw throughput, where ‘batch harder’ hides idle SMs entirely — Orca reported its win as 36.9× throughput, FlexGen scoped itself as ‘latency-insensitive’. The prefill-compute-bound / decode-memory-bound mismatch only becomes central once the goal is goodput under simultaneous TTFT and TBT SLOs, introduced by DistServe and Sarathi-Serve in early 2024.

2 · The baseline had to be peeled first

Optimizations are solved in order of marginal payoff. In 2021–2022 the binding waste was idle batch slots and KV-cache fragmentation, not idle SMs. Orca's continuous batching (2022) and vLLM/PagedAttention (2023) removed those first; only once a GPU could be kept full did residual compute-underutilization become the next layer worth a paper.

3 · The tooling did not exist

Concurrent prefill+decode on disjoint SM sets is mechanically impossible without per-SM masking. MPS is coarse and best-effort; MIG (A100, 2020) is static and partitions bandwidth too. The enabling primitive, transparent intra-context SM masking, first became usable as libsmctrl (Bakita & Anderson, RTAS, May 2023). NVIDIA's sanctioned Green Contexts arrived even later (driver API CUDA 12.4, 2024; runtime API CUDA 13.1, 2025).

4 · The hardware moved the roofline

Decode's intensity is fixed; whether it starves the GPU depends on the ridge point = peak FLOPs / peak bandwidth. SM counts grew (A100 108 → H100 132), and bandwidth-heavy/compute-light SKUs widened the gap: the H20 has ~148 TFLOPS FP16 (about 5× less than H100) but 4.0 TB/s bandwidth (above H100's 3.35). That pushes the ridge point up, so the same decode strands a larger share of compute — and makes co-scheduled prefill cheap. These SKUs became deployment-relevant only in 2024–2025.

5 · The economics crossed a threshold

An optimization attracts a wave once the idle resource it reclaims is economically large. In 2021–2022, chat outputs were short, so decode was a modest fraction and its low MFU was a rounding error. Reasoning models, 100k+ contexts, and agentic pipelines (2024–2025) made decode the dominant, revenue-bearing phase, so running it below 40% MFU on multi-thousand-dollar GPUs became a first-order fleet cost worth a 1.2–1.5× reclaim.

4.2 The timeline, read as preconditions resolving

Laid out by year, the milestones show the gap staying open until the enablers line up. The conceptual unlock (SLO/goodput), the baseline unlock (memory waste solved), the tooling unlock (libsmctrl), the hardware unlock (roofline-shifting SKUs), and the economic unlock (decode-heavy traffic) all land within about a year of one another.

5. How the Field Received Bullet: Validated Problem, Contested Mechanism

The question that motivates this report — do later papers support Bullet or judge it insufficient — has a structured answer once every confirmed citer is read in context. The citers fall into two camps. A complementary camp works at the cache and data-plane layers and treats Bullet as an orthogonal lower layer it can sit on top of or beside. A competing camp works in Bullet's own layer — intra-GPU prefill/decode sharing — and there the verdict is consistent and pointed: the problem Bullet identifies is real and worth building on, but its reactive SM-masking mechanism is judged insufficient, and the competitors propose proactive or differently-engineered alternatives. 5.2 The sharpest critique: does Bullet's SM masking actually move?

The single most consequential downstream claim is MuxWise's. Its related-work section reports that, on reproducing Bullet's released implementation, the SM allocation for each CUDA graph did not change, and attributes this to libsmctrl's own documented statement that it does not work with CUDA graphs. Because Bullet launches the decode step as one CUDA graph to amortize kernel-launch overhead, this is not a peripheral detail — it touches the interaction between two of Bullet's four components (SM masking via libsmctrl, and the CUDA-graph decode engine). Bullet's paper claims dynamic per-graph SM reallocation does occur, so this is a genuine, unresolved disagreement between the original authors and one reproducing group, not a settled refutation.

6. Synthesis: What the Reception Tells Us

The four questions resolve into a single coherent picture. Bullet identified a problem the field now agrees is central — the prefill/decode resource mismatch and the idle compute it strands — and every competing citer builds on that exact premise, which is the clearest possible evidence that the problem framing was right and early. The thin citation count is a timing-and-indexing artifact layered on top of a subfield so young that its members were written in parallel and have not finished citing one another. The concept Bullet leans on, wave quantization, is a borrowed NVIDIA-named GPU-GEMM effect, applied to LLM serving first by SARATHI and used by Bullet as honest motivation rather than claimed novelty. And the 2025 timing reflects five preconditions resolving together, not a gap anyone missed.

Where the field parts company with Bullet is on mechanism, and the disagreement is unusually clean. Two independent critiques recur: that reactive per-step feedback should be replaced by proactive, contention-aware anticipation (Nexus, TORTA, DuetServe), and that the libsmctrl masking primitive should be replaced by NVIDIA's sanctioned Green Contexts (Nexus, MuxWise), with MuxWise additionally reporting that Bullet's masking did not move under CUDA graphs in its reproduction. The reception is therefore best summarized not as ‘support’ or ‘insufficient’ but as both at once, cleanly separated by layer: the problem is endorsed, the solution is being actively rebuilt. For a paper barely a year past preprint, having one's problem statement adopted as the shared premise of an entire competitive cluster is the more durable form of influence — and it is invisible in a citation count of seven.

Endorsed: the problem

Prefill/decode resource mismatch and stranded compute. Adopted verbatim as the shared premise by Nexus, MuxWise, TORTA, DuetServe; treated as a compatible lower layer by TokenLake and ShadowServe.

Contested: reactive control

Per-step SLO feedback is judged to react one step late under bursty load. Nexus, TORTA, and DuetServe all propose proactive or anticipatory scheduling as the replacement.

Contested: the masking primitive

libsmctrl is unofficial and, per MuxWise, incompatible with CUDA graphs. Nexus and MuxWise both move to NVIDIA Green Contexts; the field is converging on the sanctioned primitive.
