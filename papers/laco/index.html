LACO

Adaptive Latent Communication for Collaborative Driving — a training-free paradigm that lets connected vehicles exchange KV-cache latent states instead of language, while resolving cross-agent “identity confusion.”

arXiv:2605.22504 arXiv preprint 2026 cs.AI / cs.CV Collaborative Driving VLA Cross-Agent KV Cache CARLA Closed-Loop

Tianhao Chen, Yuheng Wu, Dongman Lee

KAIST (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology), School of Computing

1. Why Vehicles Cannot Simply Talk to Each Other

Collaborative driving lets connected vehicles pool their partial views of a scene so that each can act on hazards it cannot see alone. This section builds the case that the natural way to make that pooling “intelligent” — having vehicles exchange the reasoning of their onboard foundation models — runs into a wall that is not about bandwidth but about representation, and that the obvious remedies each fail for a concrete, measurable reason.

1.1 Why now: from sharing pixels to sharing reasoning

Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) collaboration began by exchanging perception: raw or bird’s-eye-view visual features that let a receiver reconstruct occluded regions of the scene. The last generation of autonomous-driving stacks has replaced hand-built perception heads with vision-language-action (VLA) foundation models — ORION, SimLingo, LMDrive and their kin — that reason about the scene in natural language before emitting control. The tempting next step is to let these models collaborate at the level of reasoning rather than pixels: one vehicle tells another, in words, “a pedestrian is stepping off the curb on the receiving vehicle's blind side.” The problem is timing. Autoregressive decoding of such a message, measured on the paper’s own models, takes between 1.3 and 8.5 seconds end-to-end. A vehicle travelling at 60 km/h covers roughly 20 to 140 metres in that window, so by the time the sentence has been generated, transmitted, and parsed, the geometry that made it useful has already changed.

1.2 One concrete failure at trace granularity

Consider the ORION 7B model coordinating two vehicles at an occluded intersection. The collaborator has line of sight to a pedestrian that the ego vehicle cannot see. Under language-based collaboration the collaborator must decode a description of the hazard; on ORION this exchange is measured at 7802 ms, so the ego vehicle receives the warning almost eight seconds after the observation was made. A natural reflex is to skip language entirely and let the collaborator hand the ego its full internal state — the complete key-value (KV) cache of its VLA. When the paper does exactly this, the outcome is worse rather than better: the ego vehicle’s driving score falls to 30.70, below the 35.48 that a careful design reaches, because the ego begins to brake for a hazard that exists only in the collaborator’s lane while its own lane is clear. The paper names this failure agent identity confusion: the receiver over-attends to another agent’s internal states and its own control policy is, in the authors’ words, hijacked.

Language is safe but too late; naive latent is timely but hijacks the ego’s policy, dropping its driving score to 30.70. Neither is acceptable, which is the gap LACO sets out to close.

1.3 Quantified baseline pain

Before any of the paper’s improvements are shown, the status quo can be summarised by three numbers drawn from its main table. Language-based collaboration incurs 7802 to 8509 ms of communication latency per exchange — three orders of magnitude above what closed-loop control can absorb. Visual feature sharing is fast (52 to 95 ms) but heavy: a single ORION exchange transmits 8208 KB, so a modest platoon saturates a V2V radio link. Naive full-KV latent fusion is both fast and compact yet actively harmful: it drives ORION’s ego driving score down to 30.70 and, in the depth sweep, as low as 32.46 under full-depth fusion, against 35.48 for the paper’s design. Each of the three obvious interfaces therefore fails on a different axis — latency, bandwidth, or safety.

The table below lays the three status-quo interfaces side by side on ORION so that the three failure axes are visible at once; the final row is LACO, included here only to mark the target, with its mechanism deferred to later sections.

Reading the three status-quo rows together makes the design problem concrete: language and visual sharing sacrifice, respectively, latency and bandwidth, while naive latent fusion pays neither of those costs yet forfeits the one thing a driving system cannot forfeit, which is control over its own decisions. The remainder of the paper is the argument that a fourth row is reachable.

1.4 The strawman: just send the whole cache

The reader is entitled to ask why the collaborator does not simply transmit its entire KV cache, giving the ego the richest possible signal. The measurements above answer this: more information makes the ego drive worse. Injecting a collaborator’s full-depth cache places its states into the exact region of the network where the ego’s own perception has already fused with its emerging control intent, so the ego cannot tell which decision-relevant activations are its own and which belong to the other vehicle. The design space is therefore not “compress the full cache” but “decide which part of another agent’s cache can be admitted at all” — a question about representation, not about transport.

1.5 The fundamental obstacle: depth-wise entanglement

The structural reason a naive fusion cannot work is that in a driving VLA, perception and ego-conditioned intent become geometrically entangled as depth increases. The paper measures the per-layer attention entropy and finds a U-shaped trajectory: early layers parse the global scene with high entropy, intermediate layers collapse onto ego-conditioned evidence, and deep layers show entropy resurgence as the model synthesises an action. At the deep layers where entropy rises again, there is no coordinate along which a collaborator’s perception can be inserted without also inserting the collaborator’s decision. The obstacle is therefore representational rather than one of capacity or bandwidth: another agent’s deep states cannot be fused without importing its identity. This U-shape is the pivot on which the whole method turns, and the architecture returns to it in every component.

  • Not a bandwidth problem: a bigger radio link would not help — the harmful states arrive intact and still hijack the ego.
  • Not a capacity problem: a larger receiver model would still entangle the two agents in its deep layers.
  • A representational problem: the fix must decide, per state, what belongs to whom — which is exactly where selection and truncation enter.

1.6 Position versus prior work

LACO sits at the intersection of two lines that had not previously met. The first is collaborative perception — V2VNet, CoBEVT and related BEV-feature-sharing systems — which exchange perceptual tensors but carry no high-level reasoning and are bandwidth-heavy. The second is language-level driving collaboration, exemplified by LangCoop, which is interpretable but bottlenecked on autoregressive decoding and lossy tokenisation. Cutting across both is the recent line on cross-agent KV-cache reuse for language models: KVCOMM (arXiv:2510.12872) reuses a shared segment’s cache across agents by decomposing it into a base plus a context-dependent offset interpolated from an online anchor pool, and a companion line aligns and reuses agents’ caches through an anchor pool without changing parameters. The paper explicitly cites both and argues that porting them to the physical world exposes an architectural mismatch: driving agents run in parallel and each receiver must integrate information that is not subsumed by its own computation, so alignment alone leaves the identity-confusion failure untouched. LACO’s claim to novelty is a training-free latent V2V interface for driving VLAs that confronts identity confusion head-on.

  • Collaborative perception (V2VNet, CoBEVT): exchanges BEV feature tensors — rich perception but no reasoning, and megabyte-scale payloads.
  • Language-level collaboration (LangCoop): interpretable and compact, but bottlenecked on autoregressive decoding (seconds) and lossy tokenisation.
  • KVCOMM (arXiv:2510.12872): cross-agent KV reuse for text LLMs via base-plus-offset anchor-pool alignment — additive re-alignment, no identity-confusion notion, single-agent-context assumption.
  • What LACO adds: parallel, physical-world agents whose received information is not subsumed by local computation, forcing the identity-confusion question that alignment alone never raises.
In one paragraph: LACO lets connected vehicles exchange KV-cache latent states instead of language. Its analysis shows that naively fusing a collaborator’s full cache causes agent identity confusion — the ego over-attends to another vehicle’s decision and is hijacked. LACO fixes this by writing intent into the cache (ILD), keeping only the most salient 30% of tokens (CHSA), and transmitting only the shallow ~10% of layers (SSKD), so the collaborator supplies context while the ego alone decides. On CARLA it lifts driving score above every prior interface at roughly an order of magnitude less latency than language and up to 90% less bandwidth than visual sharing.

2. LACO at a Glance

Having established that the barrier is representational, this section previews how LACO turns a full, entangled KV cache into a compact, safe message. The design is a pipeline of three training-free operators applied on top of a frozen pretrained driving model, followed by a depth-asymmetric fusion rule on the receiver. The reader should leave this section knowing what each operator does and which of the three baseline failures it addresses.

Iterate the frozen model to write intent into its own KV cache
2
CHSA
Score every token by saliency, keep the Top-K most informative
3
SSKD
Transmit only the shallow layers; drop the entangled deep ones
4
Fuse
Concatenate at shallow layers; stay ego-only at deep layers
5

Ego synthesises its own control from a clean shared prior

Mapping each operator onto the baseline failure it addresses keeps the roles straight, because two of the three are about efficiency and only one is about safety.

  • Language latency → ILD reuses prefilled states and never decodes text, so an exchange costs a fixed few forward passes rather than seconds of generation.
  • Visual bandwidth → CHSA keeps only the salient 30% of tokens, cutting the payload to kilobytes.
  • Identity confusion (safety) → SSKD transmits only shallow layers, so the deep, decision-carrying states are never fused into the ego.

2.1 Core contributions

The paper frames its contribution as an analysis followed by three operators that each answer one part of the analysis.

Diagnosis: identity confusion is depth-wise

An attention-entropy study localises the failure: cross-agent entanglement arises only when fusion occurs after perception has merged with ego-conditioned control synthesis in the deep layers.

ILD + CHSA: write intent, then keep only what matters

Iterative Latent Deliberation crystallises perception into intent inside the KV cache without decoding to text; Cross-Horizon Saliency Attribution then keeps only the Top-30% most salient tokens for transmission.

SSKD: share shallow layers, keep deep ones private

Shallow-Stream Knowledge Distillation transmits only the first $L_{comm}$ layers (about 10% of depth) so that the collaborator supplies spatial priors while the ego retains sole authority over deep action synthesis.

These three ORION ego driving scores, all drawn from the paper’s own tables, bracket the entire contribution: the more of the collaborator’s deep cache is admitted, the worse the ego drives, and LACO’s gain is the distance from 30.70 to 35.48 that shallow-only sharing recovers.

3.1 Observation 1 — spatial reasoning is sparse

When the model attends over its visual tokens, a small subset carries most of the attention mass while the majority remain weakly activated. The paper reports that about 30% of tokens account for the bulk of the model’s attention, which means spatial reasoning is intrinsically sparse. This is the licence for pruning: if only a minority of tokens drive the decision, transmitting the rest wastes bandwidth. Observation 1 is what CHSA later exploits.

  • Because most tokens are weakly activated, dropping the bottom 70% costs little accuracy while saving most of the bandwidth.
  • Sparsity is a property of the frozen model’s attention, so it holds without any training or architectural change.

3.2 Observation 2 — the entropy U-shape

The paper defines the per-layer attention entropy and plots it across depth. The curve is U-shaped, and the three regimes it separates are the backbone of the whole method.

(1) $$e^{(l)}=-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{h,j}^{(l)}\log\!\left(\alpha_{h,j}^{(l)}+\epsilon\right)$$

Here $e^{(l)}$ is the entropy at layer $l$, averaged over $H$ attention heads, and $\alpha_{h,j}^{(l)}$ is the attention weight that head $h$ places on token $j$; a small $\epsilon$ keeps the logarithm finite. High entropy means attention is spread broadly (global scene parsing); low entropy means it has contracted onto a few tokens (ego-conditioned focus). The three depth bands below name what each regime does and why only the first is safe to share.

Shallow (high entropy)Global scene parsing. Attention is broad and agent-agnostic; these states are spatial priors that any vehicle can use. Safe to share.
Middle (entropy collapse)Attention contracts onto ego-conditioned evidence. The representation is beginning to specialise to the observing vehicle.
Deep (entropy resurgence)Action synthesis. Perception and emerging intent are geometrically entangled in a control-oriented space; injecting a collaborator here causes identity confusion. Must stay private.

4.1 The full loop in pseudocode

The pipeline is compact enough to state in full. The listing below traces one collaboration round from the perspective of an ego vehicle receiving from one collaborator; the equation numbers refer back to the definitions given in the mechanism sections. Nothing here is trained — every operator is a deterministic transform of a frozen model’s cache.

# Sender side (collaborator vehicle), run once per vehicle
def build_message(model, obs, m=10, rho=0.3, L_comm):
    kv_prefill, h = model.prefill(obs)            # normal forward over own sensors

    # ILD: m latent deliberation steps write reasoning into the cache  (Eq. 2)
    kv_latent, A = [], []
    for t in range(m):
        e_hat = h @ W_a                          # W_a ~= pinv(W_out) @ W_in, closed form
        h, kv_t, attn_t = model.step(e_hat)     # append keys/values, record attention
        kv_latent.append(kv_t); A.append(attn_t)

    # CHSA: per-token saliency, keep Top-K salient prefill tokens       (Eq. 3, 4)
    S = (1/m) * sum(attn_t.max(dim=("layer","head")) for attn_t in A)
    keep = topk_indices(S, k=int(rho * S.numel()))    # sender-side importance
    kv_salient = kv_prefill[sorted(keep)]              # original sequence order
    kv_chsa = concat(kv_salient, kv_latent)

    # SSKD: transmit only the first L_comm (~10%) layers               (Eq. 5)
    return kv_chsa[:L_comm]                          # P  -- the message on the wire

# Receiver side (ego vehicle): asymmetric collaborative inference   (Eq. 6, 7)
def ego_forward(model, obs, P, L_comm):
    h = model.embed(obs)
    for l in range(model.n_layers):
        kv_ego = model.layer_cache(l)
        if l <= L_comm:
            kv = concat(kv_ego, P[l])            # shallow: fuse collaborator prior
        else:
            kv = kv_ego                          # deep: ego-only, identity stays clean
        h = model.block(l, h, kv)
    return model.action_head(h)

The single conditional inside the receiver loop — share below the cut-off, stay private above it — is the entire safety mechanism. Everything else is bandwidth engineering.

Architectural insight: The three operators are not independent compression tricks bolted together; they are three cuts along three different axes of the same cache. ILD adds a reasoning axis (time / deliberation steps), CHSA cuts along the token axis (keep salient tokens), and SSKD cuts along the depth axis (keep shallow layers). Only the depth cut addresses safety; the other two address efficiency. Conflating them — as a reader skimming the abstract might — obscures which mechanism actually resolves identity confusion.

5. Iterative Latent Deliberation (ILD)

ILD answers a preliminary question: what should a vehicle share, if not words? Its answer is that the model already holds task-relevant information in its hidden states, so there is no need to decode it into low-dimensional language at all. ILD instead lets the model ‘think’ a few more steps in latent space, writing the resulting reasoning directly into its KV cache, so that the cache itself becomes the message.

5.1 The plain idea before the mechanism

After the ordinary prefill, the model has a final-layer hidden state that summarises its current understanding of the scene. Ordinarily this state would be projected to vocabulary logits to produce the next word. ILD skips the vocabulary and feeds the hidden state back into the model as if it were the next input, for $m$ steps. Each step appends fresh keys and values to the cache, so the cache accumulates a short latent chain of thought, none of which ever surfaces as text.

  • Spatial semantics — where the drivable space, other vehicles and obstacles are relative to the ego.
  • Risk assessment — which elements of the scene are hazards that constrain the next maneuver.
  • Prospective intent — the driving action the model is converging toward, expressed as latent state rather than a token.

5.2 Keeping the feedback in-distribution

Feeding a raw hidden state back as an input is dangerous: the model expects an input-embedding-space vector, not an output-space one, and the mismatch can push activations out of distribution. ILD inserts a lightweight linear projection $W_a$ that maps the previous step’s hidden state into the input embedding space before re-injection.

(2) $$\hat{e}^{(t)} = h^{(t-1)} W_a \qquad \text{where} \qquad W_a \approx W_{out}^{\dagger}\, W_{in}$$

The projection is not learned. It is constructed in closed form as $W_a \approx W_{out}^{\dagger} W_{in}$, where $W_{out}^{\dagger}$ is the pseudo-inverse of the language-model head and $W_{in}$ is the input embedding matrix. Intuitively, $W_{out}^{\dagger}$ undoes the mapping from hidden space to logits, and $W_{in}$ re-embeds the result as an input, so the composition carries a hidden state back to where the next layer expects to read it — without any training. The paper fixes $m = 10$ deliberation steps across all experiments.

Figure 6 (from paper): Impact of the reasoning horizon. A moderate deliberation phase optimises the crystallisation of raw perception into collaborative intent; too few steps under-develop the intent, too many add cost without benefit.

Because ILD reuses the prefilled states and never enters autoregressive text decoding, it is also the source of LACO’s latency advantage: the deliberation is a fixed, small number of parallelisable forward passes rather than an open-ended token-by-token generation.

6. Cross-Horizon Saliency Attribution (CHSA)

ILD produces a rich cache, but transmitting all of its prefill tokens would waste the bandwidth that latent communication was meant to save. CHSA is the token-axis cut: it scores every prefill token by how much the model’s own latent reasoning attended to it, and keeps only the most salient. This is an importance-selection operator, and its precise definition matters for how it relates to receiver-side decontamination, discussed later.

6.1 The saliency score

For each prefill token $j$, CHSA looks at how strongly the latent reasoning steps attended to it. The score aggregates attention across all heads and all layers by taking a maximum, then averages that maximum over the $T$ latent reasoning steps.

(3) $$S_j = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\max_{l\in[1,L],\,h\in[1,H]} \mathcal{A}_{t,l,h,j}\right)$$

$\mathcal{A}_{t,l,h,j}$ is the attention that reasoning step $t$, at layer $l$ and head $h$, places on prefill token $j$. Taking the maximum over layers and heads, rather than an average, is deliberate: it preserves a rare but critical cue — the paper’s example is a distant pedestrian detected by a single head — that averaging would dilute. Averaging over the $T$ reasoning steps then enforces temporal consistency, so that a token which mattered at only one transient step is not over-rewarded. The output is one scalar $S_j$ per token.

  • What the score captures: tokens the sender’s own reasoning repeatedly attended to across its deliberation horizon.
  • What it does not capture: whether a token is relevant to the receiver’s decision — the score is computed with no knowledge of the ego at all.

6.2 Top-K selection and cache assembly

Tokens are ranked by $S_j$ and the Top-K (a fixed retention ratio $\rho = 0.3$, i.e. 30% of tokens) are kept in their original sequential order. Their key/value vectors are pulled from the prefill cache to form $\mathcal{KV}_{salient}$, which is then concatenated with the latent reasoning trace.

(4) $$\mathcal{KV}_{CHSA} = \left[\,\mathcal{KV}_{salient}\ \|\ \mathcal{KV}_{latent}\,\right]$$

Two details of the assembly are easy to miss but matter. The salient tokens are kept in their original sequential order rather than sorted by score, so positional structure the model relies on is preserved; and they are concatenated with, not substituted for, the ILD latent trace, so the message carries both the raw evidence and the reasoning over it.

  • Order-preserving: Top-K tokens re-enter in sequence order, keeping the positional structure intact.
  • Evidence plus reasoning: the salient observations and the latent intent trace travel together in one cache.

The result $\mathcal{KV}_{CHSA}$ is a distilled cache that carries the model’s most decision-relevant observations together with the intent that ILD crystallised, at roughly 30% of the original token count. It is important to be exact about what has happened here: CHSA removes tokens the sender itself judged unimportant, using the sender’s own attention. It is a selection over what to send, not a modification of what is kept, and it is computed entirely on the sender before any receiver is involved.

Reading the retention sweep: The paper’s CHSA sweep shows driving score rising almost monotonically with retention — ORION reaches 31.05 at 10% retention, 35.48 at 30%, and 36.03 at 100%. Keeping every token is therefore the accuracy ceiling; 30% is the point at which the paper judges the bandwidth saving worth a small accuracy give-up. Selection here is a bandwidth-versus-fidelity trade-off, not a safety mechanism — the safety comes entirely from the depth cut in the next section.

7. Shallow-Stream Knowledge Distillation (SSKD)

SSKD is the operator that actually resolves identity confusion, and it does so along the depth axis. The section title deserves a note: the method body and the overview figure both call this component Shallow-Stream Knowledge Distillation, whereas the paper’s abstract calls it Structured Semantic Knowledge Distillation. The mechanism described under both names is identical — a truncation to shallow layers — and this page follows the method-section name.

7.1 The hypothesis

The entropy U-shape of Section 3.2 supports a sharp hypothesis: the high-entropy Shallow-Stream provides pure spatial priors and intents that are universally beneficial, whereas the Deep-Stream contains the complex, task-specific synthesis that triggers identity confusion. If that split is real, a receiver should accept the shallow stream and refuse the deep one.

Shallow-Stream — shared

  • High attention entropy; broad, global scene parsing.
  • Agent-agnostic spatial priors any vehicle can reuse.
  • Carries the collaborator’s observation, not its decision.

Deep-Stream — withheld

  • Entropy resurgence during action synthesis.
  • Perception geometrically entangled with ego-conditioned intent.
  • Injecting it here is precisely what causes identity confusion.

7.2 The mechanism: truncate to the first $L_{comm}$ layers

SSKD is a truncation. Rather than transmit the CHSA-distilled cache at every layer, the sender packs only its first $L_{comm}$ layers into the message $\mathcal{P}$. In the authors’ words, this distils the collaborator’s internal states into a stable structured contextual prior, stripped of the perceptual artifacts and decision-making noise that reside in deeper layers.

(5) $$\mathcal{P} = \mathcal{KV}_{CHSA}^{(1:L_{comm})}$$

The cut-off $L_{comm}$ is set to about 10% of the model’s total depth. Three properties of this cut matter for how it compares to a decontamination operator, and each is a limitation as much as a design choice.

  • Coarse: it acts at the granularity of whole layers, never within a state.
  • Content-independent: the same cut applies to every token and every channel regardless of what they hold.
  • Receiver-blind: it depends only on layer index, not on what the ego is trying to decide.

7.3 Asymmetric collaborative inference

The receiver then runs an asymmetric fusion rule. At shallow layers — those covered by the message — it concatenates its own key/value states with the collaborator’s, so the received spatial prior participates in reasoning. At deep layers it ignores the message entirely and attends only to its own states, so action synthesis is governed solely by the ego vehicle.

(6) $$\mathcal{KV}^{(l)}_{fused} = \left[\,\mathcal{KV}_{ego}^{(l)}\ \|\ \mathcal{P}^{(l)}\,\right] \qquad (l \le L_{comm})$$
(7) $$h^{(l)} = \mathrm{TransformerBlock}^{(l)}\!\left(h^{(l-1)},\ \mathcal{KV}_{ego}^{(l)}\right) \qquad (l > L_{comm})$$

Equations (6) and (7) are the whole of the safety argument. For layers up to the cut-off the ego reads a concatenation of its own and the received cache; beyond the cut-off the received cache is simply not in scope, so the deep, control-oriented computation can never attend to another vehicle’s states. The collaborator supplies context; the ego alone decides.

  • Shallow layers ($l \le L_{comm}$): fused — the received spatial prior widens the ego’s effective field of view.
  • Deep layers ($l > L_{comm}$): ego-only — action synthesis is provably isolated from the collaborator’s states.

7.4 The depth sweep confirms the hypothesis

The clearest single piece of evidence for the whole paper is the SSKD depth sweep: driving score is high for shallow cut-offs and collapses as more layers are shared, exactly as the entanglement hypothesis predicts. The table lists ORION’s ego (V0) driving score as the shared fraction grows.

  • That the optimum is a shallow band rather than a single point is what makes a fixed 10% cut-off robust across the three backbones.

This is the empirical counterpart of the earlier claim that the difficult part of the problem is deciding what of another agent’s cache to admit.

How the metrics are defined: Driving Score (DS) is the CARLA closed-loop product of route completion and an infraction penalty coefficient, so a high DS requires both reaching the goal and doing so without collisions or traffic-rule violations. Route Completion (RC) is the percentage of the route distance actually driven. Because DS multiplies completion by a penalty that shrinks with each infraction, an interface that improves RC but causes more collisions can still lose on DS — which is exactly the failure mode that identity confusion produces and that LACO avoids.

9. Relation to Decontaminated KV Selection

This section positions LACO against our own thesis, which treats cross-agent KV reuse as a subtractive, per-token, receiver-decision-relevance decontamination problem: when one agent reuses another agent’s KV, a contaminating component (the source agent’s identity and intent) must be removed from the reused states, per token, conditioned on what the receiver is actually trying to decide. KVCOMM sits at the other end of that axis, performing additive re-alignment (a learned or interpolated offset that re-renders a shared segment’s cache for the new context). LACO is the closest paper we have found on framing, so it is worth stating precisely what it does and does not share with our approach.

9.1 Does it name the contamination phenomenon? Yes.

This is the strongest overlap and it is genuine. LACO independently arrives at the same diagnosis we build on: naive full-KV fusion ‘can induce agent identity confusion, where the receiver over-attends to another agent’s internal states, leading to cross-agent representation entanglement.’ The vocabulary — identity confusion, entanglement, receiver over-attending — is almost exactly our framing of cross-agent KV contamination. Any reviewer of our work who knows LACO will expect us to distinguish ourselves from it, and we should treat the naming overlap as a real prior-art event on the framing axis rather than dismiss it.

9.2 Does it do importance selection? Yes, but sender-side.

CHSA is a genuine per-token Top-K selector, so the second overlap with our approach is also real: LACO scores each token and keeps the salient 30%. The crucial qualifier is what the score measures. The saliency $S_j$ in Equation (3) is computed from the sender’s own latent-reasoning attention over its own tokens — a max over the sender’s heads and layers, averaged over the sender’s deliberation steps. It is an importance measure in the SnapKV / H2O / SpecKV family: keep the tokens the model itself attended to most. It is not conditioned on the receiver’s decision, and it never asks whether a kept token will help or mislead the specific vehicle receiving it. Our selection, by contrast, is receiver-decision-relevance: a token is kept or cleaned according to what the receiver must decide, not according to how much the sender attended to it.

9.3 Does it subtract / decontaminate? No — it avoids by truncation.

This is the decisive distinction. The words ‘subtract’, ‘decontaminate’, and ‘remove a component’ appear nowhere in the paper. LACO resolves identity confusion by refusing to share the layers that carry it: SSKD transmits only the first ~10% of layers and the receiver’s deep layers stay ego-only. This is avoidance, not decontamination. Nothing is ever removed from a state that the receiver reuses; instead, the contaminated states are never admitted in the first place. CHSA does remove tokens, but that removal is importance pruning for bandwidth applied to the sender’s own prefill, not the removal of a contaminating identity component from a representation the receiver keeps. In our terms LACO makes a coarse, content-independent layer cut, whereas our approach performs a fine-grained, content-dependent subtraction within the states that are reused — removing the source-identity direction per token while retaining the shared spatial information in those same deep layers.

LACO (this paper)

  • Names the phenomenon: identity confusion / entanglement (yes)
  • Per-token selection: yes, but sender-side saliency importance
  • Contamination fix: layer-truncation avoidance (SSKD)
  • Subtraction from reused states: none
  • Domain: driving VLA, V2V, vision-token KV

Our decontamination thesis

  • Names the phenomenon: cross-agent KV contamination (yes)
  • Per-token selection: yes, by receiver-decision relevance
  • Contamination fix: subtractive removal of the identity component
  • Subtraction from reused states: the core operation, at all depths
  • Domain: LLM multi-agent systems, text-token KV

9.5 One-line differentiator and novelty-threat verdict

Differentiator: LACO avoids contamination by not sharing the entangled deep layers and selects tokens by sender-side saliency; our approach retains those deep layers and subtracts the contaminating identity component per token, conditioned on the receiver’s decision. Avoidance-by-truncation versus subtractive decontamination is the whole difference.

Before the prose verdict, the account can be compressed to four bullets — three genuine overlaps that must be cited, and the one thing that stays open.

  • Overlap 1 (framing): names the phenomenon in our vocabulary — identity confusion / entanglement / receiver over-attending.
  • Overlap 2 (selection): does per-token Top-K KV selection, though by sender-side saliency importance rather than receiver relevance.
  • Overlap 3 (positioning): explicitly cites KVCOMM and the anchor-pool alignment line our thesis engages.
  • Still open (mechanism): no subtractive decontamination of reused states and no receiver-relevance conditioning — safety comes entirely from coarse layer truncation.

Honest verdict on the novelty threat: LACO narrows our gap on the framing axis but does not close it on the mechanism axis. The full text confirms three things the abstract only hinted at — it names the contamination phenomenon in essentially our vocabulary, it does per-token Top-K selection, and it explicitly cites KVCOMM and the anchor-pool alignment line our thesis engages. Those overlaps are real and must be cited and distinguished, not ignored. What the full text also confirms is that LACO does no subtractive decontamination of reused states and no receiver-relevance conditioning: its safety comes entirely from a coarse layer-truncation that declines to reuse the contaminated part, and its selection is sender-side importance. The subtractive, per-token, receiver-decision-relevance decontamination of reused cross-agent KV therefore remains open, now in a slightly more crowded neighbourhood. The one caveat worth flagging plainly is domain transfer: because LACO lives in a driving VLA with vision-token KV rather than an LLM multi-agent text setting, a reviewer could argue our contribution is ‘LACO’s idea moved to text’ unless we make the mechanism difference — subtraction versus truncation, receiver-relevance versus sender-saliency — the explicit crux of the paper.

  • Cite it, do not ignore it: the framing overlap is real and a reviewer will know it.
  • Lead with the mechanism: subtractive decontamination of reused states versus layer-truncation avoidance is the defensible line.
  • Own the domain move: pre-empt the ‘LACO moved to text’ reading by showing receiver-relevance selection has no analogue in CHSA’s sender-side saliency.

10. Strengths and Limitations

LACO is a clean, training-free contribution whose empirical story is unusually legible, but its design also leans on assumptions that a careful reader should register. The two columns below summarise what the paper establishes well and where it leaves questions open.

Strengths

  • Training-free: adapts frozen ORION / SimLingo / LMDrive backbones with no fine-tuning, so the method is immediately portable.
  • The entropy U-shape gives a crisp, testable account of why deep fusion fails, and the depth sweep confirms it directly.
  • Exceeds visual feature sharing on driving score at a fraction of the bandwidth, across three backbones and both vehicles.
  • Closed-loop CARLA evaluation, not open-loop metrics, so the safety claim is tested in interaction.
  • The ablation cleanly separates efficiency from safety, so the reader can see exactly which operator earns which gain.

Limitations

  • Safety rests on a single global cut-off $L_{comm}$; the paper does not show it transfers across scenes, agent counts, or backbones with different depth profiles.
  • Because deep layers are never shared, genuinely decision-level collaborator information (its intent in its own deep space) is discarded, not integrated.
  • Evaluation is two-vehicle; whether the shallow-only prior scales to dense multi-agent traffic without re-entangling is untested.
  • Selection is sender-side; a collaborator cannot tailor what it sends to the specific receiver’s current decision, only to its own saliency.
  • The abstract and method disagree on the name of SSKD, a small sign the writing was finalised in haste.

10.1 Open questions

Several questions follow directly from the design and are, in our reading, the natural next steps — and, not coincidentally, the places where a decontamination formulation would have something to say.

  • Can the deep-layer intent of a collaborator ever be used safely, rather than discarded — for instance by cleaning it instead of truncating it?
  • Does a single global cut-off $L_{comm}$ remain safe as the number of collaborating agents grows and shallow priors begin to overlap?
  • Would a receiver-conditioned selection outperform sender-side saliency when the ego and collaborator face very different local decisions?
  • Does the entropy U-shape and its safe-shallow boundary transfer to text-based LLM multi-agent systems, where our own thesis operates?

11. Experimental Setup, Benchmark and Key Parameters

Because LACO is training-free, reproducing it amounts to fixing three hyperparameters on top of a frozen backbone and running the LangCoop closed-loop benchmark. This section records the benchmark, the sensor configurations that make the backbones comparable, and the small set of numbers that fully specify the method.

11.1 Benchmark and evaluation map

Evaluation is closed-loop in CARLA on the Town05 map used by the LangCoop benchmark. The map provides multiple predefined routes across intersections, roundabouts and multi-lane roads, which are the scenarios in which occlusion and cross-agent coordination actually matter. Closed-loop scoring is essential to the paper’s argument: identity confusion is a control failure, and only an interactive rollout can reveal that a hijacked policy brakes for a hazard in the wrong lane.

11.4 Why each choice is made the way it is

Three of LACO’s smaller design decisions are easy to overlook yet each is load-bearing; the cards below record the rationale the paper gives.

Max, not mean, over heads and layers

A max in Equation (3) preserves a cue that only a single head detects — the paper’s distant-pedestrian example — which an average would wash out. Selection must not discard rare but safety-critical tokens.

Closed-form $W_a$, no training

Constructing $W_a \approx W_{out}^{\dagger} W_{in}$ from the frozen head and embedding keeps the whole method training-free, so it drops onto any pretrained backbone without a data-collection or fine-tuning stage.

10% depth, not more

The depth sweep shows scores peaking at 10–30% and collapsing at full depth; 10% is chosen because it sits in the safe band while also minimising the bytes on the V2V link.
