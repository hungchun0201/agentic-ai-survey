JITServe

SLO-aware LLM Serving with Imprecise Request Information — just-in-time bandwidth allocation for diverse SLO classes (latency-sensitive, deadline-sensitive, compound)

arXiv:2504.20068 v3 · Dec 2025 SLO-aware Scheduling Service Goodput vLLM-based

Wei Zhang, Zhiyu Wu, Yi Mu, Rui Ning, Banruo Liu, Nikhil Sarda, Myungjin Lee, Fan Lai

University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign · Cisco Research · Google

1. Overview & Core Insight

Modern LLM serving stacks (vLLM, Sarathi-Serve, DistServe, Autellix) optimize one aggregate metric — usually average latency or throughput — under the assumption that all requests share a single SLO. JITServe's measurement of millions of real production requests plus 550+ user surveys finds the opposite: requests bring three fundamentally different SLO classes, and a real workload mixes all three at once.

1.1 Why now? — what changed in 2024–2025

Through 2023, production LLM serving was dominated by a single workload class — chatbot streaming — for which vLLM's FCFS scheduler with paged attention proved entirely sufficient. Two shifts in 2024 and 2025 disturbed that assumption at the same time. The first was the rise of agentic frameworks such as MetaGPT, AutoGen, and the deep-research and GAIA workloads, which now issue tens to hundreds of inter-dependent LLM calls per program under a single end-to-end deadline. The second was the LLM-as-tool pattern, in which RAG ingestion, batch summarization APIs, and AIOps pipelines depend on full responses arriving by a hard deadline rather than on streaming. The paper's measurement study, drawn from six organizations, more than 550 LLM users, and millions of requests sampled across LMSys Chatbot Arena, WildChat, MetaGPT, GAIA, and deep-research traces, confirms what every large SaaS LLM provider now observes in production: the same queue carries three coexisting SLO classes, and any scheduler that optimizes only one of them fails to satisfy at least one other.

1.2 What earlier serving systems left on the table

Each of the immediate prior generation solved one piece of the puzzle and left the rest exposed. JITServe argues that combining their solutions does not work — the assumptions are mutually inconsistent.

System (year) What it solved What it left on the table
vLLM (SOSP'23) High-throughput single-class serving via PagedAttention Assumes one SLO; FCFS HOL-blocks compound jobs
Sarathi-Serve (OSDI'24) Decode-prefill interleaving via chunked-prefill Throughput-optimal; chunk size is global, not per-SLO-class
DistServe (OSDI'24) Prefill / decode disaggregation across replicas Single SLO per request; static role assignment

2.3 Type C — Compound (the agentic case)

A deep-research agent answering a request to produce a market analysis of a given company issues, in a representative trace, twelve inter-dependent calls — a planning LLM, a web-search tool, a reader LLM, a reflector LLM, a writer LLM, and so on — within a single program deadline of 240 seconds. The latency of any individual call is not, by itself, a constraint; only the program-level deadline is. A scheduler that imitates SJF at the stage granularity, such as Autellix's PLAS, does not observe this dependency: it may continue to advance a downstream summarization while leaving the critical-path planner under-served.

3.2 Why the obvious fixes don't work

Before examining specific schedulers, it is worth ruling out two responses that practitioners commonly raise on first encountering this problem: separating the queue by SLO class, and improving the length predictor. Each is intuitively appealing, and each fails for reasons that are structural rather than a matter of engineering polish. Understanding why these direct approaches do not work clarifies what the design space actually demands.

Approach 1 — partition the queue, one queue per SLO class

The most direct response is to maintain three separate queues — one for latency-sensitive, one for deadline-sensitive, and one for compound — and serve each with its preferred policy. The difficulty is that capacity must be allocated to each queue in advance, while the workload mix varies by the minute. When 80% of an hour's arrivals are compound, the latency queue stands idle while the compound queue is over-subscribed; when the mix inverts, the opposite occurs. Deadlines also evolve continuously: a compound request that started with sixty seconds of slack may have only five remaining a minute later, raising the question of when, exactly, it should be promoted to a higher-priority queue. The act of redistributing capacity across queues every second is itself a scheduling problem with the same structure as the original — partitioning has not eliminated the problem so much as relocated it.

Approach 2 — improve the length predictor and apply Shortest-Job-First

The second response is to invest in a more accurate length predictor and use it as input to a classical Shortest-Job-First (SJF) policy. The paper's measurements (§2.2) show that even fine-tuned BERT and Llama-3.1 predictors exhibit substantial error at the tails of agent and deep-research traces, with the P5–P95 prediction interval exceeding five times the true response length. The deeper issue is that the predictor must run on the request's critical path: a forward pass through an 8B-parameter model to estimate the output length of one request takes longer than the decode kernels that would simply begin producing the response. The cost of producing an accurate prediction approaches the cost of producing the response itself, leaving no efficiency margin to exploit. JITServe's response (§5) is therefore to step away from point estimates entirely: a lightweight Quantile Regression Forest produces a conservative upper bound on response length, and that bound is refined as decoded tokens accumulate.

The paper's design begins by accepting both observations: the queue cannot usefully be partitioned by SLO class, and length prediction cannot simultaneously be cheap and accurate. The next subsection examines what existing schedulers do given these constraints. Each represents a different Pareto choice within an inadequate design space, and each is consequently insufficient on at least one SLO class.

3.3 What the existing policies actually do

Each of the policies in the table below was a defensible choice for the workload it was originally designed against; none is a poor scheduling rule on its own merits. Their common difficulty is that the production workload of 2025 no longer matches the workloads these systems were validated on. Figure 3 above gives the visual summary — every baseline exhibits a cliff-shaped goodput drop as the share of compound requests rises — and the table records what each policy optimises and where it fails on a three-class queue. The paper's §6.2 deploys all six against a common trace on identical hardware: sixteen A100 GPUs serving four LLM families.

Policy What it optimizes Original target workload Failure mode on a 3-class queue
FCFS (vLLM default) Arrival order — implicit fairness Single-class chatbot serving Long compound jobs HOL-block streaming chats; cascading violations
SJF / SRTF (classical) Average completion time Reliable length, single mean-latency target Length oracle unavailable; even given oracle, long deadline-sensitive requests are repeatedly displaced
LTR (Length-To-Run; QRF / BERT / Llama variants) SJF + learned length predictor Pure deadline-sensitive workloads Strongest baseline (1.3–1.7× gap); over-allocates to deadline class
LAS / Autellix PLAS (arXiv'25) Cumulative service per program Long programs of similar shape Weakest baseline (5.3–6.1× gap); per-stage fairness considers neither deadline nor TBT
EDF (earliest deadline first) Worst-case lateness Hard real-time, every job has deadline Streaming chats are TBT-bound, a different SLO shape
Chunked-prefill (Sarathi-Serve OSDI'24) Throughput + low TBT High-concurrency streaming chat Pacing technique, not SLO-aware; high TTLT on deadline class

Considered together, the six policies share a common structure: each is a local optimum that depends on a different unstated assumption about the workload. FCFS assumes that arrival order is a usable proxy for priority. SJF and LTR assume that response length is observable, or at least usefully predictable at a cost that does not dominate the request's own latency budget. PLAS assumes that fairness across program stages is a usable proxy for program deadline. EDF assumes that every unit of work carries an explicit, comparable deadline. Chunked-prefill assumes that throughput translates uniformly into goodput. None of these five assumptions holds simultaneously on the 2025 production workload, and any single scheduler is therefore confined to honoring at most one of them at a time. JITServe's design, presented from §4 onward, begins by setting all five aside and replacing them with a per-request, per-second computation of the marginal goodput contributed by an additional decode token — an approach that accommodates genuine uncertainty in the workload rather than attempting to engineer it away.

Theorem (informal, Appendix D.1): For each of FCFS, SJF, LAS, EDF, there exists an instance whose competitive ratio against the offline optimum is unbounded as the diversity ratio grows. The proof constructs adversarial mixes of the three SLO classes; the lower bound is matched empirically by the goodput collapse observed in Figure 11 of the paper.

4.2 The three quantities that drive every scheduling decision

At every scheduling tick, JITServe computes three quantities for each active request: an upper bound on the remaining response length $\hat L_{up}$, a predicted aggregate cost for the downstream stages of any compound request, and the minimum serving bandwidth $b_{min}$ that the request requires from this moment onward. These three quantities, together with the request's SLO class label, supply everything that GMAX needs in order to decide which requests to admit into the next batch and how much bandwidth each admitted request should receive. The remaining sections develop each quantity in detail; this subsection establishes the architectural-level intuition.

How $\hat L_{up}$ is estimated

Rather than predicting the expected response length and treating that estimate as exact, JITServe asks a Quantile Regression Forest (QRF) to provide a conservative upper bound — typically the P95 of the conditional length distribution, given the prompt features (token count, model identity, and lexical signatures of the prompt). The QRF executes in single-digit milliseconds and is therefore inexpensive enough to remain on the request's critical path; an 8B-parameter LLM used as a length oracle, by contrast, would itself be slower than starting to produce the response. The bound is refined at each subsequent tick as additional decoded tokens reveal the trajectory of the actual response, which causes the P5–P95 envelope to contract toward the ground truth. The asymmetry of this design is deliberate: an underestimate of length leads to insufficient reserved bandwidth and an irrecoverable SLO violation, whereas an overestimate merely produces a brief over-reservation, the surplus from which is returned to the capacity pool on the next tick.

jitserve/request_analyzer/prediction.py · the QRF call that produces $\hat L_{up}$ 

def predict(vectorizer, model, input):
    X_test_vec = vectorizer.transform(input)
    # KEY DESIGN: ask for the P95 quantile, not the mean. The two error
    # directions are asymmetric -- underestimating L_up causes insufficient
    # bandwidth reservation and an irrecoverable SLO miss; overestimating
    # only produces a brief over-reservation that the scheduler reclaims
    # on the next tick. A high quantile is therefore strictly preferable
    # even when the mean would be more "accurate" on average.
    y_pred_i: np.array = model.predict(X_test_vec.toarray(), quantiles=[0.95])
    return [int(element) for element in y_pred_i]


async def async_predict(vectorizer, model, input, request_info: RequestInfo) -> None:
    # KEY DESIGN: QRF runs in single-digit ms but is still wrapped in an
    # asyncio executor so it can never block the scheduler tick. Compare
    # with using a Llama-3-class LLM as a length oracle, which would
    # itself be slower than just decoding the response -- the cost of
    # being right would equal the cost of being wrong. QRF is cheap
    # enough that the wrapping is precaution, not necessity.
    loop = asyncio.get_event_loop()
    result = await loop.run_in_executor(None, predict, vectorizer, model, input)
    # The scheduler reads L_up_hat from RequestInfo.output_len on the next
    # tick. This is the only place L_up enters shared state.
    request_info.output_len = result[0]

The single line that materialises the upper bound is the quantiles=[0.95] argument passed to model.predict(); everything else in this file is plumbing — async dispatch, model loading from assets/qrf/, and a TCP socket server for cases where the QRF runs in a separate process. The output is written into RequestInfo.output_len, which the scheduler consults on the next tick when it computes $b_{min}$.

How compound requests are handled

A compound request is not a single LLM call but a graph of LLM and tool invocations linked by data dependencies, of which only a prefix has executed at the moment a scheduling decision must be made. Per-call length prediction is therefore not the binding constraint; the binding constraint is whether the sum of costs across all remaining stages will fit within the program-level deadline. JITServe addresses this by maintaining a database of historical (graph pattern, observed runtime) pairs, indexed by locality-sensitive hashing for fast retrieval. At every tick, the partial execution graph of each in-progress compound request is matched against the historical record. The match returns two values: the predicted cost of the immediately next stage, and the predicted aggregate cost of all the stages that remain. The first informs $\hat L_{up}$ for the current call. The second is subtracted from the program-level deadline to produce the time budget actually available to the current stage, which is the quantity that ultimately enters $b_{min}$. Without this aggregate prediction, a per-call length bound would tell the scheduler nothing about whether the program as a whole will meet its deadline.

jitserve/request_analyzer/graph_context.py · the entry point GMAX calls each tick to obtain $\Delta t$ for a compound stage 

def calculate_stage_ratio(self, num_stages, requests_per_stage, prompt,
                          collection_id, *, is_deepresearch=False,
                          stage_id=0, accumulate_stage_ratio=0.0):
    # KEY DESIGN: this is the entry point GMAX calls every tick to
    # learn what FRACTION of the program-level deadline the current
    # stage is allowed to consume. Per-call length prediction alone
    # does not answer that question; only the aggregate of remaining
    # stages does. Everything below trades prediction quality
    # against per-tick cost.
    if self.use_total_deadline:
        return 1.0   # degenerate: whole program budget for this stage

    default_ratio = self._default_ratio(is_deepresearch, stage_id)
    if self.graph_matching_mode == "none":
        # No historical match -- fall back to a hard-coded prior.
        # Cheapest mode, blind to which workflow this actually is.
        return default_ratio
    if self.graph_matching_mode == "static":
        # KEY DESIGN: match ONCE at admission and reuse the prediction
        # across all stages. Cheap (one LSH lookup per program) but
        # cannot react if the actual graph diverges from the match.
        return self._static_ratio(...)
    if self.graph_matching_mode == "online":
        # KEY DESIGN: re-match at EVERY stage as the partial graph grows.
        # The match becomes more accurate as more of the graph is observed,
        # at the cost of one LSH lookup per tick per active compound request.
        return self._online_ratio(...)
    return default_ratio


def _online_ratio(self, ..., is_deepresearch, stage_id):
    deepresearch_structure = self.collection_deepresearch_unfinished_dict[collection_id]

    best_match = self.dynamic_clustering.find_best_match(unfinished_graph)

        # corrects upward. Always biased toward over-reservation.
        return max(ratio_observed, ratio_default)

The dispatcher returns the fraction of the program-level deadline that the current stage is allowed to consume; the rest is reserved for the downstream stages whose costs were predicted by find_best_match. The matching modes implement the trade-off described in §4.2: static matches once at admission and reuses the prediction across all stages, while online re-matches at every stage as the partial execution graph grows, at the cost of running an LSH lookup on every tick. The historical database is extended by track_collection_completion() when a compound program finishes, which converts the recorded (graph, runtime) into a Graph object and registers it with DynamicClustering.

The meaning of the minimum serving bandwidth $b_{min}$

Given an upper bound $\hat L_{up}$ on the remaining response length and a remaining time budget $\Delta t$ before the SLO is breached, the minimum serving bandwidth is the rate, in tokens per second, at which the request must be served from the present moment onward so that it lands within its SLO under the upper-bound assumption:

$$b_{min}(r,t) \;=\; \frac{\hat L_{up}(r,t) - L_{done}(r,t)}{\Delta t(r,t)}$$

The interpretation of $\Delta t$ is what differs across the three SLO classes, and it is the only per-class difference. For a latency-sensitive request, $\Delta t$ is the streaming-SLO inter-token interval multiplied by the number of remaining tokens to produce, so that $b_{min}$ converges to the per-token rate the user expects. For a deadline-sensitive request, $\Delta t$ is the absolute deadline minus the current time, so $b_{min}$ is the rate at which the entire remaining response must be produced in order to land at the deadline. For a compound request, $\Delta t$ is the program-level deadline reduced by the predicted aggregate cost of the downstream stages, as described above; $b_{min}$ is therefore the rate at which the current call must run in order to leave enough budget for the calls that follow.

$b_{min}$ is a rate target rather than an absolute reservation. GMAX guarantees that every active request whose deadline remains reachable receives at least $b_{min}$ during the current tick, but capacity that exceeds the sum of all such reservations is then redistributed to whichever requests yield the largest marginal goodput. Because $L_{done}$ grows and $\Delta t$ shrinks each tick, $b_{min}$ is recomputed continuously, and a request that began with a comfortable bandwidth requirement may rise to demand more capacity later if its actual response is approaching the QRF upper bound. This per-tick recomputation is the precise sense in which scheduling decisions are made just-in-time rather than at admission time.

jitserve/scheduler/policy.py · $b_{min}$ as a finish-time predictor combined with a deadline-ratio penalty 

class SLOPolicy(BasePolicy):

    def reward_estimation(self, seq_group):
        cur_time           = time.time()
        decode_len         = seq_group.seqs[0].get_decode_len()         # = L_done
        predict_output_len = seq_group.request_info.output_len           # = L_up_hat from QRF
        # KEY DESIGN: this is the numerator of paper equation (1):
        #     b_min = (L_up_hat - L_done) / delta_t
        # remain_len shrinks every tick as decode_len grows; if the actual
        # response approaches L_up_hat, remain_len -> 0 and the request
        # naturally drops off the priority queue. If the actual response
        # OVERSHOOTS L_up_hat, remain_len goes negative -- handled by the
        # preemption rule (paper §7.2) which fires when the bound was wrong.
        remain_len = predict_output_len - decode_len

        unit_time   = self._get_interval_time()
        remain_time = self._compute_remain_time(seq_group, remain_len, unit_time)
        serve_time  = cur_time - seq_group.arrival_time

        if seq_group.request_type == RequestType.LATENCY:
            reward = self._estimate_latency_reward(...)
        elif seq_group.request_type in (RequestType.THROUGHPUT, RequestType.COLLECTIVE):
            reward = self._estimate_throughput_reward(...)
        return reward, remain_time

    def _compute_remain_time(self, seq_group, remain_len, unit_time):
        # KEY DESIGN: this is where the per-class delta_t of paper §4.2 enters.
        # The same formula b_min = (L_up - L_done) / delta_t is used everywhere;
        # only delta_t differs.
        if seq_group.request_type == RequestType.COLLECTIVE:
            # Compound (multi-call) request: delta_t for the CURRENT call is
            # the program-level deadline reduced by the predicted aggregate
            # cost of all DOWNSTREAM stages. allocation_control() walks the
            # collection's siblings and sums their predicted remaining time;
            # what is left is the budget for this stage.
            return max(self.allocation_control(seq_group), 1e-6)
        # Single-call (latency-sensitive or deadline-sensitive): delta_t is
        # local. unit_time is the average scheduler-tick interval, so this
        # is "predicted wall-clock to finish remain_len tokens at the
        # current scheduling rate" -- a finish-time estimate, equivalent
        # to b_min's denominator after a change of variables.
        return max(unit_time * remain_len, 1e-6)

    def get_priority(self, seq_group):
        reward, remain_time = self.reward_estimation(seq_group)
        # KEY DESIGN: density = reward / remain_time = MARGINAL GOODPUT
        # PER UNIT TIME. This is the operational form of b_min from
        # paper §4.2 -- a request whose remain_time is shrinking
        # (whose b_min is therefore rising) gets a proportionally
        # larger priority next tick. Negated because Python's heap
        # is min-heap; the request with the LARGEST density floats
        # to the front of the GMAX priority queue.
        density = reward / remain_time
        return (-density, remain_time)

The code does not store $b_{min}$ as an explicit variable; instead it computes the equivalent ratio in two halves. remain_len = L_up_hat − L_done is the numerator of equation (1) above, and remain_time from _compute_remain_time is its denominator. get_priority divides reward by remain_time, so a request whose remaining time is shrinking — that is, whose $b_{min}$ is rising — receives a proportionally larger priority on the next tick. _compute_remain_time is also where the per-class behaviour of $\Delta t$ enters: for compound (COLLECTIVE) requests the call to allocation_control() subtracts the predicted aggregate cost of the downstream stages, exactly the operation described in §4.2.

def _desired_decode_len(self, seq_group, cur_time):
    # KEY DESIGN: this IS the latency-class b_min, expressed inversely.
    # Instead of "tokens per second I must produce", we ask "how many
    # tokens should I have produced by now to honour the TBT_constraint
    # rate target?" The denominator is TBT_constraint = the contracted
    # time per token; the numerator is elapsed time since first token.
    # _latency_service_gain compares this with the actual output_len
    # below and penalises any shortfall, which is the per-tick
    # mechanism that escalates a falling-behind request in priority.
    metrics = seq_group.concord_metrics
    if metrics.TTFT is None:
        return 0.0
    req_ttft = min(metrics.TTFT, seq_group.TTFT_constraint)
    return (cur_time - req_ttft -
            seq_group.arrival_time) / seq_group.TBT_constraint


def _latency_service_gain(self, seq_group, cur_time, ...):
    ...
    desire_decode_len = self._desired_decode_len(seq_group, cur_time)
    if delta_output > 0:
        if desire_decode_len <= 0:
            service += delta_output
        else:
            # decode_ratio < 1 means the request is BEHIND its rate target.
            decode_ratio = self._clamp_ratio(output_len, desire_decode_len)
            # KEY DESIGN: raise to penalty_factor. A small lag in decode
            # rate produces a small reduction in service gain, but a
            # large lag produces a much larger reduction -- next-tick
            # density (= reward / remain_time) drops disproportionately,
            # so the request's GMAX priority falls. This is what makes
            # the system shed irrecoverable requests gracefully under
            # load (paper §10.4, Fig 15) instead of letting one
            # struggling request pull everyone else down.
            service += decode_ratio**self.penalty_factor * delta_output * RequestPhaseWeight.DECODE.value
    return service

For latency-sensitive requests, $b_{min}$ is most naturally read as the per-token rate target set by TBT_constraint. _desired_decode_len implements this target directly: given the elapsed time since first token, divided by TBT_constraint, it returns the number of decoded tokens the request should have produced by now in order to remain on its SLO trajectory. The latency-reward path in SLOPolicy compares this target with the actual output_len and penalises any shortfall, which is the per-tick mechanism by which a falling-behind latency request is escalated in priority.

5. Request Analyzer — Living with Imprecise Information

5.1 Why predict an upper bound, not a point estimate

Most prior systems attempt to predict the expected response length and treat the predicted value as exact. The paper's measurements indicate that this approach is not viable in practice: at the tail of the distribution, even fine-tuned Llama-3-based predictors deviate from the true length by more than a factor of five (Figure 2b). JITServe departs from this design and asks instead for a conservative upper bound $\hat L_{up}$. The motivation lies in the asymmetry of the two error directions.

Underestimating the response length

If the predicted length is below the true length, the bandwidth reserved for this request is correspondingly too low, the request fails to complete by its deadline, and its contribution to service goodput becomes zero. Once the deadline has passed, the cost is unrecoverable.

Overestimating the response length

If the predicted length exceeds the true length, the request finishes earlier than expected and the surplus reserved bandwidth is returned to the global capacity pool, where it remains available to GMAX for allocation to other requests on the next tick. The cost in this direction is a brief over-reservation, which is recoverable within the same scheduling cycle.

A Quantile Regression Forest (QRF) is a natural choice for this purpose: given the prompt features as input, it directly estimates the conditional quantiles of the response length distribution. JITServe queries a high quantile — for example, the P95 — rather than the mean, which yields the conservative upper bound the system requires.

The paper's predictor-comparison sub-figures are tightly bound to LaTeX labels and are not exposed as standalone images on the arXiv HTML rendering. The supporting numbers are reported in the §5.1 prose: the QRF predictor runs in single-digit milliseconds, while BERT and Llama-3-based predictors are an order of magnitude slower; the QRF's P5–P95 interval narrows toward the ground truth as decoded tokens accumulate.

5.2 Pattern-Graph Matching for Compound Requests

A compound request is not a single LLM call but a graph, in which the nodes are LLM and tool invocations and the edges are data dependencies between them. Most production agents are templated: the same orchestration code is invoked repeatedly with different inputs, and the resulting execution graphs are structurally similar across invocations. JITServe relies on this regularity. It maintains a database of observed (graph, runtime) pairs and, for each in-progress compound request, matches the partial execution graph against the historical record to estimate the cost of the remaining stages.

Pattern-graph matching captures this aggregate structure directly, whereas per-call length predictors do not.

5.3 Computing the minimum serving bandwidth

Given an upper bound $\hat L_{up}$ on the response length and the remaining time $\Delta t$ before the request's SLO is breached, the minimum serving bandwidth — that is, the rate in tokens per second at which the request must be served from the present moment onward in order to satisfy the SLO under the upper bound — is given by:

(1) $$b_{min}(r,t) \;=\; \frac{\hat L_{up}(r,t) - L_{done}(r,t)}{\Delta t(r,t)}$$

Here $L_{done}(r,t)$ denotes the number of tokens already generated for request $r$ by time $t$. As time advances and $L_{done}$ grows, both the numerator and the denominator decrease; the resulting ratio is the quantity by which GMAX ranks requests.

The interpretation of $\Delta t$ depends on the SLO class. For a latency-sensitive request, $\Delta t$ is the streaming-SLO rate multiplied by the number of remaining tokens. For a deadline-sensitive request, it is $D - t$, where $D$ is the explicit absolute deadline. For a compound request, $\Delta t$ is the remaining program-level deadline reduced by the predicted aggregate cost of the downstream stages.

# Algorithm 1 — GMAX (one scheduling tick)
def gmax_schedule(active_requests, capacity_C, batch_size_B):
    # 1. compute per-request demand
    for r in active_requests:
        r.b_min      = (r.L_up_hat - r.L_done) / r.delta_t
        r.priority   = r.b_min                      # tighter requests first
        r.margin_gp  = marginal_goodput(r)        # dgoodput / dbandwidth

    # 2. reserve b_min for every reachable request (SLO compliance)
    reachable = [r for r in active_requests
                 if r.delta_t > 0 and r.b_min <= capacity_C]
    reachable.sort(key=lambda r: -r.priority)
    chosen, used = [], 0
    for r in reachable:
        if used + r.b_min <= capacity_C and len(chosen) < batch_size_B:
            chosen.append(r); used += r.b_min

    # 3. distribute SURPLUS by marginal goodput
    surplus = capacity_C - used
    while surplus > 0:
        candidates = [r for r in chosen if r.can_take_more()]
        if not candidates: break
        winner = max(candidates, key=lambda r: r.margin_gp)
        share  = min(surplus, winner.cap_increment())
        winner.bandwidth += share
        surplus          -= share

    # 4. compose a batch of similar input lengths
    return group_by_input_length(chosen)

6.3 Why this outperforms single-priority schedulers

The reservation step in line 2 of Algorithm 1 gives GMAX a behavior that interpolates between two well-known regimes. When capacity is tight, the reservations consume essentially all of the available bandwidth and each request receives exactly the bandwidth its SLO requires; in this regime GMAX behaves much like Earliest Deadline First. When capacity is loose, surplus remains after all reservations are honored, and the surplus distribution step in line 3 directs the additional bandwidth to whichever requests yield the largest marginal goodput; in this regime GMAX behaves much like SJF or LAS. The same algorithm covers all three SLO classes; the only per-class difference is in how $\Delta t$ and the marginal-goodput function are defined.

The form of the marginal-goodput function in each SLO class:
  • Latency-sensitive. The marginal goodput is zero whenever the current serving rate already meets or exceeds the streaming SLO rate, since delivery faster than the user's reading speed contributes no additional value. The marginal goodput is positive only while the request is currently behind its required pace.
  • Deadline-sensitive. The marginal goodput is zero once the request has been allocated enough bandwidth to complete by its deadline $D$, and positive otherwise — additional bandwidth has value only insofar as it converts an as-yet-unmet deadline into a met one.
  • Compound. The marginal goodput is proportional to the increase in the probability that the program-level deadline is met if the current stage is accelerated by a single decode step. Because the program deadline depends on the sum across remaining stages, this proportionality requires the predicted downstream cost provided by pattern-graph matching.

7. Batch Composition & Preemption

7.1 Why similar-length batching matters

Even with Flash Decoding, the per-layer attention kernel runs at a cost determined by the longest sequence in the batch. A batch that mixes one 8K-token request with four 200-token requests therefore executes at the speed of the 8K-token request, and the four shorter requests consume their slots without producing a corresponding increase in throughput.

The group_by_input_length step in GMAX partitions the selected request set into batches of similar input length and submits them to the backend in sequence. The partitioning is a one-pass greedy procedure: sort the chosen requests by input length and split the sorted sequence at the largest gaps.

7.2 Preemption — discontinuing an in-progress request

The reasoning developed so far depends on $\hat L_{up}$ being a useful upper bound on the response length. When the actual response continues to grow beyond this bound, the requested $b_{min}$ rises sharply, since the numerator of equation (1) no longer contracts as new tokens are generated. GMAX therefore requires an explicit rule for this contingency, in which a request is removed from the reserved set before its SLO is satisfied.

8. Theoretical Guarantees

8.1 Optimal scheduling is NP-hard

Even given complete prior information about every request — its response length, deadline, and dependency graph — maximizing service goodput on a fixed pool of GPUs is NP-hard. The paper establishes this by reduction from the multi-dimensional knapsack problem (Appendix C). This result rules out the existence of an exact online algorithm of polynomial complexity, and the design space therefore reduces to approximation algorithms accompanied by provable competitive ratios.

13.3 SLO threshold calibration (§6.1)

The numerical SLO thresholds used throughout the evaluation are derived from the P95 latencies measured across one thousand real DeepSeek API calls: TTFT of approximately two seconds, TBT of approximately one hundred milliseconds, end-to-end latency of twenty seconds for deadline-sensitive requests, and twenty seconds per stage for compound requests. The aggregated P95 numbers themselves appear in §6.1, but the raw call records were not released with the paper. Reproducing identical thresholds therefore requires access to a comparable production API or a similar measurement campaign.

Reproducibility summary: The §6 main results — service goodput on the chatbot, deep research, agentic code generation, and math reasoning workloads — are reproducible in principle. Every dataset and framework required for those experiments is publicly available, and the numerical SLO thresholds are documented in the paper. The §2.1 motivation numbers, by contrast, depend in part on industrial production traces and on raw user-study responses that the authors retain privately; only aggregate statistics from those sources appear in the paper, and replicating the exact §2.1 figures would require comparable industrial collaborations rather than off-the-shelf public data.
