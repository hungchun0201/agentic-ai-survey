Attention Entropy is a Key Factor

Why splitting a context into independently encoded pieces degrades a full-attention LLM — traced to irregularly high attention entropy — and two training-free repairs: shared attention sinks and selective attention.

ACL 2025 Long arXiv:2412.16545 Parallel Context Encoding Attention Analysis Training-free

Zhisong Zhang, Yan Wang, Xinting Huang, Tianqing Fang, Hongming Zhang, Chenlong Deng, Shuaiyi Li, Dong Yu

Tencent AI Lab

1. Why Parallel Context Encoding Matters — and Why It Fails

By 2025, the dominant cost in LLM serving has migrated from generating tokens to processing contexts. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) prepends dozens of retrieved documents to every query; in-context learning (ICL) prompts carry tens to hundreds of demonstrations; agentic systems resubmit an ever-growing history of tool outputs on every one of their 50–200 inference calls. These contexts share one structural property: they are concatenations of pieces that are logically independent of one another, yet full self-attention — the mechanism every mainstream decoder-only LM is pre-trained with — ignores that structure entirely. Encoding an $N$-token context requires scoring $\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)$ token pairs, so a 16K-token RAG prompt costs roughly $1.3\times 10^{8}$ pair computations even when its 16 retrieved passages never need to read one another. The computation therefore grows quadratically while the information that actually crosses piece boundaries stays close to zero.

Parallel context encoding is the natural response: split the context into $P$ sub-pieces, encode each piece independently under its own causal mask, and let only the final query tokens attend across all pieces. The attention cost drops to approximately $1/P$ of the full-attention figure, and a second benefit follows for serving systems — the KV cache of a document encoded in isolation no longer depends on which other documents happen to sit beside it, so per-document caches become reusable across requests regardless of retrieval order.

1.1 The failure, at trace granularity

The difficulty is that mainstream LMs have never seen a parallel mask during training, and applying one naively degrades them severely. The paper's measurements with Llama-3.1-8B make the damage concrete. Consider a needle-in-a-haystack recall task over a 16K-token context, the synthetic stand-in for a RAG assistant that must retrieve one specific fact from its passages. With ordinary full attention the model answers 97.25% of queries correctly. When the same context is split into $P=16$ independently encoded pieces, accuracy falls to 2.00%; at $P=32$ it reaches 0.00%.

1.2 Why the obvious fixes do not close the gap

The first suspect is position arithmetic: perhaps the pieces merely need their RoPE position IDs assigned more carefully. The paper already performs this correction — each piece counts its own positions toward a shared target length — and an alternative serialized assignment, evaluated in the appendix, reproduces the same qualitative failure. Position bookkeeping is therefore not the cause. A second reflex is fine-tuning: teach the model the parallel mask, as CEPE does by training a dedicated context encoder with cross-attention layers.

The fundamental obstacle is a distribution shift in the model's internal states rather than an interface mismatch. A full-attention LM trained on ordinary text develops a characteristic internal geometry: the initial tokens of the sequence become attention sinks whose key states carry abnormally small norms, and key-state norms grow steadily along the sequence dimension. Parallel encoding replicates this geometry once per piece — $P$ sink tokens and $P$ norm ramps where training produced exactly one of each.

1.4 Contributions and headline numbers

With the baseline damage established above (97.25% to 2.00% recall at $P=16$; 72.20% to 19.80% ICL accuracy at $P=64$), the paper's four contributions can be read as a diagnosis-and-treatment arc.

1. Fair, systematic measurement

Direct full-attention vs. parallel-encoding comparisons on LM, ICL, RAG, and synthetic recall at 4K/8K/16K lengths and $P \in \{2,\dots,64\}$, holding the model, data, and position-encoding treatment fixed.

2. Attention entropy as indicator

Query-token attention entropy $H(p)=-\sum_i p_i \log p_i$ rises irregularly under parallel encoding and tracks performance loss with Pearson $R\approx 0.95$ across lengths and parallel degrees.

3. A mechanistic chain

Per-piece sink tokens carry abnormally small key norms; key norms restart their ramp in every piece; attention logits shrink; the softmax flattens; entropy rises. Each link is measured on real model internals.

4. Two training-free repairs

A shared prefix absorbs sink behavior once for all pieces; selective attention hard-selects the top-$K$ pieces per attention operation and re-normalizes. Both lower entropy and narrow the gap; combined they yield balanced recovery across tasks.

The key insight of the paper is that the failure of naive parallel encoding is neither a position-encoding artifact nor an inherent limit of KV reuse, but a measurable internal-state anomaly. Attention entropy over query tokens serves as a white-box health indicator for any system that stitches independently encoded KV caches together, and two attention-side interventions that lower this entropy recover much of the loss without touching a single weight.

2. What Parallel Context Encoding Actually Changes

Before diagnosing the failure it helps to state precisely what a system does when it encodes a context in parallel. Two things change relative to ordinary full attention, and only two: the way position identifiers are assigned, and the shape of the attention mask. Everything else — the weights, the RoPE scheme, the softmax — is left exactly as the pre-trained model shipped it.

2.1 Attention entropy, the metric under study

A single attention head, when it processes one query position, produces a probability distribution over the preceding tokens: how much weight it places on each key. Attention entropy summarizes the shape of that distribution in one number. It is the Shannon entropy of the attention weights, and it distinguishes a head that spreads its attention thinly over hundreds of tokens from a head that concentrates almost all of its weight on one or two.

(1) $$H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i, \qquad p_i = \text{attention weight on key } i$$

A high value of $H(p)$ means the query token distributes its attention broadly, which suggests a global, diffuse reading of the context. A low value reflects a concentrated pattern, which is precisely what retrieval-style tasks require — the head has found the token it needs and is ignoring the rest. The paper's central empirical claim is that parallel encoding pushes $H(p)$ upward on the final query tokens, and that this upward push is what correlates with the loss in task accuracy.

2.2 Two modifications: position counting and the mask

The first modification concerns position. Under full attention, three context pieces of lengths $L_1$, $L_2$, $L_3$ are laid out sequentially and assigned positions running from $0$ up to $L_1+L_2+L_3$. Under parallel encoding no order exists among the pieces, so each piece counts its own tokens starting from $0$. When pieces have unequal lengths the paper takes the maximum length as a shared target and distributes each piece's positions evenly toward it, using RoPE's ability to assign real-valued position IDs. The second modification concerns the mask: every token inside a piece attends only to earlier tokens within the same piece, while only the final query tokens attend across all pieces.

2.3 The compute saving, and why serving systems want it

The saving is what motivates the whole line of work, so it is worth deriving. Encoding $N$ tokens under full causal attention requires scoring every token against all its predecessors, which is $\frac{1}{2}N(N+1)$ token pairs. Splitting the context evenly into $P$ pieces of $N/P$ tokens and encoding each independently reduces the pair count to the expression in Equation (2), approximately $\frac{1}{P}$ of the full-attention figure.

(2) $$P \cdot \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{N}{P}\left(\frac{N}{P}+1\right) = \frac{1}{2P}\,N(N+P) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{P}\cdot\frac{1}{2}N^2$$

For serving infrastructure the second benefit matters as much as the raw FLOP reduction. Because a piece is encoded without reference to its neighbors, the key-value cache it produces is position-independent: the same document, demonstration, or tool output yields the same KV tensor no matter which request it lands in or where in the prompt it sits. That property is what lets a system cache one KV tensor per document and reuse it across requests — the foundation under prefix caching, CacheBlend, EPIC, and TurboRAG.

3. Diagnosis: Entropy Rises, and Why

This section is the analytical core of the paper. It establishes three facts in sequence: that naive parallel encoding degrades performance and that the degradation deepens with the parallel degree; that the degradation is accompanied by irregularly high attention entropy on the query tokens, correlated at Pearson $R\approx 0.95$; and that the elevated entropy can itself be traced to measurable irregularities in the model's hidden-state scales.

3.1 The degradation deepens with the parallel degree

The table reports Llama-3.1-8B at 8K tokens as the parallel degree $P$ climbs from full attention ($P=1$, listed as Full) to $P=64$. Reading down each column, every task worsens monotonically, but the tasks that depend most on pinpoint retrieval fall fastest. Synthetic recall drops from near-perfect to a total floor of zero once the context is fragmented into sixteen or more pieces; RAG substring exact match degrades more gently because its answers can survive on partial context; language-modeling perplexity roughly doubles.

3.4 Where the entropy comes from: hidden-state scales

Elevated entropy is a symptom; the paper pursues its cause into the model's internal states. Entropy is high when the softmax over keys is flat, and the softmax is flat when the attention logits feeding it are small. The left panel of Figure 2 confirms the first link: averaged absolute attention logits are smaller under parallel encoding. The right panel shows that key-state L2 norms ordinarily grow along the sequence but under parallel encoding are smaller overall, with each piece restarting the ramp from a low value.

Figure 2a: Averaged absolute attention-logit scale. Parallel (orange) drops far below Full (blue) and restarts at each piece boundary.
Figure 2b: Key-state L2 norm. Norms ramp along the sequence under Full; under Parallel each piece restarts low, and the per-piece sink tokens show dramatically small norms.

Assembling these measurements gives a chain from the structural change to the observed failure. Each link in the chain below is measured on real model internals rather than assumed, though the paper notes that the deepest step — why per-piece encoding produces small-norm sink states in the first place — involves interactions with LayerNorm and the MLP that it leaves to future work.

4. Two Training-free Repairs

Correlation does not establish causation, so the paper tests whether attention entropy is a genuine lever on performance rather than a passive symptom. If deliberately lowering the entropy recovers accuracy, the causal reading is supported. The paper introduces two mechanisms that reduce entropy in different ways, both applied at inference time without altering a single weight.

Figure 4 (from paper): The two entropy-reduction methods. A shared Sink prefix (green, top) is prepended to every sub-piece so the sink behavior is absorbed once rather than per piece; SEL (bottom row) hard-selects a subset of pieces for the query to attend to, marking the rest with crosses.

4.1 Attention sinks — give every piece a shared prefix to sink into

A well-known finding is that the initial tokens of a sequence act as attention sinks: heads that have nothing useful to attend to allocate their otherwise unused attention mass to these early positions, which lets the rest of the distribution stay sharp. In parallel encoding each piece grows its own set of sinks, and the model — trained on sequences with a single leading sink — has never seen this multi-sink arrangement. The repair is to prepend the same short prefix to every sub-piece, so the sink behavior is absorbed by that shared prefix rather than manufactured afresh inside each piece.

A striking detail is that the content of the prefix barely matters: preliminary experiments report that even a prefix of nothing but linebreaks is effective, indicating that the prefix's function is to absorb unneeded attention rather than to convey meaning. For reproducibility the authors use a simple instruction as the shared prefix and prepend it to each piece. By eliminating the per-piece sink tokens, the shared prefix raises the attention logits back toward their full-attention scale and lowers the entropy.

4.2 Selective attention — hard-select the pieces worth reading

Rather than raising the logits so the softmax sharpens on its own, selective attention forces the sharpening: for each attention operation it scores the sub-pieces, keeps only the top-$K$ of them, masks out the rest, and re-normalizes. Where the naive scheme lets a query token spread thin weight across all $P$ pieces, selective attention confines the weight to the two or three pieces most likely to hold the answer, which drives the entropy down.

Algorithm 1 states the four steps precisely. The input is the attention probability tensor $p_{in}$ of shape $[N_{layer}, N_{head}, L_{query}, L_{key}]$; the output is the modified tensor $p_{out}$. Grouping reduces the key dimension to one score per sub-piece, using the sum of that piece's top-5 attention values as the reduction — a choice found to work better than sum or average.

# Algorithm 1 — Selective Attention (parameter-free, applied per attention op)
# Input : p_in  attention probabilities, shape [N_layer, N_head, L_query, L_key]
# Output: p_out modified attention probabilities

s_group = group_key(p_in)      # per sub-piece score along the Key dim:
                               #   reduce each group via the SUM of its top-5 values
                               #   -> shape [N_layer, N_head, L_query, P]
i_sel   = top_k(s_group)       # keep the top-K sub-pieces (default K=2)
                               #   -> shape [N_layer, N_head, L_query, K]
m       = expand_mask(i_sel)   # piece choice -> token-level mask
                               #   -> shape [N_layer, N_head, L_query, L_key]
p_m     = p_in * m             # zero out tokens in unselected pieces
p_out   = p_m / p_m.sum(-1)    # re-normalize each row back to sum 1
return p_out

Between grouping and selection an optional reduction can aggregate the group scores across the query, head, or layer dimensions before the top-$K$ is taken, which forces a common choice of pieces instead of a per-query one. The paper applies no aggregation for LM (which benefits from diverse per-position context) and aggregates over the head and query dimensions by default for the other tasks.

5. Results: Lowering Entropy Recovers Accuracy

The experiments serve a single purpose: to check whether the two interventions that lower attention entropy also raise task accuracy, which is the test that turns the §3 correlation into a causal claim. They do, and the pattern of recovery is itself informative — the two methods help different tasks, because they lower entropy through different mechanisms.

5.1 Both methods reduce entropy and lift performance

Figure 5 sweeps the parallel degree from 1 to 64 on Llama-3.1-8B at 8K and plots, for each task, both performance (top row) and averaged attention entropy (bottom row). The naive Parallel curve degrades steeply and its entropy rises steeply with the parallel degree; each intervention pulls both curves back toward the full-attention dashed line. With shared sinks the entropy is lower than the naive scheme but still grows above the full-attention level, whereas with selective attention the entropy can fall even below full attention.

5.2 The two repairs help different tasks

Selective attention is more helpful for RAG and synthetic recall, because those tasks are retrieval by nature. Shared sinks are more beneficial for ICL, because a classification prompt often needs evidence from more demonstration examples than the default $K=2$ selection would admit. Combining the two yields the most balanced profile across the four task families. At the hardest setting of $P=64$ the selective method recovers synthetic SubEM from the naive floor of $0$ to $24.88$, and ICL accuracy from $10.80$ to $33.00$.

5.3 Selective attention has task-dependent knobs

Because selection is parameter-free it can be tuned freely per task, and the paper's ablation at $P=64$ shows that the tuning genuinely matters. 5.4 A control: value states matter too

The paper checks the objection that the whole problem lives in the key states with an oracle experiment: it replaces the parallel key states with full-attention key states while leaving the value states parallel. Figure 6 shows that, except for the LM task, these oracle key states do not always beat the training-free methods, which means the value states carry irregularity of their own and also contribute to the failure. The entropy chain built on key norms is therefore an important part of the story but not the whole of it.

Figure 6 (from paper): Value-only parallel encoding: parallel value states are kept while key states are replaced with full-attention (oracle) keys. Except on LM, the Oracle-K curve does not consistently exceed the training-free P+Both curve, indicating that value states also carry irregularity.

6. Where This Sits in the KV-Reuse Landscape

For an agentic serving stack the interest in this paper is less about language modeling and more about the KV cache. Position-independent per-piece encoding is the same primitive that lets a system cache one KV tensor per document or per tool output and reuse it across requests. This paper is the analysis layer beneath that family: it explains, in terms of a measurable internal-state quantity, why the reuse degrades quality when applied naively, and it offers two training-free interventions that recover much of the lost quality.

System How it reuses KV Relation to this paper
CacheBlend
(EuroSys'25)		 Concatenates per-chunk KV caches, then selectively recomputes a small fraction of tokens to restore cross-chunk attention Recomputes on the input side; this paper repairs the same quality gap from the attention side, without recomputation
EPIC
(ICML'25)		 Position-independent caching: recompute a few tokens at each chunk boundary so any chunk can be reused at any position Shares the position-independence goal; the attention-entropy indicator here is a diagnostic for when such reuse is safe
TurboRAG / Block-Attention
(2024)		 Pre-compute per-document KV offline; fine-tune the model to accept the concatenated caches Pays a training cost to fix the same distribution shift this paper diagnoses and repairs training-free
Prefix caching
(vLLM, SGLang)		 Reuses the KV of a shared prompt prefix across requests — a strict, order-preserving special case Safe precisely because it preserves order and a single sink; this paper explains why dropping that guarantee costs quality

Aggressive cross-request KV reuse — the kind that fragments a long agent history or a document set into many independently encoded pieces — is the high-parallel-degree regime where this paper shows the sharpest quality collapse, and where an entropy monitor would register the largest deviation. A system that reuses caches at low parallel degrees, preserves a shared prefix as a sink, and prunes the pieces each query reads is operating in the regime the paper shows to be recoverable. The shared-prefix intervention composes with existing recompute-based schemes rather than competing with them.

7. Assessment and Takeaways

The paper's contribution is diagnostic rather than architectural: it does not propose a new serving system, it explains why an existing efficiency trick fails and supplies two low-overhead interventions that soften the failure. Judged on that basis it is unusually clean — a single measurable indicator, a mechanistic chain checked link by link on real model internals, and interventions whose only cost is a short prefix or a top-$K$ mask.

Strengths

  • A single, cheap-to-compute indicator (query-token attention entropy) predicts parallel-encoding quality at Pearson R ≈ 0.95, giving serving systems a white-box health signal for KV reuse.
  • The mechanistic chain from per-piece sink tokens to small key norms to flattened softmax is measured, not merely asserted, and confirmed by intervention.
  • Both repairs are training-free, apply to frozen pre-trained models, and compose with recompute-based reuse systems like CacheBlend and EPIC.
  • The evaluation is fair and broad: four task families, three lengths, parallel degrees to 64, and replication on Mistral-7B-v0.3 and Qwen2-7B.

Limitations

  • The repairs narrow the gap but do not close it: at very high parallel degrees a substantial deficit versus full attention remains, and the authors say lightweight fine-tuning may still be needed.
  • The deepest causal step — why per-piece encoding produces small-norm sink states, given LayerNorm and MLP interactions — is left unresolved and deferred to future work.
  • An oracle control shows the value states carry irregularity too, so an entropy indicator built on key norms captures only part of the failure.
  • The analysis needs white-box access to attention internals, so it applies only to open-weight models; the selective-attention knobs are also task-dependent, with no single best setting.
In summary, attention entropy over query tokens is a reliable, inexpensive gauge of whether a full-attention model is coping with independently encoded context. When it is elevated, quality is failing; a shared sink prefix and top-K selective attention pull it back down and recover much — though not all — of the loss, without any fine-tuning. For agentic serving that reuses per-document or per-tool KV caches, the entropy signal marks the boundary between safe reuse and reuse that silently corrupts the answer.
