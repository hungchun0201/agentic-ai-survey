Energy Efficiency and Performance Trade-offs in LLM Inference

A controlled study that treats GPU core frequency as an independent variable, sweeping it across tasks and sequence lengths to map the joint energy–latency surface of LLM inference.

arXiv 2025 · 2501.08219 DVFS / Frequency Scaling LLM Inference Energy NVIDIA A100

Paul Joe Maliakel, Shashikant Ilager, Ivona Brandic

TU Wien (Austria) · University of Amsterdam (Netherlands)

1. Why Frequency Became a First-Class Variable

Large language model inference moved from a research curiosity to a continuously running production service within roughly two years. A single deployed assistant now answers millions of requests per day, and the marginal cost of each answer is dominated not by the model weights, which are fixed, but by the electrical energy the accelerator draws while it produces tokens. Operators that once optimized only for latency now report energy per request to finance and sustainability teams, because at fleet scale a ten-percent change in joules per token is a multi-megawatt change in datacenter draw. The question this paper raises is direct: for a given model and a given task, what core frequency should the GPU run at, and how much energy is wasted by leaving that choice to the default firmware governor?

1.1 A concrete failure of the default governor

Consider an operator running a BoolQ-style classification service that serves GPT-2 on a single A100 under a 50 ms per-request latency SLO. At a small per-request batch the paper measures each answer at 5.43 J and 0.03 s, comfortably inside the SLO; on this task GPT-2 sustains roughly 40 tokens per second, so the accelerator clears the deadline with substantial headroom and draws power it does not need to meet it. The natural first response is to reduce the SM clock, since dynamic power scales with frequency. The paper's frequency sweep shows that this response backfires by close to 2×: the same completed work that costs least in the 1005–1410 MHz band rises toward double its energy at the 210 MHz floor, because the lengthened runtime accumulates static leakage and idle-subsystem draw faster than the lower clock reduces dynamic power. The measured remedy runs in the opposite direction. Enlarging the batch lowers the per-request cost to 3.68 J and 0.02 s — a 32% energy reduction and a 33% latency reduction that still clears the 50 ms SLO — and pairing that batch with a deliberately chosen high clock, rather than the default governor, removes a further increment of energy. The difficulty is that the three levers — clock, batch, and model — interact, so an operator who tunes any one of them in isolation can settle on the worst region of the surface rather than the best.

1.2 The status-quo cost being paid today

The pain of leaving frequency untuned is not abstract. The paper measures that across its task suite the wrong frequency choice can roughly double total energy for the same completed work, and that the gap between the default governor and a tuned high-clock setting is on the order of fifteen percent of total energy. Model choice compounds this: GPT-2 consumes between fifty and eighty-five percent less energy than the larger models for the same task, while GPT-J-6B can peak at roughly seventy thousand megabytes of memory on the SQuADv2 question-answering task. An operator who selects model, batch size, and frequency independently, without measuring their interaction, is therefore exposed to a multiplicative energy penalty rather than an additive one.

1.3 The obvious fix and why it is insufficient

An obvious response is to simply minimize the clock, on the reasoning that dynamic power scales with frequency and voltage, so a slower clock must save energy. This approach fails because total energy is the product of average power and runtime, and lowering the clock lengthens runtime faster than it lowers power once the workload becomes latency-bound rather than power-bound. A second obvious response is to trust the vendor's default boost governor, which already adjusts the clock dynamically. This also proves insufficient: the governor optimizes for responsiveness under an unknown workload, not for energy under a known one, and the paper shows a measurable band of settings that the governor does not reach but that a workload-aware policy can. The remaining design space is therefore not whether to scale frequency but how to choose the operating point given the specific task, model, and sequence-length regime.

1.4 The fundamental obstacle: the energy surface is not separable

The structural reason a one-line policy cannot exist is that energy per request is a function of several interacting variables that do not separate. Energy depends jointly on the chosen frequency, on the task — whether the request is a short-output classification or a long-output generation — on the input and output sequence lengths, on batch size, and on which model is loaded. The roofline position of the kernel determines whether frequency even matters: a compute-bound kernel responds strongly to clock changes, whereas a memory-bandwidth-bound kernel barely responds because it spends most of its cycles waiting on memory rather than on arithmetic units. Since prefill and decode sit on opposite sides of this roofline, no single clock setting is simultaneously optimal for both phases of the same request. The optimization problem is therefore inherently a joint search over a non-separable surface, which is precisely what the paper sets out to measure rather than assume.

1.5 Position relative to prior work

Energy-aware computing has a long lineage, but the prior work that a knowledgeable reader expects to be addressed leaves specific gaps for LLM inference. Classical GPU DVFS studies — power models such as GPUWattch and the frequency-scaling characterizations of Mei et al. — established that the core clock trades latency for power on accelerators, yet they were built on HPC and graphics kernels and do not characterize the prefill–decode structure of autoregressive generation. Throughput-oriented serving systems, notably vLLM with its paged-attention KV management and Orca with continuous batching, raise tokens per second and per-request efficiency, but treat the GPU clock as a fixed property of the hardware rather than a tunable knob. LLM energy- and carbon-accounting work, such as LLMCarbon and the estimates of Luccioni et al., reports joules and emissions per query at the default operating point, leaving the frequency dimension unexplored. The present paper occupies the gap these leave open: it varies frequency deliberately as an independent variable, crosses it with the task and sequence-length dimensions, and reports the resulting joint energy–latency surface, including which phase of inference the clock actually governs.

2. What the Paper Establishes

Having argued that the energy surface is non-separable and that neither low-clock intuition nor the default governor suffices, the paper proceeds empirically. Its contribution is not a new serving system but a careful measurement study that pins down, for a fixed accelerator, how energy and latency respond to frequency once the task and sequence length are held as controlled factors. The headline results below are reported as measured on an NVIDIA A100 across six models and a task suite spanning text generation, summarization, and several question-answering styles — Boolean classification (BoolQ), commonsense reasoning (OpenBookQA), and span extraction (SQuADv2).

The four findings the page develops in detail are summarized in the cards below; each is grounded in a measured figure later in this analysis.

Phase-asymmetric clock sensitivity

Combining the paper's task-level frequency data with the roofline model implies that the compute-bound prefill phase responds strongly to frequency, while the memory-bandwidth-bound decode phase is largely frequency-insensitive. This asymmetry is the single most reusable consequence for downstream latency and energy models.

A non-monotone energy–clock curve

Total energy is high at very low clocks (runtime dominates), falls through the mid band, and reaches its minimum in the upper-clock region — the opposite of the intuition that slower always saves energy.

Task and length shape the surface

Energy, latency, peak memory, and throughput differ markedly across generation, question-answering, and summarization, and scale with input and output sequence length rather than being constant per model.

Batch size is a measured lever

Increasing batch size improves per-request energy and latency up to the point where memory pressure rises — for BoolQ, energy drops from 5.43 J to 3.68 J and latency from 0.03 s to 0.02 s.

Key insight: Because energy is the product of average power and runtime, and because frequency moves these two terms in opposite directions, the energy-minimizing clock is an interior or upper-band point that depends on how compute-bound the active kernel is. The same accelerator therefore has different optimal clocks for prefill and decode within a single request.

3. Measurement Design

The study is constructed as a controlled factorial experiment. The independent variables are the GPU core (SM) frequency, the task, the model, the input and output sequence lengths, and the batch size; the dependent variables are total energy, latency, throughput, and peak memory. Holding the accelerator and the software stack fixed isolates the effect of each input factor, which is the methodological discipline that lets the paper attribute energy changes to frequency rather than to confounds. 3.1 The energy identity that drives the whole study

Every result in the paper rests on a single relation: total energy is the time integral of instantaneous power over the runtime of the job. Because the firmware reports power and clock as telemetry samples, the measured energy for a configuration is the discrete sum of power samples multiplied by the sampling interval. The frequency enters this identity twice and with opposite sign, which is the mathematical origin of the non-monotone curve.

(1) $$E = \int_{0}^{T} P(t)\, dt \;\approx\; \Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{N} P_i$$

The runtime $T$ and the average power $\bar{P}$ both depend on the SM frequency $f$. To first order, dynamic power rises super-linearly with frequency because supply voltage must increase to sustain a higher clock, while runtime falls only for the fraction of the kernel that is actually compute-bound. Writing the energy as the product of average power and runtime makes the competition explicit.

(2) $$E(f) = \bar{P}(f)\cdot T(f), \qquad P_{\mathrm{dyn}}(f) \propto C\, V(f)^2 f, \qquad T(f) = T_{\mathrm{mem}} + \frac{W_{\mathrm{compute}}}{f}$$

Equation (2) captures the central mechanism. The term $T_{\mathrm{mem}}$ is the memory-bound portion of runtime that does not shrink when the clock rises, while $W_{\mathrm{compute}}/f$ is the compute-bound portion that does. When a kernel is dominated by $T_{\mathrm{mem}}$ — as decode is, because each step reads the full key–value cache and weights for a single token — raising $f$ increases $P_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ without meaningfully reducing $T$, so energy rises. When a kernel is dominated by $W_{\mathrm{compute}}/f$ — as prefill is, because it multiplies the full prompt against the weight matrices — raising $f$ shortens runtime enough to lower energy until voltage scaling overtakes the gain. The energy-minimizing frequency is therefore the point at which the marginal power increase equals the marginal runtime saving, and that point shifts with the compute-bound fraction of the workload.

4. How Task and Length Shape the Surface

Before turning to frequency, the paper establishes that the workload itself — the task and its sequence lengths — sets the stage on which frequency acts. A task is not a single number but a distribution of input and output lengths, and that distribution determines how much of each request is prefill compute versus decode memory traffic. The figures in this section show the average sequence length per task and the resulting energy, performance, memory, and throughput, all measured before any deliberate frequency scaling.

Figure 1 (from paper): Average sequence length per task. The spread across tasks confirms that a single 'LLM workload' assumption is unsafe: summarization carries long inputs, whereas classification-style question answering carries short ones.
Figure 2 (from paper): Input sequence length analysis. Energy and latency scale with input length because the prefill phase must process every input token before the first output token is produced.

The two figures above make the input-length dependence concrete. Because prefill processes the entire input in one compute-heavy pass, a task with long inputs spends a larger fraction of its runtime in the compute-bound regime, which is exactly the regime in which frequency will later prove to matter. A task with short inputs and long generated outputs spends most of its runtime in decode, where frequency will prove nearly irrelevant. The workload thus pre-determines how responsive a request will be to clock changes before any clock is touched.

5. The Frequency Sweep

This section presents the paper's central measurement: the joint behavior of runtime, average power, and total energy as the SM clock is swept from 210 MHz to 1410 MHz. The two figures below correspond to the two halves of the energy identity in Equation (1) — the first shows how runtime and power each respond to the clock, and the second shows the product of the two, which is total energy. The non-monotone shape of the energy curve is the result that overturns the slower-is-greener intuition.

Figure 8 (from paper): Time taken and average power versus SM clock speed. As the clock rises, runtime falls steeply for the compute-bound portion of the work while average power climbs super-linearly, because supply voltage must rise to sustain the higher clock. The two curves move in opposite directions, which is why their product has an interior structure rather than a monotone trend.

Figure 8 isolates the two competing terms. The falling runtime curve is the $W_{\mathrm{compute}}/f$ term of Equation (2): each additional megahertz removes a slice of compute-bound time, but the slices shrink as the clock approaches the boost ceiling because the memory-bound floor $T_{\mathrm{mem}}$ cannot be removed by a faster clock. The rising power curve is the $C V(f)^2 f$ term: power grows faster than linearly because voltage and frequency both increase. The energy-minimizing clock is the point where one more megahertz costs more in added power than it saves in reduced runtime.

Figure 9 (from paper): Total energy for each SM clock setting. Energy is highest at the lowest clock (about 400,000 J at 210 MHz, where prolonged runtime dominates), falls through the mid band (about 270,000 J at 405–810 MHz), and reaches its minimum in the upper band (about 180,000 J at 1005–1410 MHz). Manual tuning in the upper band uses less energy than the default governor (about 210,000 J).

Figure 9 is the product of the two curves in Figure 8 and is the paper's most consequential result. The minimum sits in the upper-clock band rather than at the lowest clock, which directly contradicts the assumption that a slower clock saves energy. The practical reading for an operator is that, for this compute-heavy workload, the right policy is to run near the boost ceiling but at a deliberately chosen point, not to throttle down and not to accept the default governor's choice, which sits about fifteen percent above the tuned optimum.

Consequence: The energy-optimal frequency is workload-dependent. A compute-bound workload such as the one in Figure 9 minimizes energy near the boost ceiling, whereas a memory-bound workload would minimize energy lower, because its runtime barely shrinks as the clock rises. This is the bridge from a single-job DVFS study to a per-request, phase-aware serving policy.

6. Phase-Asymmetric Clock Sensitivity

The paper reports frequency sensitivity at the task, model, and sequence-length level rather than separately for the prefill and decode phases. Combining those measurements with the roofline model yields the most reusable consequence for serving: the two phases of autoregressive inference respond to the clock in opposite ways. Prefill is a compute-bound matrix-multiplication phase: it consumes the entire prompt at once, saturating the arithmetic units, so its runtime falls when the clock rises. Decode is a memory-bandwidth-bound phase: each step produces one token and is dominated by reading the key–value cache and weights from device memory, so its runtime barely changes when the clock rises. The diagram below places both phases on the roofline to explain why frequency governs one and not the other.

6.1 Why the asymmetry follows from the roofline

The roofline model explains the asymmetry without appeal to any LLM-specific detail. A kernel's achievable performance is the lesser of its arithmetic-throughput ceiling, which scales with the clock, and its memory-bandwidth ceiling, which does not. Prefill has high arithmetic intensity — many floating-point operations per byte read — so it sits under the compute ceiling, and lifting that ceiling by raising the clock lifts its performance. Decode has low arithmetic intensity — a single token reuses each loaded weight only once — so it sits under the bandwidth ceiling, which a higher clock leaves untouched. The latency a clock can buy is therefore proportional to the compute-bound fraction of the kernel, which is the quantity the next section folds into a serving predictor.

(3) $$\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{prefill}}}{\partial f} \ll 0, \qquad \frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{decode}}}{\partial f} \approx 0$$

Equation (3) states the asymmetry compactly: prefill runtime has a strongly negative sensitivity to frequency, while decode runtime has a sensitivity near zero. This single pair of partial derivatives is the property our own predictor reuses, as the following section explains.

7. Relevance to Our Per-Step Latency Predictor

Our own work builds a per-step vLLM serving latency predictor that folds the GPU SM clock in as a hidden, involuntary, input-token-driven term: rather than setting the clock, we treat it as a quantity the workload induces on itself, observing the clock fall from boost toward throttle as the input-token count M rises and as the die heats, then learning latency from that induced clock. This paper is directly relevant because it supplies the missing half of that story from the controlled, frequency-as-independent-variable direction. Where we observe the clock moving on its own as a function of load and report its effect on latency, Maliakel and colleagues deliberately set the clock and report which phase of inference its effect lands on. The two studies meet at the same conclusion from opposite experimental designs. (The quantitative droop and correlation figures of our predictor are reported and verified in our companion measurement paper, not on this analysis page.)

7.1 What this paper confirms for us

The phase-asymmetric clock sensitivity established in Section 6 corroborates exactly why our clock term must be load-dependent rather than a constant offset. Because prefill is frequency-sensitive and decode is largely frequency-insensitive, a clock change matters precisely when a step is compute-bound — high input-token count M, prefill, or large fused prefill chunks — and matters little when a step is a light decode. This is the structural justification for modeling the clock as a function of M rather than as a fixed per-device constant, and it explains the regime split our predictor relies on.

7.2 Why decode-serving stays at boost

The same asymmetry explains an observation in our measurements that would otherwise look like an exception: under light decode-serving the SXM cards stay pinned at the boost clock. If decode draws little arithmetic load, the card does not heat enough to trigger the involuntary thermal droop, and the firmware keeps the clock high. The clock term is therefore quiescent in the decode-dominated regime and becomes active only under the compute-bound, high-M, prefill-heavy conditions this paper shows are frequency-sensitive. The two papers agree that the clock matters exactly where the work is compute-bound, which is the regime our term is designed to capture.

7.3 How our contribution differs

The novelty of our work is partial and we state the boundary precisely. The droop mechanism — that a hot GPU lowers its clock — and the learnability of latency from clock are both established, and this paper is one of the works that establishes the second. What this paper does not do is model the clock as an involuntary function of the input-token count inside a general serving latency predictor. Maliakel and colleagues set the clock as an external control and measure the response; they do not predict the clock that a load will involuntarily induce, nor fold that induced clock into a per-step latency model. Our gap is exactly that: treating the droop as $f(M)$ — a hidden term the workload imposes on itself — and embedding it in a predictor that a scheduler can query before the step runs.

8. Strengths and Limitations

As a measurement study the paper is valuable precisely because it controls the frequency variable that most serving work leaves implicit, but its scope also bounds how far its numbers transfer. The two columns below separate what the study demonstrates convincingly from what a reader should not over-extend.

Strengths

  • Treats frequency as a controlled independent variable, isolating its effect from model and task confounds.
  • Reports the full energy–latency surface rather than a single operating point, exposing the non-monotone energy curve.
  • Spans six models and three task families, so the prefill/decode asymmetry is not a single-architecture artifact.

Limitations

  • Measured on a single SKU (NVIDIA A100); the absolute joule and megahertz values do not transfer directly to H100, H200, or Blackwell.
  • The clock is set externally; the study does not model the involuntary, load-induced droop that a serving workload imposes on itself.
  • No online controller is proposed; the work characterizes the surface but stops short of a per-request, phase-aware policy.

The most important limitation for our purposes is the second one, because it is the precise seam our work fills. This paper proves that the clock governs the compute-bound phase; our predictor takes the next step of predicting, rather than setting, the clock that a given input-token count will involuntarily produce, and of using that prediction inside a per-step latency model that a scheduler can consult ahead of execution.
