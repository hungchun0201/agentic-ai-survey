Shift Parallelism

A serving runtime that switches between tensor parallelism and Ulysses sequence parallelism at iteration granularity, using the KV-cache invariance between the two layouts to deliver low latency at low traffic and high throughput under bursts.

Mert Hidayetoglu, Aurick Qiao, Michael Wyatt, Jeff Rasley, Yuxiong He, Samyam Rajbhandari

1. Overview & Motivation

A modern inference fleet rarely serves one kind of request. The same set of GPUs handles interactive chat that must return its first token within a few hundred milliseconds, and at other moments absorbs large batch jobs whose only concern is aggregate token throughput. Shift Parallelism addresses the long-standing observation that the parallelism strategy which minimizes per-request latency is not the one that maximizes throughput, and that production traffic moves between these two regimes faster than an operator can re-shard a model. The section below establishes why this matters in 2025-era deployments, walks through a concrete trace where the two standard strategies fail, and isolates the structural reason no existing system can switch between them on the fly.

Figure 1 (from paper): Response speed (input tokens per TTFT), generation rate (1/TPOT), and throughput (tokens/sec). Shift Parallelism obtains higher throughput than TP under high traffic, and lower latency than both TP and DP under low traffic, occupying the corner of the trade-off space that neither single strategy reaches.

1.1 Why Now: One Fleet, Two Conflicting SLOs

The serving workload of 2025 differs in character from the steady, single-objective traffic for which tensor parallelism was tuned. Three changes coincide. First, agentic applications issue long chains of dependent model calls — a planner, several tool-invocation steps, and a synthesizer — each of which is on the critical path of a single user-visible action, so per-request response time has become a first-class objective rather than a secondary one. Second, the same deployment is expected to fold in batch jobs such as offline summarization and synthetic-data generation, whose service-level objective is tokens per second and not first-token latency. Third, arrivals are bursty: a fleet may sit at a low steady stream of requests for minutes and then receive a short, intense burst, so the instantaneous batch size swings across two orders of magnitude within the same minute. The consequence is that one fixed parallelism configuration is being asked to satisfy two service-level objectives that pull in opposite directions, on traffic whose mix changes faster than the configuration can.

1.2 A Concrete Bad Scenario: A Bursty Trace on Llama-70B

Consider eight H200 GPUs serving Llama-70B in FP8 against the bursty arrival pattern the paper replays: a steady low-frequency stream of requests punctuated by occasional high-frequency bursts. Suppose the operator has pinned the deployment to tensor parallelism across all eight GPUs, the configuration that gives the fastest response when only one request is in flight. During a burst, dozens of requests arrive within a window of a few seconds and queue behind one another while the all-reduce-bound forward step processes a large fused batch; the median time-to-first-token measured over the trace reaches 3,930 ms, far outside any interactive target. Faced with that, the operator instead pins the deployment to data parallelism — eight independent replicas, each holding the full model. The burst is now absorbed across replicas and throughput recovers, but a single request can no longer be accelerated by more than one GPU, so even in the quiet intervals the median time-to-first-token sits at 1,355 ms because each replica must prefill the entire prompt alone. Neither pinned configuration meets an interactive first-token target across the whole trace; one fails during bursts and the other fails during the quiet stretches.

Figure 2 (from paper): The bursty workload used in the trace replay: a low-frequency steady stream of requests with occasional high-frequency bursts. The latency-versus-throughput tension is most acute precisely on such traffic, because the optimal parallelism differs between the quiet stretches and the bursts.

1.3 The Quantified Baseline Pain

The pain that the two pinned configurations impose can be read directly from the trace numbers, before any of Shift Parallelism's own gains are introduced. Tensor parallelism pays for its low single-request latency with communication: every transformer layer performs an all-reduce whose volume grows with the processed sequence length, so as the burst fuses many requests into one large batch the communication-to-compute ratio rises and combined throughput falls; the peak throughput TP sustains on the production-style benchmark is 51,162 tokens per second, roughly two-thirds of what an embarrassingly parallel data-parallel deployment reaches. Data parallelism pays for its high throughput with response time: because no single request is split across GPUs, the median time-to-first-token under the bursty trace is 1,355 ms even outside the bursts. Tensor parallelism, by contrast, lets the queue build during bursts and reaches a median time-to-first-token of 3,930 ms. A reader comparing only these two status-quo numbers can already see that no single pinned strategy clears an interactive target across the trace, and that the loss is large — on the order of seconds of first-token latency, or a third of peak throughput, depending on which objective the operator chooses to sacrifice.

1.4 Strawman: Statically Pick TP or DP per Deployment

The obvious response is to size each deployment for its dominant workload: run latency-sensitive endpoints on tensor parallelism and throughput-oriented endpoints on data parallelism, choosing once at provisioning time. This fails for the bursty single-endpoint case because the dominant workload is not stable within the lifetime of a deployment. The same endpoint that is quiet enough for tensor parallelism to be the right choice for several minutes will, within the same window, receive a burst for which data parallelism is the right choice, and the mix can invert again minutes later. Re-provisioning is not a per-second operation: switching a live deployment from tensor parallelism to data parallelism would require re-sharding the model weights and, critically, relaying out the accumulated KV cache of every in-flight request, which cannot be done without draining the deployment first. By the time a static re-provision completes, the burst that motivated it has passed. The static choice therefore reduces to committing in advance to violating one of the two objectives whenever the workload shifts.

1.5 The Fundamental Obstacle: KV-Cache Layout Differs Across Parallelisms

The reason live switching is normally impossible is structural rather than a matter of engineering effort. Tensor parallelism and data parallelism place the attention key-value cache differently in GPU memory: under tensor parallelism the attention heads are partitioned across GPUs, so each GPU stores the KV cache only for the heads it owns, whereas under data parallelism each replica stores the complete KV cache for the requests it alone serves. Switching from one to the other mid-stream would require physically rearranging every byte of accumulated KV cache to match the destination layout, an operation that scales with the total cached context and cannot be hidden behind a forward step. The central observation of Shift Parallelism is that this obstacle disappears for one particular pairing. Ulysses-style sequence parallelism, which partitions the token sequence rather than the attention heads, leaves the KV cache in the same memory layout as tensor parallelism: the cache produced under one is byte-for-byte usable under the other. Because the layout is invariant between tensor parallelism and sequence parallelism, the runtime can switch between them between iterations without touching the cache, and the design choices in the rest of the paper follow from preserving that invariance.

Shift Parallelism sits between three lines of work an informed reader will expect to see addressed. The result is a single configuration that follows the latency-optimal strategy in quiet stretches and the throughput-optimal strategy under bursts.

2. Architecture: The Shift Runtime and KV-Cache Invariance

At the system level Shift Parallelism is a single serving runtime that holds the model in a fixed group of GPUs and, before each forward step, decides whether that step will execute under a tensor-parallel layout or a sequence-parallel layout. The decision depends only on the number of tokens batched into the step, and the switch is free because the only state that persists across steps — the KV cache — has the same shape and placement in both layouts. This section first contrasts the two data layouts so the invariance is visible, then shows how the runtime turns the per-iteration batch size into a layout choice.

2.1 Two Layouts, One KV Cache

The diagram below contrasts how the two layouts distribute work and where each places the KV cache, on a two-GPU example. Tensor parallelism splits the attention heads across GPUs; sequence parallelism splits the token sequence across GPUs and gathers heads through all-to-all communication. The point of placing them side by side is that the bottom row — the KV cache each GPU ends up holding — is identical in both, which is the property the runtime depends on.

The two top rows differ entirely: tensor parallelism gives each GPU a slice of every token but only some heads, while sequence parallelism gives each GPU every head but only some tokens, performing an all-to-all so that during the attention computation each GPU temporarily holds all tokens for the subset of heads it is responsible for. After that all-to-all, however, each GPU is computing and storing exactly the heads it would have owned under tensor parallelism, so the KV cache it writes lands in the same place. The bottom rows are therefore identical, and a cache written by one layout is read without modification by the other.

2.2 The Per-Iteration Decision

The runtime treats the number of batched tokens in an iteration as the single control signal. When few tokens are present — the quiet stretches and the latency-critical decode steps of an interactive request — it selects tensor parallelism, which minimizes the time per output token. When many tokens are present — a burst, or a large prefill that can be split across the sequence dimension — it selects sequence parallelism, which avoids the per-layer all-reduce and so sustains throughput closer to data parallelism. The dial below places the two regimes on a single axis governed by a token threshold.

Figure 5 (from paper): Although sequence parallelism and tensor parallelism are different parallelisms, Shift Parallelism uses the KV-cache invariance between them to switch across them swiftly. The cache produced in one mode is reused in the other without rearrangement; only the query, key, and value weight shardings change with the mode.
Architectural insight: Because the switch is keyed on batched token count and the KV cache never moves, the runtime can change layout between any two consecutive iterations at no cost beyond re-selecting which slices of the query weights each GPU uses. The model weights are resident in both shardings, so there is no reload; the decision is therefore a per-step branch rather than a re-provisioning event, which is what makes it usable against second-scale traffic shifts.

2.3 The Per-Step Decision Pipeline

It is worth tracing the sequence of events inside one forward step, because that is where the design's simplicity becomes apparent. The pipeline below follows a single iteration from the moment the scheduler hands a batch to the runtime through to the point where the KV cache is written. The branch in the middle is the only place the two layouts diverge; everything above and below it is shared, which is why the cost of being in either mode is the same except for the collective that attention performs.

Both branches converge here: each GPU writes the heads it owns to the same cache addresses, so the next iteration may choose the other layout freely.

The reason this pipeline can change its middle branch every iteration without any settling period is that step 4 is layout-independent. Once attention has computed, each GPU holds exactly the heads its head-to-GPU map assigns it under either layout, so the write target is the same address range in both cases. A run therefore alternates freely between the two branches as the batched token count rises and falls with the traffic, and the only per-step overhead the switch adds is the constant-time selection of which weight shardings to use.

3. Core Mechanisms

The design rests on four mechanisms that build on one another. Sequence parallelism is first adapted from training to inference, then the invariance that makes switching free is established formally, then the per-iteration switching policy and its load balancing are described, and finally the composition with tensor parallelism is given so the scheme works on models with grouped-query attention. Each subsection states the plain idea before showing the formulas or pseudocode that make it precise.

3.1 Ulysses-Style Sequence Parallelism for Inference

Ulysses sequence parallelism partitions the token sequence across GPUs and, because every attention head needs the full sequence, performs an all-to-all immediately before the attention computation to re-gather complete sequences for a subset of heads on each GPU, then a second all-to-all afterward to restore the sequence partition. The reason this matters for throughput is that the volume of an all-to-all is set by the model's hidden dimension and the sequence length, and does not grow with the parallelism degree the way a tensor-parallel all-reduce does. The figure below shows the vanilla transformer block whose attention layer is the operator being re-partitioned.

Figure 4 (from paper): The vanilla transformer architecture and its attention mechanism. The attention layer is the only operator whose per-head computation requires the full token sequence, which is why sequence parallelism needs an all-to-all around it while the feed-forward layers stay sequence-partitioned.

The latency-versus-throughput contrast between the two layouts can be made quantitative by comparing their communication volumes. Under row-parallel tensor parallelism, every transformer layer issues an all-reduce whose cost grows with the processed sequence length $n$, so the communication a step performs is proportional to the number of layers times that sequence-dependent volume; as the parallelism degree rises the same data must be reduced across more participants and the communication-to-compute ratio increases. The per-step all-reduce volume can be written as

(1) $$V_{\text{TP}} \;=\; 2L \cdot n \cdot d_{\text{model}} \cdot \frac{p-1}{p}, \qquad p = \text{TP degree}$$

where $L$ is the number of transformer layers, $n$ the sequence length, $d_{\text{model}}$ the hidden dimension, and the factor $\frac{p-1}{p}$ is the standard ring all-reduce coefficient. The volume scales with $n$, so a step that fuses a large burst into one batch pays an all-reduce cost that rises with the fused token count. Sequence parallelism instead exchanges activations through two all-to-all operations per layer whose combined volume is

(2) $$V_{\text{SP}} \;=\; 2L \cdot n \cdot d_{\text{model}} \cdot \frac{p-1}{p}, \qquad \text{partitioned, not reduced}$$

The two volumes share the same leading form, but the all-to-all of sequence parallelism moves each activation slice exactly once to the GPU that needs it rather than reducing a full-width tensor across all participants, so on the NVSwitch fabric used in the evaluation it sustains throughput far closer to data parallelism while keeping the model split across GPUs. The practical effect is that sequence parallelism removes the per-layer all-reduce that makes tensor parallelism throughput-bound under bursts, which is the property the runtime selects for when it shifts into sequence-parallel mode for large batches.

3.2 KV-Cache Invariance Between TP and SP

The invariance that makes switching free can be stated precisely in terms of which heads each GPU ends up owning. Let a deployment use $g$ GPUs. Tensor parallelism assigns head $h$ to GPU $\lfloor h / (H/g) \rfloor$, where $H$ is the total number of attention heads, so the assignment of a head's KV cache to a GPU is fixed by the head index alone. Sequence parallelism, after its pre-attention all-to-all, hands the same head $h$ to the same GPU for the duration of the attention computation, and writes that head's KV entries to the same GPU. Formally, the cache-to-GPU map is

(3) $$\text{GPU}_{\text{TP}}(h) \;=\; \text{GPU}_{\text{SP}}(h) \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{h}{H/g} \right\rfloor \quad \text{for every head } h$$

Because the map is the same function of the head index in both layouts, the physical placement of every cached key and value is identical, and the difference between the two layouts is confined to how the query, key, and value projection weights are sharded before the attention call. A deployment that switches mode therefore only re-selects weight shardings; the cache that was written in one mode is read in the other from the same address. The figure below illustrates the invariance across six heads for a base configuration and its shifted counterpart, showing that the shifted configuration shards the query weights according to the sequence-parallel and tensor-parallel degrees of the base configuration while leaving the cache untouched.

Figure 7 (from paper): KV-cache invariance of six heads across the base and shift configurations. The shift configuration shards the query weights according to the SP and TP degrees of the base configuration, so the cached keys and values keep the same GPU placement and no rearrangement is required at the switch.

3.3 Dynamic TP↔SP Switching and Load Balancing

The switching policy itself is a threshold rule on the number of tokens batched into an iteration, applied before the forward pass is dispatched. The pseudocode below expresses the rule at the level of the forward pass: the runtime reads the batched token count, compares it to a threshold, and dispatches the appropriate layout, treating tensor parallelism as the degenerate case in which the sequence-parallel degree is one.

# Shift Parallelism forward pass — illustrative pseudocode (Algorithm 3 in paper)
def shift_forward(batch, threshold, g):
    n = batch.num_tokens                # tokens batched into this iteration
    if n > threshold:                     # burst / large prefill: favor throughput
        return forward(batch, sp=g, tp=1)   # sequence-parallel across all g GPUs
    else:                                # quiet / latency-critical decode: favor latency
        return forward(batch, sp=1, tp=g)   # tensor-parallel across all g GPUs

The deep-dive subtlety is load balancing at small batch sizes, which is where a naive sequence partition fails. When the batch size is comparable to the sequence-parallel degree, the tokens cannot be divided evenly across GPUs, so some GPUs receive a full slice and others receive none, leaving part of the fabric idle while the attention computation waits on the busiest GPU. The runtime addresses this by padding each batch up to the next multiple of the sequence-parallel degree before partitioning, so every GPU receives an equal slice and the all-to-all has uniform participants. The padding adds a small amount of wasted compute on the padded positions, but it removes the load imbalance that would otherwise make small-batch sequence parallelism slower than its even-batch behavior would predict.

3.4 Combined SP×TP for Grouped-Query Attention

Modern serving models use grouped-query attention, in which the number of key-value heads is far smaller than the number of query heads — Llama-70B has 64 query heads but only 8 key-value heads. This complicates a pure sequence partition because, when the sequence-parallel degree exceeds the number of key-value heads, there are not enough distinct key-value heads to give each GPU one, and the head-to-GPU map of the previous subsection can no longer be one-to-one. The runtime resolves this by replicating the key-value cache: GPUs within a grouped-query group hold a copy of the same key-value head, and the replication is performed with multi-step neighborhood collectives so that the copies are produced without a global broadcast.

Figure 6 (from paper): KV-cache replication in a model with two KV heads on four GPUs. The grouped-query groups (0, 1) and (2, 3) each replicate the KV cache through neighborhood collectives, so the cache placement stays consistent with the head-to-GPU map even when the sequence-parallel degree exceeds the number of KV heads.

The composition also governs how the two parallelisms stack when a model is too large to replicate across every GPU. For a throughput-oriented configuration the runtime uses tensor parallelism only to the extent that memory requires — enough to make each partition fit in GPU memory with room for the KV cache — and applies sequence parallelism across the remaining GPUs. Writing the sequence-parallel degree and the tensor-parallel degree as a pair, the combined forward pass is selected as

(4) $$(\text{SP}, \text{TP}) = \begin{cases} (g / t_{\min},\; t_{\min}) & \text{if } n > \text{threshold} \\ (1,\; g) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where $t_{\min}$ is the smallest tensor-parallel degree at which a model partition fits in GPU memory, $g$ is the total GPU count, and $n$ is the batched token count. In the throughput regime the product of the two degrees still equals $g$, so all GPUs remain in use; tensor parallelism handles only the memory-driven minimum and sequence parallelism handles the rest. This is what lets the same eight-GPU deployment behave as full tensor parallelism for a single decode step and as a memory-minimal tensor-parallel core wrapped in sequence parallelism for a burst, while the KV-cache invariance of the previous subsection guarantees the transition between the two costs nothing on the cache.

4.3 Context Length and the Time Breakdown

The advantage is not uniform across context lengths, and the paper is explicit about where it narrows. As the input sequence grows, the attention computation comes to dominate the forward step and the communication savings of sequence parallelism become a smaller fraction of the total, so peak throughput falls with large context sizes for all three strategies. The end-to-end cost breakdown makes the regime boundary visible: at short sequence lengths the serving-runtime overhead is the dominant cost, whereas at long sequence lengths the attention time dominates, and the shifting policy is most valuable in the former regime where the communication structure, not the raw attention compute, sets the step time.

Caveats: The reported gains are measured on multi-GPU H200-class hardware with a high-bandwidth NVSwitch fabric; the communication advantage of the all-to-all over the all-reduce depends on that fabric, and the margins narrow on slower interconnects. The whole design also assumes the model and runtime preserve KV-cache compatibility across the switch — a model or kernel that lays the cache out differently under the two modes would not be a candidate. Finally, single-GPU kernel efficiency, host-side overhead, and arbitrary network topologies are outside the evaluated regime, and the throughput advantage shrinks at long context lengths where attention compute rather than communication sets the step time.

# Illustrative — shape of the per-step mode selection in a vLLM plugin runner
# Not a verbatim copy of ArcticInference source.
class ShiftModelRunner(ModelRunnerBase):
    def execute_model(self, batch):
        n = batch.num_scheduled_tokens
        mode = "sp" if n > self.shift_threshold else "tp"
        self.attn.set_mode(mode)        # re-select Q/K/V weight shardings; cache untouched
        return self.model.forward(batch)   # KV cache read/written at the same addresses

The single point worth emphasizing in this sketch is that set_mode changes only which slices of the query, key, and value weights are active and which collective is issued around attention; it does not touch the KV-cache buffers, which is the implementation counterpart of the invariance argued in Section 3.2. Anyone reading the actual source should confirm the cache buffers are allocated once at startup and reused across modes rather than reallocated per switch, since that reuse is what the invariance buys at runtime.

5.2 Strengths

The strengths below follow from the design choices rather than from tuning, and each is stated with the condition under which it holds.

  • The KV-cache invariance is a clean structural observation rather than a tuned heuristic, so the switch carries no cache-movement cost regardless of how much context has accumulated.
  • The policy reduces to a single threshold on batched token count, which is cheap to evaluate per step and exposes one interpretable knob to operators.
  • Releasing the work as a vLLM plugin lowers the adoption barrier, since existing vLLM deployments keep their scheduler and KV-cache management.

5.3 Limitations

The limitations mark where the result is conditional, naming the assumption or hardware regime on which the reported numbers depend.

  • The communication advantage of the all-to-all over the all-reduce is fabric-dependent; the reported margins assume a 900 GB/s NVSwitch and would narrow on lower-bandwidth interconnects.
  • A single token-count threshold does not capture every workload feature; mixed prefill and decode within one step, or skewed sequence lengths, may call for a richer signal than batched token count alone.
  • The throughput advantage narrows at long context lengths, where attention compute rather than communication sets the step time.

7. Runtime Verification

Shift Parallelism ships inside Snowflake's ArcticInference, an open-source plugin that monkey-patches vLLM. To check that this runtime behaves as described on real hardware, the page is paired with a separate cross-hardware benchmark of ArcticInference, hosted on this site and linked below. The study is deliberately scoped: it isolates the runtime's single-GPU optimizations and treats the multi-GPU Shift Parallelism and Ulysses path — the contribution of this paper — as out of scope, so it validates the system that Shift Parallelism is built on rather than the multi-GPU shift itself.

Full report: Arctic Inference on a Single GPU — Cross-Hardware Benchmark →

The benchmark covers five GPUs spanning four generations — H100, H200, A100-40GB, L40S, and RTX 5090 — serving Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on vLLM 0.14.1 with arctic_inference 0.1.3 built from source. Two optimizations are isolated against a same-GPU vanilla-vLLM baseline: SwiftKV, which reduces prefill compute by reusing key-value projections across layers, and the Arctic Speculator paired with Suffix Decoding, which reduces the number of sequential decode steps. The speculative configuration shares the baseline's base weights, so under greedy decoding its output is identical and the measured speedup is a clean comparison.

7.1 What the benchmark shows

The speculative-decoding pipeline delivers a single-stream speedup of roughly 1.8 times on the A100-40GB and roughly 2.1 times on the L40S over vanilla vLLM on the same card, output-identical under greedy decoding; because the speculative runs disable CUDA graphs through enforce_eager, these figures are conservative. SwiftKV is approximately neutral on this fixed decode workload, near 0.99 times, which is the expected outcome rather than a negative one: SwiftKV reduces prefill compute, while the benchmark deliberately stresses the decode phase, so the optimization has little of its target work to remove. 7.2 How this relates to the paper's claim

The benchmark establishes that the ArcticInference runtime is sound and that its single-GPU acceleration is real and portable across four GPU generations, which is the substrate Shift Parallelism extends. It does not, on its own, confirm the paper's multi-GPU latency-and-throughput result, because the tensor-to-sequence-parallel switching and the KV-cache invariance of Section 4 require a multi-GPU run that this report marks as out of scope. The two are therefore best read together: the paper's multi-GPU measurements for the shift mechanism, and this study for the single-GPU foundation on which that mechanism stands.
