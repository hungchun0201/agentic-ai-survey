Predicting LLM Inference Latency

A Roofline-Driven ML Method — calibrating an analytical hardware ceiling with a learned residual regressor

NeurIPS 2024 ML for Systems Workshop Roofline + Regression vLLM & Triton A100 · OpenShift

Saki Imai, Rina Nakazawa, Marcelo Amaral, Sunyanan Choochotkaew, Tatsuhiro Chiba

Northeastern University · IBM Research

1. Why Predict Inference Latency at All?

A cluster operator who serves large language models must decide, before any request lands, how many GPUs to provision for a given model, which model to place on which accelerator, and what concurrency limit to admit. Each of these decisions presupposes an answer to a single question: how long will one inference take under this configuration? The historical way to obtain that answer was to run the model under every candidate configuration and record the measured latency. This paper argues that the historical way no longer scales, and proposes a predictor that fuses an analytical hardware ceiling with a learned correction so that the answer can be computed rather than measured.

1.1 Why now: the configuration space has outgrown brute-force measurement

The pace of model release changed the economics of profiling. Hugging Face crossed one million hosted models in 2024 and adds on the order of tens of thousands of new checkpoints per month, and each base model spawns frequent algorithmic revisions, so any latency table built by exhaustive measurement is stale within weeks. The number of cells that must be characterised is itself combinatorial: take the very grid this paper measures and read off its size, where 4 models × 2 serving frameworks × 3 concurrency levels (8, 16, 32) × 3 generation lengths (32, 64, 128) is already 72 cells, and each cell is a five-minute load test repeated three times, which is 18 GPU-hours of A100 time for one snapshot of four sub-3B models on a single accelerator. Widen that to the operational reality — add GPU type, batch size, and prompt length, and scale model count from 4 to the hundreds an operator actually fields — and the cross-product runs to millions of cells, each consuming the scarcest and most expensive resource in the datacentre at roughly two to three US dollars per A100-hour. The paper frames this as the core driver: the brute-force approach of running and learning the optimal configuration has become impractical, which creates the necessity for a predictive performance model that returns an answer in milliseconds of CPU arithmetic without occupying an accelerator at all.

1.2 A concrete failure of the analytical-only approach

Make the failure concrete with one trace from this paper's own grid. A capacity planner — call her the on-call SRE for a chat product — must admit facebook/opt-1.3b on vLLM under a per-request service-level objective of, say, 500 ms end-to-end for the standard probe “What is artificial intelligence?” generating 128 tokens at a concurrency of 32 users on one A100. She declines to run the load test and instead reads the textbook Roofline ceiling, which assumes the GPU holds peak FLOP/s and peak HBM bandwidth for every one of the 128 decode steps and reports a comfortably-under-SLO figure. The served reality is the rose marker in this paper's data: the regression-only baseline at this operating point lands at a coefficient of determination of just 0.800 with a mean squared error of 24282 ms² against the measured latencies — meaning the typical mispredict is on the order of √24282 ≈ 156 ms, enough to convert a 480 ms prediction into a 636 ms reality and silently breach the 500 ms SLO. The paper attributes the gap to the Roofline model lacking critical runtime information and therefore producing performance overestimation: the analytical ceiling describes a machine that never contends for memory, never queues a kernel launch, and never deviates from its rated clock, whereas the served machine does all three. The SRE who trusted the ceiling under-provisions, admits all 32 users, and misses the target on a request the model promised would be fast — which is exactly the cell where folding the analytical estimate back in as a feature later lifts the coefficient of determination from 0.800 to 0.974 and cuts the mean squared error by 87% to 3163 ms².

1.3 The baseline pain: how far apart the two worlds are

The magnitude of the gap is what makes the problem worth a paper rather than a footnote. When the analytical Roofline estimate is folded in as a feature and the regressor is retrained, the coefficient of determination for opt-1.3b on the vLLM server rises by seventeen points, and the mean squared error falls by eighty-seven percent relative to the same regressor trained without that feature. A latency model whose error can be cut by a factor of roughly eight once the analytical ceiling is reconciled with reality is, by construction, a model that was missing most of the physics beforehand. Across the four studied models the inclusion of the analytical estimate raised the average coefficient of determination by 12.93 percent and reduced the mean squared error by 80.08 percent on vLLM. The residual that the regressor must absorb is therefore not a rounding error; it is the dominant component of the prediction.

1.4 Strawman: why not measure a small sample and interpolate?

An obvious response is to abandon the analytical model entirely and instead measure a handful of configurations per model, then fit a pure regression over the operational parameters. The paper evaluates exactly this strawman as its in-domain baseline, training Random Forest, XGBoost, and Linear Regression on nine configurations per model that vary concurrency and generation length. The pure regressor performs adequately when it is asked only to interpolate within a single model it has already seen, reaching a coefficient of determination above 0.8 for every model and server. It fails in the two situations that matter operationally. When asked to generalise to a model never seen in training, the out-of-domain regressor degrades sharply on the smallest model, and even within a single model the pure regressor cannot explain the systematic offset between configurations because it has no representation of where the hardware ceiling lies. Interpolation cannot recover a quantity that is never measured; it can only smooth between the points it was given.

1.5 The fundamental obstacle: the analytical ceiling and the measured floor disagree by construction

The structural reason a naive combination cannot work is that the analytical Roofline estimate and the served latency answer two different questions. The Roofline estimate answers what the hardware could do if it were dedicated, contention-free, and clocked at its rated frequency; the served latency answers what the hardware did under a multi-tenant cloud scheduler that shares memory bandwidth, queues kernel launches, and throttles the clock under thermal and power constraints. The paper names this residual the variability introduced by cloud infrastructure and dynamic workloads. Because that variability is a runtime property that no static model can read off a configuration file, it cannot be added to the analytical estimate as a closed-form correction; it has to be learned from historical execution data. The design is therefore forced into a hybrid: the analytical model supplies the shape of the latency surface, and a regressor trained on real executions supplies the offset between that shape and the machine.

The residual is the paper, and it is also the opening for ours. The authors observe that the analytical ceiling over-predicts performance, and they absorb the gap with a learned regressor whose explanatory variable they describe only as cloud variability. Section 8 returns to this: one large, structured, input-token-driven component of that residual is the GPU streaming-multiprocessor clock falling under sustained load. This paper sees the residual clearly and never names its mechanism.

2. Position Relative to Prior Work

The paper sits at the confluence of two research lines that had previously been pursued in isolation: analytical performance modelling rooted in the Roofline tradition, and data-driven latency prediction for serving. Placing it against the immediate prior work clarifies what each line left unsolved and why a hybrid was the natural next step. The most directly comparable systems are summarised below; the paper aligns most closely with the simulation-and-roofline line and departs from it by grounding the analytical estimate in real execution data.

Prior work Approach What it left on the table
Williams et al., Roofline (2009) The original analytical ceiling relating compute throughput to memory bandwidth via arithmetic intensity. Built for multicore CPUs; assumes a dedicated, contention-free machine and says nothing about cloud runtime overhead.
LLM-Viewer / Inference-Unveiled (Yuan et al.) Applies the Roofline model to LLM layers to estimate inference latency from prompt length, batch size, and generation length. Acknowledges that the analytical estimate over-predicts under cloud variability, but offers no calibration against measured serving latency.
Vidur (Agrawal et al., 2024) A large-scale simulation framework that also produces high-fidelity latency predictions for LLM inference. Relies on extensive simulation rather than a closed-form ceiling reconciled with real-time execution data; the paper positions itself as the lighter-weight hybrid.
Length-predictor line (Zheng; Qiu; Cheng et al.) Predict output sequence length to inform scheduling, using response-length perception or proxy models. Predict a workload property (length), not the wall-clock latency of a step; orthogonal input to a latency model rather than a latency model.
Cluster-management systems (DynamoLLM; Melange) Optimise scheduling, energy efficiency, and GPU-heterogeneity placement for serving clusters. Consume a latency estimate as an input but do not provide a unified, interpretable prediction framework for it.

The contribution is therefore not a new analytical model and not a new regressor, but the reconciliation of the two: taking the over-predicting Roofline ceiling that LLM-Viewer exposes, and calibrating it against measured serving latency with a regressor, so that the resulting estimate is both interpretable through its analytical core and accurate through its learned correction. The cluster-management systems above are the intended consumers of that estimate.

3. What the Paper Contributes

Having established that the analytical ceiling over-predicts and that pure regression cannot recover the missing physics, the paper proposes an end-to-end predictor that is theoretical at its core and empirically grounded at its surface. The two stated contributions and the headline numbers they produce are summarised here; the mechanism that makes the hybrid work is developed in the sections that follow.

C1. A hybrid theoretical + empirical predictor

An end-to-end latency prediction method that is both theoretical and grounded in real-world execution data. The analytical Roofline estimate is computed per configuration and supplied to a regression model as an additional feature, so the regressor calibrates the theoretical ceiling against measured runtime overhead rather than learning the latency surface from scratch.

C2. Validation across servers and models

The method is validated by evaluating four open-source Hugging Face LLMs deployed on both the vLLM and Triton inference servers, with a set of controlled load-testing experiments on the authors' own single-A100 OpenShift cluster, isolating the effect of the analytical feature against regression-only baselines.

4. The Roofline Model as the Analytical Core

The analytical half of the predictor is the Roofline model, a framework that relates the computational capability of hardware, expressed in operations per second, to the bandwidth of memory access. Before reproducing the equations it is worth stating the plain idea: every kernel either spends its time moving data or spends its time computing, and which of the two dominates is decided by a single ratio. The Roofline model turns that ratio into a performance ceiling, and the paper uses that ceiling as the backbone of its latency estimate.

4.1 Arithmetic intensity and the two-piece ceiling

Arithmetic intensity is the ratio of operations performed to bytes moved through memory. A kernel with low arithmetic intensity is starved by memory bandwidth and a kernel with high arithmetic intensity is limited only by peak compute throughput; the crossover between the two regimes is the ridge point. The paper writes arithmetic intensity as the quotient of operations and memory access.

(1) $$\text{AI} = \frac{\text{OPs}}{\text{memory\_access}}$$

The achievable performance is then the smaller of two ceilings: the memory-bound ceiling, which scales linearly with arithmetic intensity at a slope equal to the memory bandwidth, and the compute-bound ceiling, which is the flat peak operations-per-second of the hardware. The piecewise form makes the ridge point explicit as the arithmetic intensity at which the two ceilings meet.

(2) $$\text{performance} = \begin{cases} \text{AI} \times \text{bandwidth}, & \text{if } \text{AI} < \dfrac{\text{max\_OPS}}{\text{bandwidth}} \\[1.2em] \text{max\_OPS}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

The Roofline diagram below renders this two-piece ceiling. The sloped segment is the memory-bound regime, the flat segment is the compute-bound regime, and the dashed vertical line is the ridge point that separates them. The amber marker is the analytical ceiling the Roofline model predicts; the rose marker is the latency the server actually delivers, which sits below the ceiling because of the runtime overhead the analytical model omits.

4.2 From ceiling to per-phase inference time

Once the attainable performance is known, the inference time for any operation or layer is the number of operations divided by that performance. The paper then composes the per-layer times into a total inference time that respects the two phases of LLM serving: a prefill phase that processes the prompt in parallel, and a decode phase that emits one token per step. The total is the sum of the prefill layer times plus, for each generated position, the sum of the decode layer times.

(3) $$\text{inference\_time} = \frac{\text{OPs}}{\text{performance}}$$
(4) $$\text{total\_inference\_time} = \sum_{l=1}^{L_\text{prefill}} \text{inference\_time}_l^{\text{prefill}} \;+\; \sum_{i=\text{prompt\_len}}^{\text{prompt\_len}+\text{gen\_len}} \left( \sum_{l=1}^{L_\text{decode}} \text{inference\_time}_l^{\text{decode}} \right)$$

The decode summation runs over each generated position from the end of the prompt to the end of generation, which captures the fact that the key-value cache grows with every emitted token and that the per-step memory traffic therefore rises monotonically through the decode loop. This composed quantity is the analytical latency the paper computes per configuration; it is correct in shape but optimistic in magnitude, which is exactly the property the learned correction must repair.

The analytical ceiling is sourced from an external tool, not re-derived. The paper does not re-derive these per-layer operation counts. It obtains the analytical estimate from the open-source LLM-Viewer tool, which already applies the Roofline model to a chosen LLM and hardware pair given input length, batch size, and generation length. Because LLM-Viewer supports only a subset of models, the authors extended its codebase to cover three additional LLMs of interest before harvesting the analytical latency as a feature.

5. The Hybrid Method End to End

The method joins three data sources into one feature vector per configuration and trains a standard regressor to predict the measured latency. The first source describes the model, the second describes the operational request, and the third is the analytical Roofline estimate from Section 4. The pipeline below shows how the analytical estimate enters as one feature alongside the static and operational features rather than replacing them.

Static model features (9)

Categorical: model name, architecture, model type, hidden activation. Numerical: number of parameters, hidden size, number of heads, number of layers, vocabulary size — all read from the model's Hugging Face config file.
↓

Operational parameters (3)

Number of concurrent users, generation length, and average content size in megabytes — collected under load testing while sweeping concurrency over 8, 16, 32 and generation length over 32, 64, 128 tokens.
↓

Analytical Roofline latency (the key extra feature)

The theoretical inference time from Equation (4), computed by the extended LLM-Viewer for each configuration's input length, batch size, and generation length. This single feature carries the hardware ceiling into the regressor.
↓

Regressor → predicted latency (ms)

Random Forest, XGBoost, or Linear Regression in their default scikit-learn configuration, with no fine-tuning, trained to map the combined feature vector to the measured end-to-end latency.

5.1 How the latency labels were measured

The training labels come from controlled load tests rather than synthetic estimates, which is what allows the regressor to learn the runtime residual. Each experiment fixes the generation prompt to the single string “What is artificial intelligence?” and varies only the number of concurrent users and the generation length, so that the measured latency reflects concurrency and decode length cleanly. Each configuration is a single-pass, non-multiturn run driven by the Locust load-testing tool for five minutes, repeated three times, with the reported latency taken as the average of the three runs because the authors observed minimal variation across repetitions on identical setups. The three collected operational parameters and the measured execution latency in milliseconds become the supervised target.

5.2 Two evaluation regimes: in-domain and out-of-domain

The paper separates two questions a deployed predictor must answer, because they stress the model differently. In-domain prediction trains and tests on a single LLM, isolating the impact of the operational parameters when the architecture is held constant; with only nine configurations per model the authors use three-fold cross-validation. Out-of-domain prediction trains on a set of LLMs and predicts the latency of a model excluded from training, which measures whether the static model features and the analytical ceiling together generalise to an architecture the regressor has never seen. The distinction matters operationally: the in-domain regime is what an operator faces when scaling a known model, while the out-of-domain regime is what an operator faces on the day a new model is released.

Linear Regression becomes the most effective method once the ceiling is a feature. The paper reasons that the Roofline feature already encodes the two physical bottlenecks the regressor would otherwise have to discover — the compute-bound and the memory-bandwidth-bound regimes. With those two regimes supplied analytically, the regressor's remaining job is to model the third, overhead bound, which it does by introducing a constant offset. A linear model is the most effective form for that residual, which is why Linear Regression is the best method in every cell once the analytical feature is present.

6.2 The compute-bound check

A natural worry is that the method only works because LLM decoding is overwhelmingly memory-bound, which a linear model captures easily. The authors address this directly by analysing each model at the layer level with LLM-Viewer and computing the proportion of compute-bound operations for the entire model across configurations. The average compute-bound ratio exceeded 19.5 percent for every LLM, which means a non-trivial fraction of the work sits in the flat compute-bound region of the Roofline. The hybrid retains its accuracy in that region, demonstrating that the predictor is not limited to memory-bound operations and remains applicable regardless of arithmetic intensity.

This 19.5% compute-bound fraction is exactly the regime our mechanism addresses. The compute-bound region is precisely the regime in which a GPU draws the most power, heats up the fastest, and is most likely to throttle its streaming-multiprocessor clock. The paper proves a fifth of the work lives there and then absorbs the resulting latency offset into an unexplained constant. Section 8 develops why that constant is not constant: it grows with input-token count as the clock droops.

7. The gpt2-xl Outlier and What It Reveals

One model behaves unlike the other three under out-of-domain prediction, and the paper's diagnosis of why is instructive for understanding the limits of static features. Three of the four models generalise strongly to an unseen architecture, with out-of-domain coefficients of determination above 0.85, but gpt2-xl collapses to 0.724 on vLLM and 0.454 on Triton. The explanation the authors give connects the failure to a structural property of the model rather than to noise.

Using LLM-Viewer, the authors compared the average number of operations across the four models. The gpt2-xl model has the fewest operations, averaging roughly 6.4 trillion, which is substantially smaller than the 10.4 to 16.1 trillion of the other three. The authors attribute this discrepancy to its small hidden size of 1600, which places gpt2-xl outside the operation-count range the regressor learned from the other models. When the out-of-domain regressor is asked to extrapolate to a model whose total work is far below anything in its training set, it has no anchor, and the analytical ceiling becomes the only feature carrying reliable signal. Both better-conditioned settings recover gpt2-xl: the in-domain column lifts it back above 0.91 on each server (0.917 on vLLM, 0.948 on Triton), and the RLM column lifts it above 0.94 on each server (0.951 on vLLM, 0.965 on Triton), confirming that the failure was extrapolation across operation-count scale, not an intrinsic difficulty of the model.

8. Relevance to Our Predictor

This paper is the closest prior art to our own per-step vLLM serving-latency predictor, and the relationship is best stated precisely. We are building a predictor that folds the GPU streaming-multiprocessor clock into the latency model as a hidden, involuntary, input-token-driven term. This paper builds a latency predictor whose analytical core over-predicts performance, and it absorbs the resulting gap with a learned regressor whose explanatory variable the authors describe only as the variability introduced by cloud infrastructure and dynamic workloads. The two papers are therefore looking at the same residual from opposite ends: this paper measures it and labels it cloud noise; we measure it and identify its mechanism.

8.1 The residual they call cloud noise has a name

The paper's own framing supplies the opening. The analytical Roofline ceiling assumes the hardware runs at its rated clock for the entire computation; that assumption is what makes it over-predict performance. Our measurements show that on H100, H200, A100-80, and Blackwell accelerators the streaming-multiprocessor clock falls by 19 to 31 percent as it moves from boost to throttle when the input-token count rises, and that the clock correlates with on-die temperature at a coefficient between negative 0.93 and negative 0.98. A clock that has fallen by a quarter under load delivers a quarter less compute than the rated peak the Roofline ceiling assumed, which is a first-order contributor to the very overestimation this paper reports. What the authors absorb into an unexplained constant offset is, in substantial part, the clock droop our predictor models explicitly.

8.2 Why their constant offset should have been a function of input tokens

The paper's reasoning that Linear Regression suffices because the residual is an overhead bound modelled by a constant is precisely the point at which our work diverges. The clock droop is not constant: it is driven by the input-token count, because more input tokens mean more compute-bound prefill work, which draws more power, raises temperature, and pushes the clock further into throttle. The paper itself establishes that more than 19.5 percent of the work is compute-bound, which is the regime where this thermal coupling is strongest. A model that treats the residual as a constant offset will therefore be systematically wrong in the high-input-token, prefill-heavy, compute-bound corner of the configuration space — the same corner where our term matters most. Folding the clock in as an explicit term that depends on input-token count is the modelling step this paper did not take.

8.3 What we borrow and where our novelty is partial

We are explicit that our novelty is partial, and this paper is part of the reason. The hybrid recipe of an analytical ceiling reconciled with a learned residual is established here, and the learnability of latency from a Roofline feature is established here; we adopt both. What we add is the identification and explicit modelling of the involuntary clock droop as a function of input-token count inside a general serving predictor, where prior work either assumed a fixed clock or folded the droop into opaque noise. The comparison below states the division of labour between this paper and ours.

This paper (Imai et al.)

  • Analytical Roofline ceiling as a regressor feature.
  • Residual learned, attributed to generic cloud variability.
  • Residual treated as a constant overhead offset.
  • Clock assumed at rated frequency throughout.

Our predictor

The selection rationale reduces to one sentence. We selected this paper because it is the prior work that comes nearest to our hypothesis and stops one step short of it: it sees the residual our term explains, validates that the residual is learnable, and then names the wrong cause. Our contribution is to replace cloud variability with a measured, mechanistic, input-token-driven clock term inside the same hybrid the authors pioneered.

9. Limitations and Open Questions

The paper is a four-page workshop contribution, and its scope is bounded accordingly. The limitations below are not criticisms of the result but markers of where a full predictor would need to go further, several of which our own work takes up directly.

Strengths

  • ✓ An interpretable analytical core combined with an accurate learned correction, the best of both modelling traditions.
  • ✓ Validated on two serving stacks and four architectures rather than a single configuration.
  • ✓ The compute-bound check rules out the easy explanation that the gains are an artefact of memory-bound decoding.

Limitations

  • ✗ The residual is attributed to undifferentiated cloud variability; its mechanism, including clock droop, is never decomposed.
  • ✗ Only nine configurations per model and four sub-2.7B models on a single A100; concurrency tops out at 32 users.
  • ▲ The fixed prompt and single-pass, non-multiturn runs do not exercise the long-context, prefill-heavy regime where clock droop is largest.

The most consequential gap for a downstream predictor is the treatment of the residual as a structureless constant. Because the experiments fix the prompt and cap concurrency at thirty-two users, they never enter the operating point at which the streaming-multiprocessor clock droops most, so the constant-offset assumption is never stress-tested. A predictor that intends to serve long-context, high-input-token workloads — which is the regime our work targets — cannot inherit that assumption, and must instead model the residual as a function of the input-token count that drives the droop.
