POLCA

Power Oversubscription in LLM Cloud Providers — characterizing when LLM inference draws GPUs to their power cap, and reclaiming the headroom with a frequency-capping controller.

arXiv:2308.12908 ASPLOS 2024 DOI 10.1145/3620666.3651329 Power / GPU Frequency

Pratyush Patel, Esha Choukse, Chaojie Zhang, Íñigo Goiri, Brijesh Warrier, Nithish Mahalingam, Ricardo Bianchini

University of Washington · Microsoft Azure Research

1. Motivation: Why LLM Power Provisioning Wastes the Datacenter

A modern GPU datacenter is built to a fixed power envelope. Every rack, every power-distribution unit, and every uninterruptible-power-supply branch is sized to the nameplate thermal-design power (TDP) of the equipment underneath it, because the building’s electrical infrastructure—not its floor space—is the binding constraint. This section establishes why that sizing convention, inherited from CPU-era clouds, leaves a large fraction of the most expensive resource in an LLM cluster permanently unused, and why recovering it is now a first-order economic question rather than a tuning detail.

1.1 Why now?

Until 2022, GPU clusters at hyperscale were overwhelmingly used for training, where every accelerator runs flat-out for hours and power draw sits close to TDP for the duration of a job. Provisioning to the nameplate was therefore close to correct. The arrival of ChatGPT-class services inverted the fleet mix: inference now consumes a growing majority of accelerator-hours, and an inference GPU spends most of its time in the memory-bandwidth-bound token-generation phase, far below its rated power. The paper measures that across a range of models, inference peak power utilization reaches only about 79% of the provisioned envelope, against 97% for training on the same hardware. Provisioning the building for the training-era worst case therefore strands close to one fifth of the most capital-intensive resource in the facility on every inference rack.

1.2 A concrete bad scenario

Consider one row of an Azure GPU datacenter populated with eight-way A100-80GB servers, each server provisioned at its nameplate TDP so that the row breaker is sized to the sum of nameplates. The row serves a BLOOM-176B inference endpoint. During the brief prompt-processing phase of a request—the compute-bound matrix multiplication over the full input—a server’s aggregate GPU power can momentarily climb above the summed GPU TDP by as much as 500 W, and the prompt spikes of nearby servers can momentarily align. For the remaining, far longer token-generation phase, each GPU settles to a stable draw well under TDP. Because the operator cannot know in advance that the spikes will not all align, the row is provisioned for the case where they do. In practice that worst case is observed to consume only 79% of the provisioned row power, and the largest power swing across a two-second window is just 9%. The remaining 21% of breaker capacity is paid for, cooled, and never delivered to a workload.

1.3 Quantified baseline pain

The cost of conservative provisioning is best stated as stranded power. Under nameplate provisioning, an inference row that peaks at 79% of its envelope wastes roughly one fifth of the single most expensive and longest-lead-time resource a GPU operator buys: datacenter power capacity, whose construction lead time is measured in years. The paper’s production measurements also show that GPU power constitutes on average 60% of server-level power, so the stranded fraction is concentrated precisely in the components the operator most wants to amortize. Before any of POLCA’s machinery is introduced, the status quo is therefore a fleet whose inference rows run at four-fifths of their paid-for power ceiling, indefinitely.

1.4 The obvious fix, and why it fails

An obvious response is to oversubscribe power exactly as CPU clouds have done for a decade: install more servers than the breaker can simultaneously sustain at nameplate, and rely on the statistical fact that not every machine peaks at once. The strawman fails on LLM hardware for two structural reasons. First, GPUs draw a far larger and far spikier fraction of server power than CPUs, so a coincident alignment of prompt-phase spikes can exceed the breaker not by a tolerable margin but by hundreds of watts per device, and the existing safety mechanism—the hardware powerbrake—collapses every affected GPU to 288 MHz, an order of magnitude below base clock, with severe and indiscriminate performance damage. Second, a naive CPU-style oversubscription policy has no notion of workload priority, so when an excursion does occur it throttles latency-critical and best-effort work identically. Recovering the headroom safely therefore requires a control mechanism that is both fast enough to act before the powerbrake and selective enough to protect the workloads that matter.

1.5 The fundamental obstacle

The obstacle is an online-control gap between two very different timescales. A request’s prompt-phase power spike lasts well under a second and cannot be predicted at the row level in advance, yet the only out-of-band actuator that does not depend on the tenant’s software stack—capping the GPU streaming-multiprocessor clock through the baseboard management controller—takes about 40 seconds to take effect across a row. The safety device that does act fast, the powerbrake, is so blunt that triggering it is itself a failure. The operator must therefore hold the row below its breaker using a 40-second-latency knob against sub-second excursions, without ever falling back on the 288 MHz brake. This timescale mismatch, not the existence of headroom, is the hard part of the problem, and the design of POLCA is organized entirely around bridging it.

1.6 Position relative to prior work

Power oversubscription itself is an established CPU-cloud technique, and several mechanisms that POLCA composes are individually well known; the paper’s contribution is to characterize LLM inference power specifically and to assemble those mechanisms into a priority-aware controller for it. Prior CPU power-capping work such as Dynamo and the broader thread of datacenter power management assumed CPU-dominated servers whose power is comparatively flat and whose actuators (RAPL) respond in milliseconds; none of that transfers cleanly to GPU servers with sub-second multi-hundred-watt spikes and 40-second actuators. GPU frequency scaling for energy efficiency has long been studied in the HPC literature, but for throughput jobs rather than latency-SLO inference, and without a row-level oversubscription objective. The LLM-serving systems most familiar to this audience—such as Sarathi-Serve and prefill/decode disaggregation—optimize latency and throughput within a fixed power budget and treat power as a constant; POLCA is complementary, treating the same prefill/decode phase distinction as a power signal to be exploited at the cluster level rather than a scheduling signal at the engine level.

2. What POLCA Contributes

Having established that inference rows run a fifth below their power ceiling and that the obstacle is a timescale-mismatched control problem, the paper makes three contributions. It first characterizes LLM inference and training power at production scale, isolating the prompt/token phase distinction as the source of the headroom. It then quantifies how much power GPU frequency capping can reclaim against how much latency it costs. Finally it composes these findings into POLCA, a reactive, priority-aware, dual-threshold controller that holds an oversubscribed row below its breaker. The headline outcome is the ability to deploy 30% more inference servers in the same power-constrained cluster with minimal performance loss.

C1 — Phase-resolved power characterization

The compute-bound prompt phase spikes a GPU above its TDP, while the memory-bound token phase settles well below it. This phase asymmetry, not model size alone, is what makes inference power oversubscribable.

C2 — Quantified frequency-capping trade-off

Capping the GPU SM clock reclaims up to 20% of peak power for at most a 7% inference latency cost, and the power-versus-performance relationship is superlinear: the first watts are the cheapest to give back.

C3 — Dual-threshold priority-aware controller

POLCA caps low-priority GPUs first at a lower row-power threshold and only touches high-priority GPUs at a higher one, holding the oversubscribed row below the breaker with a 40-second actuator and never invoking the powerbrake.

3. Phase-Resolved Power: Prompt Spikes, Token Plateau

The central measurement of the paper is that an LLM inference request does not draw a single, steady amount of power; it draws power in two phases whose profiles differ sharply. The prompt phase ingests the entire input at once as a large, dense matrix multiplication that saturates the GPU’s compute units; the token phase then emits one output token at a time, each step a small operation gated by the bandwidth of reading the model weights and the key-value cache from memory. This section walks through the measured timeseries that establish the asymmetry and then explains, in terms of the underlying roofline, why it holds for every model the paper tested.

3.1 The two-phase power timeseries

The clearest evidence is a power-over-time trace of a single inference. The prompt phase appears as a brief, tall spike; the token phase appears as a long, lower plateau. The two figures below show this for OPT-30B and BLOOM-176B, and the same shape recurs across the model range the paper measured.

Figure 4c: GPU power over time for OPT-30B inference. The prompt phase produces a short, high spike; the token phase settles to a lower, stable plateau.
Figure 4d: The same two-phase signature for BLOOM-176B. A larger model raises the absolute plateau but does not erase the prompt-versus-token gap.

The token-phase plateau is what makes inference look low-power on average; the prompt-phase spike is what forces conservative provisioning. A scheduler that interleaves many requests smooths the prompt spikes of any one request into the aggregate, but it cannot remove the worst case in which several requests begin their prompt phase together, and it is that worst case the breaker must survive.

3.2 What drives the spike: input length, batch, output

The paper isolates the three request parameters that could plausibly move power and finds them sharply unequal in effect. Input length and batch size both raise the prompt-phase peak, because both enlarge the compute-bound matrix multiplication. Output length, by contrast, leaves the peak essentially unchanged and merely lengthens the low token plateau, because each additional output token is one more memory-bound step at the same modest power. The figure below shows the input-length sweep; it is the clearest single piece of evidence that the GPU’s peak draw is governed by the size of the compute-bound prefill, not by the request as a whole.

Figure 5a: Peak GPU power versus input (prompt) length across models. Longer inputs enlarge the compute-bound prompt matrix multiplication, raising the peak across every model tested; output length, in contrast, does not move the peak.

The consequence for provisioning is that peak power is a function of input-token count and batch, the two quantities that set the arithmetic intensity of the prefill. A workload of long-context, large-batch requests is far closer to TDP than a workload of short prompts streaming long answers, even when both keep the GPU equally busy by the usual utilization metric. Power, in other words, is sensitive to which phase a request is in, in a way that GPU-utilization percentage is not.

4. The Frequency Knob and Its Price

Knowing that inference rows carry headroom is useless without a safe way to enforce a power ceiling when an excursion threatens. The paper studies two actuators the GPU exposes—an explicit power cap in watts and an SM-clock-frequency cap in MHz—and measures, for each, how much peak power they remove and how much inference performance they cost. This section first names the two knobs and the powerbrake of last resort, then presents the measured power-versus-performance curve that justifies frequency capping as the controller’s primary lever.

4.1 Two knobs and a brake

An A100 exposes a power cap configurable from 100 W to 400 W and an SM clock configurable from 0.2 GHz to 1.4 GHz, with a base frequency of 1275 MHz. Beneath both sits the hardware powerbrake, a fast (~5 s) safety mechanism that collapses the clock to 288 MHz when row power exceeds 100% of provisioned capacity. The table summarizes the three control points, their latency, and their intended role. The contrast that matters is between the fine, slow software knobs and the blunt, fast powerbrake: the controller’s entire job is to use the former often enough that the latter never fires.

The paper prefers capping the SM clock directly over setting a power cap. A power cap states a watt target and lets the GPU pick whatever frequency keeps it under that target, which makes the resulting performance harder to predict across models; a frequency cap states the performance point directly, so the operator can reason about the latency cost up front. 4.2 The superlinear power-performance curve

The economic case for frequency capping rests on the shape of the trade-off, not merely its existence. Because dynamic power scales super-linearly with clock frequency and voltage, a small reduction in clock removes a disproportionately large amount of power, so the first watts reclaimed are nearly free in latency while the last are expensive. Across the models tested, capping the clock reclaims up to 20% of peak power for a maximum latency cost of about 7%, and a model as large as BLOOM shows only a 5% slowdown at the operating point where a smaller model such as GPT-NeoX shows none. The figure plots this curve for every model, and the knee—the region where a controller should operate—sits at the low-performance-cost end.

Figure 7a: Peak power reduction versus performance reduction across all inference models. The relationship is superlinear: the early power savings cost almost no latency, justifying a controller that operates at the knee of the curve.

This superlinearity is the quantitative foundation of the whole design. It means the controller need not choose between a safe row and a fast row; it can hold the row safely under the breaker while giving up only single-digit percentages of performance, and it can spend that small budget preferentially on the workloads that can most afford it.

5. Where the Headroom Lives

The phase asymmetry of a single request becomes oversubscribable capacity only at aggregate scale, where many requests on many servers are statistically multiplexed. This section quantifies the gap between provisioned and consumed power at the server and row level, distinguishes inference from training, and explains why the inference headroom is both large and safe to harvest while the training headroom is neither.

5.1 Peak power versus nameplate TDP

The first headroom measurement compares observed peak power to the nameplate TDP that provisioning assumes. Across the production fleet, server and GPU peaks sit visibly below their TDPs for inference, and the gap between the bar and the 100% line is exactly the power an operator pays for but never uses. 5.2 Why inference is safe to oversubscribe but training is not

Oversubscription is safe only when the aggregate power is both low on average and slow to move, so that a 40-second actuator can react before a coincident excursion reaches the breaker. Inference satisfies both conditions and training satisfies neither, which is why the paper restricts its capacity claim to inference rows. The table contrasts the two workloads on the three properties that decide oversubscribability.

The decisive number is the swing rate. Training power can move by 37.5% within two seconds, far faster than a 40-second clock cap can respond, so an oversubscribed training row would hit the breaker before any soft actuator could intervene. Inference power moves by only about 9% over the same window and at most 11.8% over forty seconds, which is exactly the timescale of the available actuator. Inference is therefore controllable with the tools at hand; training is not, and the paper deliberately confines its 30% capacity claim to inference.

6. The POLCA Controller

POLCA turns the characterization into a deployable policy. It is a reactive controller that watches row-level power at the power-distribution-unit breaker and, when power crosses one of two thresholds, caps GPU clocks in a priority-aware order so that low-priority work absorbs the throttling first. The design is shaped by the timescale obstacle of Section 1.5: it must keep a row below its breaker using a 40-second actuator while never letting the 5-second powerbrake fire. This section describes the dual-threshold control loop, the priority ordering, and the safety margins that make the slow actuator sufficient.

6.1 Row-level statistical multiplexing

POLCA acts at the granularity of a row—the set of servers behind one PDU breaker—rather than at the individual server, because the headroom only exists in aggregate. A single server’s prompt spike is sharp, but across a row of many servers running independent requests, the prompt phases of different requests rarely align, so the summed row power is far smoother than any one server’s. The cluster allocator reinforces this by placing a mix of high-priority and low-priority jobs in every row, which both smooths the aggregate and guarantees that there is always low-priority work available to throttle first.

6.2 The dual-threshold control loop

The control loop is built from two row-power thresholds, T1 below T2, each tied to a graduated response. Below T1 nothing is capped and the row runs at full speed. Between T1 and T2 the controller caps only the low-priority GPUs, on the principle that the cheapest performance to surrender is the performance the operator already designated as best-effort. Above T2 the controller first deepens the low-priority cap and, only if power still rises, lightly caps the high-priority GPUs as well; even then the high-priority cap is shallow, set just below base clock. The vertical flow below traces a single excursion through these states.

Row power < T1 (e.g. 80%)

Normal operation. No GPU is capped; both priority classes run at full SM clock.
↓

T1 ≤ row power < T2 (e.g. 80–89%)

Cap low-priority GPUs to base clock (1275 MHz), then to 1110 MHz if needed. High-priority work is untouched.
↓

Row power ≥ T2 (e.g. 89%)

Low-priority already deeply capped; now lightly cap high-priority GPUs to 1305 MHz. Goal: avoid the powerbrake entirely.
↓

Row power ≥ 100% (must never happen)

Hardware powerbrake forces 288 MHz across the row within ~5 s. POLCA’s thresholds are tuned so this fallback is never reached.

The thresholds the paper recommends are T1 = 80% and T2 = 89% of provisioned row power, the pair that supports 30% extra servers while keeping the latency cost within the SLOs of both priority classes. Choosing T1 and T2 is itself an offline search over a small parameter space, refreshed only infrequently from historical power traces, so the online controller stays simple.

6.3 Bridging the timescale gap with hysteresis

The 40-second out-of-band actuator is too slow to chase a sub-second spike directly, so POLCA does not try to. It instead reserves the gap between T2 and the breaker as a safety margin that the slow knob can defend, and it relies on the row aggregate moving slowly enough—at most 11.8% over forty seconds—that acting at T2 reliably arrests the rise before 100%. To prevent the controller from oscillating between capped and uncapped states near a threshold, the uncap thresholds are set about 5% below the corresponding cap thresholds, a standard hysteresis band that keeps the row from chattering. The out-of-band path uses the baseboard management controller’s SMBPBI interface so that capping never depends on the tenant’s VM being healthy.

7. Results

The evaluation combines hardware power measurements of real models with a cluster simulator driven by production power traces, and it answers two questions: how many extra servers the recovered headroom buys, and at what performance cost to each priority class. The headline result is a 30% increase in inference servers per power-constrained cluster at minimal performance loss; the supporting results show that the dual-threshold policy keeps the powerbrake from firing across the simulated trace.

7.2 Strengths and limits of the evaluation

The evaluation is strong on real-hardware power characterization and on the production-trace-driven simulation of the controller, but the capacity claim itself rests on simulation rather than a deployed oversubscribed cluster, and the model set is the 2023 generation. The two columns below separate what the paper demonstrates directly from where a reader should apply caution.

Demonstrated directly

  • Prompt-versus-token power asymmetry on real GPUs across four model scales.
  • Superlinear power–performance curve from direct frequency sweeps.
  • 79% inference vs 97% training peak utilization from production telemetry.

Apply with caution

  • The +30% capacity figure comes from a trace-driven simulator, not a deployed oversubscribed row.
  • Models predate the 2024–2025 long-context and MoE serving regimes that shift the phase mix.
  • The latency cost is reported as an aggregate; per-request tail effects under coincident capping are not fully characterized.

8. Relevance to Our Per-Step Latency Predictor

Our work builds a per-step vLLM serving-latency predictor that folds the GPU’s streaming-multiprocessor clock in as a hidden, involuntary, input-token-driven term. The following figures are from our own measurements, not from the POLCA paper, which characterizes A100-generation power rather than per-step latency on newer parts: in our setup the clock falls by 19–31% from boost to throttle as the input-token count rises, with a clock-versus-temperature correlation of about −0.93 to −0.98 on H100, H200, A100-80GB, and Blackwell parts. POLCA contributes the orthogonal, datacenter-scale evidence that the GPU clock is load- and phase-dependent rather than a constant of the hardware, and that is precisely why we selected it as a required reference.

8.1 Three points of contact

POLCA and our predictor study the same physical phenomenon—GPU clock moving with load—from opposite ends, and they agree on the three facts our model depends on.

Phase-dependence confirms our M-driven term

POLCA shows compute-bound prefill drives the GPU to its power cap (and thus a frequency cut) while memory-bound decode does not. This is the datacenter-LLM evidence that our clock term must be a function of input-token count M, not a flat constant: the same load that raises peak power is the load that triggers the droop our predictor models as f(M).

Light decode-serving stays at boost

POLCA’s token-phase plateau sits well below TDP, so a lightly loaded decode-only endpoint never reaches the power cap. This matches our observation that under light decode-serving the SXM cards stay pinned at boost, and it bounds where our clock term is active: it matters under compute-bound, high-M, or prefill-heavy load, and is near-zero for light decode.

Involuntary vs deliberate capping

POLCA caps the clock deliberately, as a control action; our predictor models the clock dropping involuntarily, from the device’s own thermal and power limits at default settings. Read together, they say the same clock variable is exposed both to an external controller and to internal physics—so any latency model that assumes a fixed clock is wrong on both counts.

8.2 How our novelty is positioned against POLCA

Our novelty is partial and we state it plainly. The droop mechanism—clock falling under power and thermal limits—and the learnability of latency from clock are both established, and POLCA is one of the works that establishes the load-dependence at scale. Our gap is to model the involuntary droop as a function of the input-token count M inside a general per-step serving predictor, rather than as a control knob an operator turns. POLCA optimizes the cluster by deliberately moving the clock; we predict per-step latency by accounting for the clock moving on its own. The phenomenon is shared; the use we make of it is what we contribute.

9. Limitations and Outlook

POLCA is a characterization-plus-controller paper from 2023, and its scope reflects the hardware and workloads of that moment. The findings that matter most to a latency predictor—the phase asymmetry and the load-dependent clock—are robust, but several boundaries should temper how its specific numbers are reused, particularly as serving has moved toward long context and disaggregated phases.

9.1 Boundaries of the result

The boundaries below are stated so that a reader does not over-extend the paper’s conclusions to settings it did not measure.

  • The capacity claim is simulator-based; a production deployment could encounter correlated excursions the trace did not contain.
  • Specific watt and frequency numbers are A100-generation; H100, H200, and Blackwell shift both the TDP and the boost behavior our own measurements target.
  • Long-context and prefill/decode-disaggregated serving change the phase mix per server, which can raise the time-averaged power and shrink the inference headroom POLCA harvests.

9.2 The durable takeaway

Stripped of its 2023 specifics, the durable lesson is that GPU clock is a dependent variable of the serving workload, set by the interaction of phase, power budget, and thermal state, and that this dependence has cluster-scale economic value. For the operator, that value is 30% more servers; for a latency model, it is the reason the clock cannot be treated as a constant. Both readings start from the same measured fact that compute-bound prefill, driven by input-token count, is what pushes a GPU against its limits.
