throttLL'eM

Predictive GPU Throttling for Energy-Efficient LLM Inference Serving — projecting future KV-cache usage and batch size to set the lowest GPU clock that still meets the SLO.

arXiv 2024 · 2408.05235 DVFS / Energy Iteration-level Predictor A100 × 8

Andreas Kosmas Kakolyris, Dimosthenis Masouros, Petros Vavaroutsos, Sotirios Xydis, Dimitrios Soudris

National Technical University of Athens

1. Why Energy, and Why Now

The economics of large-language-model serving are dominated by inference rather than training. Training a frontier model is a one-time capital event; serving it is a perpetual operating cost that scales with every user request, and the paper notes that inference accounts for over 90% of a deployed model's compute cycles — so it dominates the lifetime energy footprint. Data-center electricity demand is on a steep curve: the paper cites a projection of roughly 1050 TWh of consumption by 2026, driven substantially by AI workloads. As providers field interactive chat, batch summarization, and agentic pipelines against the same GPU fleet, the marginal cost of one more token is increasingly an electricity-bill question rather than a hardware-availability question.

The central observation that makes savings possible is that the boost clock is frequently overkill. A GPU pinned at its maximum frequency completes each decode iteration faster than the service-level objective requires, and the surplus speed is converted directly into wasted joules. The figure below, reproduced from the paper, sweeps batch size against GPU frequency and reports throughput in tokens per second. Throughput saturates well before the top of the frequency range at the lower batch sizes that characterize latency-sensitive serving, which means a substantial band of the highest frequencies buys almost no additional useful work.

Figure 2a (from paper): System throughput as a joint function of batch size and GPU frequency. At small batch sizes (latency-sensitive serving) throughput saturates far below the 1410 MHz boost clock — the high-frequency band yields little extra work.
Figure 2e (from paper): Energy efficiency in tokens per joule across the same sweep. The most efficient operating points lie in an interior frequency band, not at the boost clock; running at maximum frequency forfeits efficiency the SLO never asked for.

1.1 A concrete waste scenario

Consider, as an illustrative scenario distinct from the Azure trace used later in the evaluation, a chat assistant serving Llama2-13B at tensor-parallel degree two, driven by a conversational workload whose median request carries roughly 161 input tokens and emits about 213 output tokens, while a long tail above the 95th percentile pushes past 1,000 generated tokens. The evaluated end-to-end SLO is a 30.2-second p99 deadline with a per-token deadline of roughly 30 ms between tokens. A typical 213-token reply therefore needs only about 6.4 seconds of decode at 30 ms per token, leaving more than 23 seconds of slack against the 30.2-second budget — and even a 1,000-token tail request at the 95th percentile finishes around 30 seconds, still inside the deadline. Pinned at the 1410 MHz boost clock the configuration completes these requests well inside that budget — the latency distributions in the evaluation sit comfortably below the deadline line — which means the boost clock is buying latency margin the contract never asked for. That margin is paid for in watts: each decode iteration runs at peak power even though a much lower clock would still finish on time, so the surplus speed converts directly into wasted joules. Across a busy hour of such requests the cluster expends a large fraction of its energy purchasing deadline slack the service contract did not require.

1.2 The baseline pain number

The status quo against which the paper measures is NVIDIA's Triton inference server with the TensorRT-LLM backend, run at its default fixed boost frequency. Under that configuration the system reaches an energy efficiency of about 0.69 tokens per joule on the evaluated trace. The paper's measurements indicate that the same SLOs can be honored at far lower clocks, and the gap between 0.69 tokens per joule and what is physically achievable is the waste the framework targets. Stated as energy rather than efficiency, throttLL'eM later recovers up to 43.8% of total energy consumption relative to this baseline while still satisfying the deadlines, which sets the size of the prize: nearly half the serving energy was margin.

1.3 Strawman: a reactive hardware governor

An obvious response is to delegate the decision to the GPU's own reactive frequency governor: let the hardware lower the clock when utilization drops and raise it when load returns. This fails for inference because the governor responds to the present, while a service-level objective is a statement about the future. A reactive governor cannot know that the batch about to be admitted will require a higher clock to meet a deadline several hundred iterations away; by the time utilization has risen enough for it to react, the request may already be mathematically late. The decode loop is also too fine-grained for a coarse hardware control loop — a single iteration lasts tens of milliseconds, and the composition of the batch changes at that cadence as requests arrive and complete. A correct setting must be chosen per iteration, with foreknowledge of how the batch will evolve, which is precisely what a reactive governor lacks.

1.4 The fundamental obstacle

The deeper difficulty is that the right clock for the current iteration depends on quantities that will only be known in the future. Decode throughput at a fixed frequency is governed chiefly by the batch size and by the total KV-cache occupancy, and both of these are moving targets: requests join and leave the batch continuously, and each surviving request's KV footprint grows by one block every few iterations as its sequence lengthens. To choose a frequency that will satisfy an end-to-end deadline, the controller must know how the batch and the cache will look across the entire remaining horizon of the request, not just at the current instant. That horizon in turn depends on how many tokens each request will ultimately generate — a quantity that is unknown at admission and, as prior work has shown, is difficult to predict precisely. The framework must therefore reason about future load under genuine uncertainty rather than assume it away.

1.5 Position relative to prior work

Energy-aware GPU scheduling and DVFS are mature topics in general HPC, but LLM serving introduces structure that earlier systems did not exploit, and the few LLM-specific energy systems each left something on the table for an iteration-level, SLO-aware design. The table below positions these prior efforts along three columns: the first names the prior approach, the second states the mechanism it uses, and the third identifies the specific gap it leaves for throttLL'eM to close.

Prior approach What it does What it left on the table
Reactive HW governor (nvidia-smi / driver) Adjusts clock from instantaneous utilization. No model of the future batch or deadline; cannot guarantee an SLO set hundreds of iterations ahead.
Zeus / batch-size energy tuning Tunes a single power-limit / batch knob for training-style jobs. Job-granular and SLO-free; does not track the per-iteration KV growth of autoregressive decode.
Orca / continuous (in-flight) batching Schedules at iteration granularity for throughput. Treats the clock as fixed; energy is never a decision variable.

throttLL'eM occupies the intersection these works leave empty: it predicts the future load of the decode loop at iteration granularity and uses that prediction to drive a frequency knob under an explicit SLO. The clock is treated as a controlled variable that the system deliberately sets to the lowest deadline-satisfying value.

2. What throttLL'eM Contributes

Having established that boost-clock serving wastes nearly half its energy and that no reactive mechanism can recover it safely, the paper proposes a predictive control framework. Its contributions cluster into a characterization study, a pair of projection mechanisms, a learned performance model, and the closed-loop controller that ties them together. The cards below summarize each; the sections that follow develop them in turn.

Characterization of the energy landscape

A systematic sweep of GPU frequency, batch size, KV-cache utilization, and tensor-parallel degree, quantifying how each shapes throughput, latency, power, and tokens-per-joule across three models spanning five engine configurations.

Analytical KV & batch projection

A scoreboard-based mechanism that projects, for every future iteration, the total KV blocks and the batch size, with mean prediction errors of 2.26% and 0.19% respectively.

Learned iteration-level performance model

A gradient-boosted decision tree mapping engine size, batch size, KV occupancy, and GPU frequency to iterations per second, with R² > 0.97 and a mean error under one iteration per second.

SLO-aware frequency + instance control

A controller that binary-searches the frequency range for the lowest SLO-satisfying clock per iteration, combined with autoscaling of tensor-parallel instances, delivering up to 43.8% energy reduction.

The key insight that organizes the design is that an SLO is a budget, and a fixed boost clock spends that budget all at once. If the system can predict how fast it needs to go to finish exactly on time, it can convert every unused millisecond of slack into saved energy by lowering the clock — but only if it can see the future shape of the workload, which is what the projection mechanisms supply.

3. System Architecture

throttLL'eM sits between the request stream and the inference backend as a predictive control layer. Each incoming query passes through a generation-length predictor, after which a scoreboard maintains the metadata needed to project future load. On every decode iteration the projected load is fed to a learned performance model, the model's throughput prediction is checked against the SLO inside the scheduler, and the controller then selects the lowest GPU frequency that still satisfies the deadline. The same projections drive an autoscaler that chooses how many tensor-parallel instances to run. The paper's overview figure, reproduced below, lays out these components and the data flowing between them.

Figure 6 (from paper): throttLL'eM overview. A query is tokenized and length-predicted; the scoreboard projects KV usage and batch size; the performance model M(E,KV,B,F) predicts throughput T; the scheduler checks SLO and KV violations; per-GPU throttling controllers pick the minimum frequency satisfying the SLOs, while autoscaling adjusts instance count from RPS.

3.1 The control loop, end to end

The framework's logic is easiest to follow as a single pass through the loop, from a query arriving to a frequency being applied. The vertical flow below traces that path; each stage corresponds to one of the boxes in the overview figure and is developed in its own section later.

① Generation-length prediction

On arrival, a query of input length |qᵢ| is assigned a predicted output length |rᵢ|̂. This sets the horizon over which the request will occupy the batch and grow the cache.
↓

② Scoreboard & projection

The scoreboard stores (scheduled iteration, input length, predicted length) per active request. From it the system computes, analytically, the KV-block vector and batch-size vector for every future iteration in roughly 2 ms.
↓

③ Performance model M(E,KV,B,F)

The projected KV and batch values, the engine size E, and a candidate frequency F are passed to a gradient-boosted tree that returns the predicted iterations per second, in roughly 3 ms on CPU.
↓

④ Scheduler admission check

Using a virtual scoreboard, the scheduler tests whether admitting the query keeps every active request within its KV capacity, its per-token deadline, and its end-to-end deadline. Failing checks send the query to a queue.
↓

⑤ Frequency throttling controller

A binary search over [210, 1410] MHz finds the lowest frequency at which the model still predicts SLO compliance, and that clock is applied to the GPU for the next iteration.
The architectural consequence of this layout is that every component above operates on the CPU and out of the critical inference path: projection costs about 2 ms and model inference about 3 ms, against decode iterations of tens of milliseconds. The control layer therefore runs concurrently with the GPU, so the prediction-and-decide overhead is hidden rather than added to user-visible latency.

4. Projecting Future KV Usage and Batch Size

The performance model can only be useful if it is fed the load the GPU will actually experience, not the load it experiences now. Because autoregressive decode follows a rigid arithmetic — each surviving request emits one token per iteration and its KV cache grows by one block every N tokens — the future load is not random but analytically computable from a small amount of per-request bookkeeping. This section explains how the scoreboard turns that bookkeeping into per-iteration vectors of KV occupancy and batch size.

4.1 The scoreboard

The scoreboard is a small table holding one row per active request. Each row records the iteration at which the request was scheduled, its input length, and its predicted generation length. These three numbers are sufficient to reconstruct, for any future iteration, whether the request is still running and how many KV blocks it occupies — which is the entire basis of the projection.

4.2 Per-request KV projection

For a request scheduled at iteration $s_i$ with input length $|q_i|$ and predicted generation length $\hat{|r_i|}$, the number of KV blocks it occupies at a future iteration $j$ is the total number of tokens it holds so far, divided by the block size $N$ and rounded up. The request holds blocks only while it is active — that is, from its scheduled iteration until it has emitted its predicted number of tokens — and contributes zero otherwise. The paper expresses this as a single closed form.

(1) $$KV^{q_i}[j] = \begin{cases} \left\lceil \dfrac{j - s_i + |q_i|}{N} \right\rceil & \text{if } s_i \leq j < s_i + \hat{|r_i|} \\[6pt] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

The numerator $j - s_i + |q_i|$ is the running token count: the input tokens present from the start, plus one decoded token for every iteration elapsed since scheduling. Dividing by $N$ and taking the ceiling converts that token count into the integer number of paged-attention blocks the request currently pins. Because the expression is exact arithmetic rather than a learned estimate, its only source of error is the predicted length $\hat{|r_i|}$, which controls the upper edge of the active window; a length misprediction shifts the moment the request stops contributing blocks, but does not perturb the per-iteration growth rate.

4.3 Aggregating into system-wide vectors

The total KV occupancy at iteration $j$ is simply the sum of the per-request occupancies over all active requests, and the batch size at iteration $j$ is the count of requests whose active window contains $j$. Summing Equation 1 over the scoreboard therefore yields the two vectors the performance model needs.

(2) $$KV[j] = \sum_{\forall q_i} KV^{q_i}[j], \qquad B[j] = \bigl|\{\, q_i : s_i \leq j < s_i + \hat{|r_i|} \,\}\bigr|$$

These vectors are the workload as the GPU will see it across the remaining horizon. The paper reports that this analytical projection achieves a mean absolute error of 2.26% on KV occupancy and 0.19% on batch size — accurate enough that the dominant remaining uncertainty is the generation-length prediction feeding it, not the projection arithmetic itself.

5. The Iteration-Level Performance Model

The projections describe the load; the performance model translates load and a candidate frequency into the speed the GPU will actually deliver. This learned function is the heart of the framework, because it is what allows the controller to ask the counterfactual question — how fast would this iteration run at frequency F? — without running the iteration. The figure below illustrates the empirical relationship the model must capture.

Figure 3a (from paper): Iteration throughput (IPS) falls monotonically as KV-cache occupancy grows, for each batch size. The decline is smooth and regular — exactly the kind of structure a regression model can learn from a modest number of samples.

5.1 Inputs, output, and model class

The model $\mathcal{M}$ is a gradient-boosted decision tree — an ensemble of shallow trees in which each successive tree corrects the residual error of the ensemble so far. This class is chosen because the target surface is smooth and low-dimensional but nonlinear, with saturation effects (throughput flattens at high frequency) and interaction effects (the frequency at which throughput saturates depends on batch size) that a single linear model cannot represent. It takes four inputs and returns one number.

$$T = \mathcal{M}(E, KV, B, F)$$

Here $E$ is the engine size (the tensor-parallel degree of the served instance), $KV$ is the projected KV occupancy from Equation 2, $B$ is the projected batch size, $F$ is the candidate GPU frequency, and $T$ is the predicted throughput in iterations per second. The presence of $F$ as an explicit input is what makes the model a control tool rather than a monitor: the controller evaluates $\mathcal{M}$ repeatedly at different values of $F$ to discover the throughput-versus-frequency curve for the current load.

5.2 Why a gradient-boosted tree

Gradient boosting builds its predictor additively: it starts from a constant prediction and then fits each new shallow tree to the residual — the gap between the current ensemble's output and the measured throughput — so that every tree specializes in the error the previous trees could not capture. The plain idea is that a sum of many weak, depth-limited trees approximates a smooth nonlinear surface far better than any single tree, while each tree stays cheap to evaluate. It is chosen here over a neural network because the input is only four-dimensional and the training set is a modest characterization sweep, a regime where boosted trees fit accurately without the data volume a network would demand; it is chosen over linear regression because the throughput surface saturates and its features interact, which a linear model cannot represent. Its weakness is that a tree ensemble extrapolates poorly outside the sampled envelope, so the model is only trusted inside the frequency and load range covered by the sweep, and the controller's binary search is confined to that same [210, 1410] MHz interval.

The condensed sketch below shows the training loop and the per-iteration inference call as the controller uses them. Training fits the ensemble on the characterization sweep; inference is the single millisecond-scale evaluation the controller invokes for each candidate frequency during admission and throttling.

# Training: fit M on the characterization sweep
X = features(E, KV, B, F)   # 4-D inputs from the sweep
y = measured_ips                 # target: iterations/sec
M = GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=..., max_depth=3)
M.fit(X, y)                     # each tree fits the running residual

# Inference: called once per candidate frequency, ~3 ms on CPU
def predict_ips(E, KV, B, F):
    return M.predict([[E, KV, B, F]])[0]   # off the GPU critical path

Because each inference is a handful of shallow-tree traversals on the CPU, the controller can afford to evaluate the model at every step of a binary search over frequency without perturbing the decode loop running on the GPU. This cheapness is what makes the counterfactual query practical inside a live serving system.

5.3 From throughput to a deadline test

The performance model returns throughput, but the SLO is stated as a deadline, so the controller converts one into the other by integrating predicted speed over the request's remaining iterations. The time between tokens at iteration $j$ is the reciprocal of the predicted throughput there, and the remaining time until a request finishes is the cumulative sum of those per-iteration times from the current iteration $k$ to the iteration $j$ at which the request will complete.

(3) $$\hat{TR}_j = \sum_{i=k}^{j} T'_i = \sum_{i=k}^{j} \frac{1}{T_i}, \qquad \forall q_i : \hat{TR}_l + t_{cur} < t_{dead}(q_i)$$

The right-hand condition is the end-to-end SLO test: for every request, the estimated remaining time to its completion iteration $l$, added to the current wall-clock time $t_{cur}$, must fall before its deadline $t_{dead}$. Because $T_i$ depends on the candidate frequency through the performance model, lowering the frequency raises every $T'_i$ and pushes the projected finish time later; the controller's task is to find the lowest frequency at which this inequality still holds for all active requests. This makes the deadline test directly differentiable with respect to the knob the controller actually turns.

6. Frequency Control, Admission, and Autoscaling

With a model that predicts throughput at any candidate frequency and a deadline test built on it, the remaining problem is a search: find the cheapest clock that keeps every promise. throttLL'eM solves this with a per-iteration binary search, guards it with an admission controller that prevents over-commitment, and stretches the energy savings further with an instance-level autoscaler. This section covers all three.

6.1 Binary search for the minimum frequency

Throughput rises monotonically with frequency, so the set of frequencies that satisfy the deadline test forms a contiguous upper interval; the cheapest acceptable clock is its lower boundary. A monotone interval is the textbook setting for binary search, which finds that boundary in a logarithmic number of model evaluations rather than scanning all 80 discrete frequency steps. The controller searches the supported range from the GPU's idle floor to its boost ceiling in 15 MHz increments.

The procedure below is a condensed sketch of the per-iteration decision. It expresses the search in terms of the deadline test of Equation 3, evaluated through the performance model at each trial frequency.

# Condensed: per-iteration frequency selection (body simplified)
def select_frequency(scoreboard, E, F_min=210, F_max=1410):
    KV, B = project(scoreboard)        # Eq. 1-2, future load vectors
    lo, hi = F_min, F_max
    while hi - lo > STEP:               # STEP = 15 MHz
        F = (lo + hi) // 2
        T = M(E, KV, B, F)            # predicted IPS at trial clock
        if slo_satisfied(T, scoreboard):  # Eq. 3 holds for all q_i
            hi = F                    # this clock works -> try lower
        else:
            lo = F                    # too slow -> need higher clock
    return hi                          # lowest SLO-satisfying frequency

The condensed routine omits the boundary handling and the bypass path, but the deep behavior worth noting is the fallback: if any request has already fallen behind its deadline — what the paper terms a lost request — the search is skipped entirely and the GPU is driven to its boost frequency, since at that point the only useful action is to recover as fast as the hardware permits. Energy minimization is suspended precisely when correctness is at risk, which keeps the optimizer from ever trading an SLO violation for joules.

6.2 Admission control via a virtual scoreboard

Before a queued request is admitted, the scheduler must confirm that adding it will not push any existing request past its deadline or exceed the KV-cache capacity. It does this by speculatively appending the candidate to a copy of the scoreboard — the virtual scoreboard — and re-running the projection and deadline test on that hypothetical state. If every active request, including the candidate, still clears its KV-capacity check, its per-token deadline, and its end-to-end deadline, the request is admitted; otherwise it remains queued. This what-if evaluation lets the system reason about the consequences of a decision before committing to it.

6.3 Instance autoscaling

Frequency throttling lowers the energy of a fixed instance, but a second lever is how many instances of what size to run at all. Every ten seconds the autoscaler estimates the minimum tensor-parallel degree — chosen from 1, 2, 4, or 8 — that can absorb the current request rate, and provisions accordingly. Because spinning up a new instance takes more than twenty seconds, the system uses shadow instancing to prepare a replacement ahead of demand and a grace period before downscaling, so that brief lulls do not trigger a teardown that would have to be reversed moments later. The autoscaler and the throttling controller compound: scaling sets the coarse capacity, and throttling trims the residual slack within whatever instances are running.

7. Evaluation

The framework is evaluated on a p4d.24xlarge AWS instance with eight NVIDIA A100 40 GB GPUs, serving Llama3-8B, Llama2-13B at three tensor-parallel degrees, and Llama3-70B, replaying an Azure LLM inference trace against the NVIDIA Triton server as the baseline. The two questions the evaluation must answer are whether the energy savings are real and whether they come at the cost of the SLOs they were supposed to respect. The figures below address both.

One caveat qualifies these evaluation results. The end-to-end deadline was satisfied in all configurations except the most resource-constrained one, Llama2-13B at TP1, where there is simply not enough headroom to both serve the trace and lower the clock. The per-token deadlines were met everywhere. The savings are therefore contingent on the configuration actually possessing slack — on a system already saturated at the boost clock, there is no margin to reclaim.

8. Relevance to Our Predictor

We selected throttLL'eM because it is the cleanest published delimiter of our own contribution. Our work is a per-step vLLM serving-latency predictor that folds the GPU streaming-multiprocessor clock into the latency model as a hidden, involuntary, input-token-driven term. throttLL'eM and our predictor both place the GPU clock at the center of an iteration-level model, and both rely on the same physical fact — that decode speed depends jointly on batch size, KV occupancy, and frequency. The difference is the direction of causation between the controller and the clock, and that difference is precisely the boundary of our novelty.

8.1 The clock as a set knob versus a taken-away quantity

In throttLL'eM the GPU frequency $F$ is a decision variable that the system writes. It enters the performance model $\mathcal{M}(E, KV, B, F)$ as a known input under the controller's command: the binary search proposes a value of $F$, the model predicts the throughput that would result, and the chosen $F$ is then applied to the hardware. The clock is something the framework knows because it is the thing the framework sets. Our predictor inverts this. We do not set the clock; we predict the clock the hardware will involuntarily impose as a function of the input load. In our measurements the SM clock falls by 19–31% — from boost down toward a throttled steady state — as the input-token count M rises and the die heats, with a clock-versus-temperature correlation of $r = -0.93$ to $-0.98$ across H100, H200, A100-80, and Blackwell parts. The clock is therefore not an input we command but a latent output we must estimate, and it must be modeled as a function $f(M)$ of the offered load before latency can be predicted at all.

8.2 Where the two views meet, and where ours extends

throttLL'eM operates in a regime where the SXM-class cards under light decode serving stay pinned at their boost clock, so for it the clock is whatever the controller writes and the thermal droop never appears. Our regime is the complement: under compute-bound, high-M, prefill-heavy load the same cards leave boost involuntarily, and a latency predictor that assumes a fixed clock — as throttLL'eM's model implicitly does on the input side, since it treats F as commanded — will misestimate the iteration time. We acknowledge that the droop mechanism itself and the learnability of latency-from-clock are not new; throttLL'eM demonstrates the latter convincingly with its R² above 0.97. Our contribution is narrower and specific: modeling the involuntary droop as a function of the input-token count $M$, $f(M)$, and embedding that term inside a general serving-latency predictor so that the clock the hardware takes away is anticipated rather than discovered after the fact.

The distinction can be stated in a single sentence: throttLL'eM predicts performance in order to choose a clock the system sets, whereas we predict the clock the system never chose, because the thermal controller took it away as the input load rose.
