Infrastructure-Level Acceleration for Multi-Agent Systems

Kernels, KV-cache mechanisms, and serving systems for LLM multi-agent workloads — and what survives when the topology is decided per query. Two independent research passes (GPT-5.6-Sol ultra + Claude Fable), 56 papers verified against primary sources.

56 verified papers 2 independent researchers 2026-07-21

1. Two research passes, one question

This page answers a question the parent survey left open: which papers attack multi-agent systems from the infrastructure side — GPU kernels, KV-cache mechanisms, serving and scheduling systems — and what happens to that machinery when the topology is dynamic, decided per query or per round? To de-risk the answer, two researchers ran the identical brief independently and blind to each other: GPT-5.6-Sol at ultra reasoning effort, and a Claude Fable agent. Together they verified 56 distinct papers against primary sources (arXiv pages, venue sites, official repos); their overlap was large — the same core systems, the same layer taxonomy, and the same two dynamic-native papers surfaced on both sides — which is itself evidence the map below is stable. Where they disagreed on a venue, the stronger primary evidence was taken and the one unresolved case is flagged in the table.

The framing that organizes everything: the prompt/topology literature (GPTSwarm, AgentPrune, MaAS, AFlow) optimizes which agents talk to whom; the infrastructure literature optimizes what the GPU does when they talk. Four layers cover the field — cache mechanisms (reuse and repair of attention state across positions, agents, and models), kernels (attention implementations that monetize shared prefixes at memory-bandwidth level), serving and scheduling (engines that see agentic structure), and workload characterization (the measurements that justify all of it). The decisive axis throughout is dynamic-topology readiness, graded on a four-step ladder: no (a schema, DAG, batch, or communication pattern must be supplied in advance), bounded (prediction works within a known agent inventory or recurring trace grammar), reactive (helps only after a call or prefix has materialized), and yes — the technique consumes structure that is selected or revealed at runtime.

2. The KV-cache layer: reuse, repair, and cache-level communication

The generic lineage answers one technical question with increasing precision: how much of a cache computed under one context survives under another, and what minimal repair restores the rest? Prompt Cache (MLSys 2024) splices precomputed modules but needs a hand-written schema; CacheBlend (EuroSys 2025 Best Paper) reuses non-prefix chunks and selectively recomputes the ~10-15% of tokens with the largest attention deviation (2.2-3.3x TTFT); EPIC (ICML 2025) makes caching position-independent by repairing only chunk-head tokens; KVLink (NeurIPS 2025) trains link tokens to restore cross-document attention; and the 2026 wave sharpens selection — ProphetKV shows query-irrelevant but globally-salient tokens crowd out recompute budgets, and Kamera proves naive position-independent reuse silently halves multi-hop accuracy, then repairs it with a training-free low-rank conditioning patch that makes reuse genuinely order-invariant.

The MAS-specific line then moves the cache across agent and model boundaries. DroidSpeak (NSDI 2026) shares KV across fine-tuned variants of one architecture by recomputing only profiled critical layers (3.1x prefill); KVCOMM (NeurIPS 2025) attacks the offset-variance problem head-on — the same content sits at different depths under each agent's prefix — with an online anchor pool that linearly re-aligns reused caches (over 70% reuse, up to 7.8x TTFT in a five-agent graph); its near-namesake KVComm (ICLR 2026) instead selects which sender layers to transmit (30% of layers suffice); Cache-to-Cache (ICLR 2026) trains a per-pair projector that fuses one model's cache directly into another's, replacing text messages entirely (2.5x latency); RelayCaching (ICML 2026) extends reuse to decode-time KV of upstream agents (over 80% reuse, 4.7x TTFT); LatentMAS goes furthest — agents exchange latent working memory and only the final agent verbalizes (70.8-83.7% fewer output tokens, 4x faster). TokenDance and ForkKV then handle the many-agent memory blow-up: collective one-shot reuse per round with block-sparse sibling diffs (17.5x less per-agent KV), and copy-on-write cache forking for LoRA-specialized agent fleets (3.0x throughput).

The recurring catch. Every cross-boundary mechanism needs to know the pair: DroidSpeak profiles sender-receiver offline, C2C trains a projector per edge, KVComm calibrates per pair, KVCOMM's anchors presume recurring prefix structure. A per-query topology that invents a new edge at runtime has no profile, no projector, and no anchor history — the exact regime dynamic MAS produces.
Three reuse regimes, three different problems: exact prefix reuse is free; non-prefix chunk reuse must repair position and cross-chunk conditioning; decode-state reuse must re-anchor state that was generated behind a different prefix. Every paper in this layer is a position in this diagram.

2.1 The contrasts that matter in the KV layer

CacheBlend versus EPIC is the founding trade of this layer, and ProphetKV supplies the verdict numbers. CacheBlend is assembly-aware: after the actual chunks and query are concatenated it computes layer 1 fully, ranks tokens by attention-map deviation, and repairs the worst ~10-20% — so it can follow damage anywhere, but pays the selection cost on every new assembly. EPIC refuses to look: it repairs a fixed first-k tokens at every chunk boundary, which is faster (up to 8x TTFT; EPIC measures CacheBlend's selection layer alone at 16-64% of its TTFT) but cannot ask which token matters to this question. ProphetKV prices the difference at an identical 20% recompute budget: on multi-value cross-chunk binding, full prefill and ProphetKV score 100, CacheBlend 73.5, EPIC 50.5 — static boundary repair loses exactly where cross-chunk reasoning lives. KVLink dissolves the choice by training the model itself (link tokens reconnect documents; -96% TTFT) — but the checkpoint, mask, and link vocabulary become a package, so a MAS that swaps models per query cannot adopt it without retraining each one.

The phrase KV communication hides three different mechanisms, and separating them is the single most valuable thing a reader can take from this layer. KVCOMM (anchors) reconstructs what the receiver's prefill would have produced: deterministic RoPE re-rotation for position, plus an interpolated contextual offset drawn from previously observed anchors — training-free, over 70% reuse, 7.8x TTFT, but its own ablation shows the anchor premise is sharp: loosening the match threshold moves GSM8K from 83.1 accuracy at 34% reuse to 78.8 at 98% reuse. KVComm (layers) reconstructs nothing: the receiver simply attends to a calibrated 30-70% subset of the sender's layers — 2.5-6x less compute, but the subset is fixed per pair. Cache-to-Cache is learned fusion across genuinely different model families — and its ablation is the deepest insight: projection alone scores 20.7, learned fusion 44.9, gating 48.0, meaning what crosses the boundary is semantics, not a numerical cache patch; a bad sender can inject wrong meaning, not just noise. DroidSpeak sits between: same-architecture fine-tune pairs, ~11% of layers recomputed by offline profile — deployable without touching weights, but the O(L²) profiling (~3 hours per pair) is exactly what a dynamically assembled team cannot amortize. RelayCaching is the most MAS-native textual object of all: it reuses the upstream agent's decode-time KV — state that already exists for free — re-anchoring it inside the next agent's prompt (over 80% reuse, 4.7x TTFT), and its ablation proves numerical deviation alone is the wrong selector: deviation-only repair scores 79.7 where the full influence-aware selector scores 84.8. LatentMAS abandons text entirely — agents pass latent working memory and only the last verbalizes (4x faster, 71-84% fewer tokens) — which is communication redesign, not cache repair, and its ordered-accumulation assumption is precisely what an arbitrary live topology violates.

The KVCOMM concept, drawn precisely: position error is fixable by a deterministic RoPE re-rotation, but conditioning error is not deterministic — it must be interpolated from previously observed anchor offsets. A prefix with no nearby anchor is the failure case, and per-query topologies manufacture exactly such prefixes.

3. The kernel layer: shared-prefix economics

One level down, kernels monetize sharing at memory-bandwidth granularity, and the mechanism is uniform: read the shared region's KV once for the whole batch instead of once per sequence. Hydragen decomposes attention into shared-prefix and unique-suffix parts, turning matrix-vector into matrix-matrix work (up to 32x decode throughput as batch and prefix grow); RelayAttention reads a long system prompt exactly once per batch (2.2x sustainable request rate); ChunkAttention detects shared prefixes at runtime in a prefix tree (3.2-4.8x kernel speedup); FlashInfer (MLSys 2025 Best Paper) provides the composable substrate — cascade inference over multi-level shared prefixes — that vLLM and SGLang build on; and SGLang's RadixAttention discovers sharing reactively in a radix tree over completed requests (6.4x throughput on agentic workloads).

The verdict both researchers reached independently: these are not MAS-aware kernels, and tolerating dynamic arrivals is not the same as using a dynamic communication graph. Radix trees and runtime chunk detection survive unpredictable request streams — but only after exact token prefixes exist; Hydragen and RelayAttention need the sharing group declared in advance; and a re-sampled topology shrinks the guaranteed-shared region toward zero, because which context lands in which agent's prompt is reshuffled per query. The kernels are ready to exploit structure; nothing tells them the structure.

The one mathematical idea behind every kernel in this layer: attention over [shared S | private c] can be split exactly, merged exactly via log-sum-exp rescaling, and the shared part batched so S is read once — no approximation anywhere. What differs across papers is only the contract for knowing S.

3.1 Kernel contrasts a reader should be able to reproduce

Hydragen versus RelayAttention: the mathematics is the same exact trick — split the softmax domain, keep each part's log-sum-exp, merge exactly — so the real difference is the contract. Hydragen accepts an arbitrary, manually declared prefix hierarchy and recurses over it (32x decode throughput at large batch, but its own figures show suffix growth eroding the gain); RelayAttention hardwires one production pattern — a single long system prompt — and integrates into vLLM, reading system KV from DRAM exactly once per batch (2.2x sustainable request rate, no benefit at batch 1, and the paper notes the benefit fades once private context reaches about twice the system-prompt length). ChunkAttention versus RadixAttention: both draw a prefix tree, but ChunkAttention's tree is a kernel data structure over the ACTIVE batch — a shared chunk becomes one attention operation, and chunks vanish when requests finish (3.2-4.8x kernel speedup, but a request that completed a second ago contributes nothing) — while RadixAttention's tree is a persistent runtime cache that remembers completed calls and orders future requests for hits (deployed hit rates 52-74%), yet ordinary decode still rereads a shared node for every active query. They compose: the radix tree supplies the live hierarchy, a chunk/cascade kernel exploits it within each batch — FlashInfer is exactly that composable middle, 16.1x faster than a single-format kernel at prefix 32K/batch 64 but only 1.03x at prefix 1K/batch 16, which is the honest scaling story.

4. The serving layer: engines that see agents

Serving systems climbed an awareness ladder over three years. Parrot (OSDI 2024) let applications declare inter-request dataflow via semantic variables (up to 11.7x on multi-agent apps); InferCept (ICML 2024) made tool-call interceptions first-class, preserving computed context instead of re-prefilling — it measured the stake: 37-40% of forwarding time was interception waste; Autellix (NSDI 2026, published as Agentix) schedules agentic programs non-clairvoyantly from their call history — no DAG needed — for 4-15x program throughput; JITServe (NSDI 2026, formerly Tempo) allocates goodput under imprecise request information; and Murakkab (OSDI 2026) remaps declared workflows onto models and hardware under SLOs (2.8x GPU reduction). On the cache side of serving, KVFlow (NeurIPS 2025) replaced LRU with steps-to-execution eviction and background prefetch over an Agent Step Graph (1.83-2.19x) — but assumes that graph is supplied; TokenCake partitions GPU memory by workflow-critical-path priority (-47% latency); Continuum pins KV with tool-duration TTLs; CONCUR treats the cache as a congested resource and admission-controls agents; SMetric routes sessions cache-affinely off a production finding that agent traffic shares over 80% of tokens.

4.1 Serving contrasts a reader should be able to reproduce

Parrot versus Autellix is the declared-versus-inferred axis in its purest form. Parrot changes how applications are written so the engine receives exact value dependencies and the end-to-end objective before execution — foresight that yields 11.7x on MetaGPT-style apps, at the price of an intrusive programming contract, and with LLM-created dynamic nodes explicitly out of scope. Autellix assumes ordinary dynamic programs and thin IDs: its PLAS/ATLAS scheduler ranks programs by attained critical-path service, needing no future graph at all — the most dynamic-compatible stance in the layer — but it is history-only, cannot prefetch the next call, and its own evaluation shows a visible gap to a clairvoyant scheduler. KVFlow versus Continuum versus TokenCake is the cache-side triangle: KVFlow converts a KNOWN step graph into exact eviction distances and prefetch (1.83-2.19x, but on a realistic mixed workload only 1.08-1.12x — the honest number); Continuum needs no graph, protecting the just-finished turn with an economically priced TTL (up to 8.18x on SWE-agent, but its sequential-ReAct assumption breaks on parallel fan-in); TokenCake couples both timescales — graph-criticality memory reservation plus tool-gap migration — and thereby carries the largest prediction hazard: with tool-duration noise at 0.25, its -14.8% improvement becomes a +8.3% regression, the sharpest quantified warning in this literature that foresight helps only when it is right. InferCept sits below all three as the reactive floor: it minimizes waste at the moment of a pause (0.69% residual GPU waste) but prices neither the future nor the graph — correct, composable, and blind.

5. Dynamic MAS × infrastructure: the direct answers

Until mid-2025 the honest answer was nothing. As of this pass, both researchers independently surfaced the same two dynamic-native systems, and the Sol pass added four more from 2026. PBKV states the gap verbatim — prior work assumes each workflow calls a static sequence of agents, while practical workflows are dynamic, the invoked sequence depending on each task's context — and answers it with a GraphSAGE predictor fusing the historical call graph with the current prefill embedding to forecast invocations several steps ahead, driving conservative eviction and prefetch: 1.85x over LRU on dynamic workflows, and 1.26x over KVFlow on KVFlow's own static turf. Pythia mines recurring path grammars from production traces and adds speculative template prefill, lookahead scheduling, and phase-aware autoscaling (2.9x lower job completion time). Around them: CacheSage (the Policy-Runtime-Layer case study) learns agent transitions online and prefetches the predicted next agent's anchor (+13-37 points of hit rate); Nalar converts runtime agent calls into futures so dependencies reveal incrementally (-34-74% tail latency); HexAGenT schedules a revealed DAG across heterogeneous GPUs with cache-transfer costs in the objective; SAGA infers execution graphs from traces for TTL, prefetch, and affinity batching (1.64x, 99.2% SLO attainment).

Yet every one of these is prediction or reaction, and that is the finding worth italicizing: they infer what the topology optimizer already knows. When GPTSwarm samples an edge set, when MaAS's controller draws a sub-architecture, when AFlow's search commits a workflow — the graph exists in the optimizer's memory milliseconds before dispatch. No published interface exports it to the serving engine; PBKV rebuilds it statistically from history instead. The one paper that names this seam is the Policy-Driven Runtime Layer position paper: the framework knows agent identities and roles but sees no engine events; the engine sees every event but knows no agents.

The information ladder: the same workflow, known at four different times. Reactive radix trees see only arrived prefixes; KVFlow-class systems get a declared static graph; PBKV/Pythia forecast it with learned or mined predictors; the rightmost panel — the live, sampled per-query graph with dead branches crossed out — already exists inside GPTSwarm/MaAS-class optimizers, and no published interface exports it.

5.1 PBKV versus Pythia: two ways to predict what could be told

PBKV asks a query-conditioned, short-horizon question: for THIS request, which agents run in the next one to three calls? A two-layer GraphSAGE over the admissible call graph, fused with the current prompt's prefill embedding, answers it — and its own accuracy curve (0.935 at horizon 1, falling to 0.771 at horizon 3) explains its conservative design: prediction drives eviction ranking and prefetch into free memory only, never displacing active state. Pythia asks a workload-level question instead: what regular language describes this application's paths? It prunes transitions below 5% frequency and keeps whole dominant path grammars — a coarser but longer lever, coordinating cache, scheduling, and autoscaling at once, with the largest single measured payoff in the corpus: keep/drop plus prefetch takes a Decomposer agent's TTFT from 4.1 s to 55 ms. The failure modes are mirror images: PBKV is bounded by its known agent inventory (an out-of-graph agent is out of vocabulary), Pythia by grammar stability (rare paths are deliberately dropped; adversarial or open-ended workflows collapse to an unhelpful mesh). Both are approximations to the same unavailable input — the sampled graph itself.

6. What the workloads actually show

Four numbers anchor the economics. SMetric's production traces (Alibaba BAILIAN coding agents) measure over 80% token-level reuse potential in agent traffic versus 54-62% for chat — the best public sharing-ratio figure. Pythia's production analysis finds realized prefix-hit rates are nonetheless low when agentic traffic is served generically — the delta between potential and realized sharing is precisely the serving layer's ignorance of structure. InferCept prices interception waste at 37-40% of forwarding time; TokenDance measures All-Gather round redundancy as 11-17x compressible. Character studies add the shape: agentic execution is decode-dominated once caching works (2605.26297), coding-agent sessions run long with heavy-tailed tool calls (TraceLab's 4,300-session open trace), and provider-scale reuse is skewed and predictable per category (KVCache in the Wild). What no trace captures — both researchers confirmed independently — is per-round graph creation: a public dynamic-topology MAS serving trace does not exist.

8. The gap, stated precisely — and five open problems

Superimposing the two reports gives the gap its sharpest form. The infrastructure literature has solved static-structure sharing (kernels, prefix trees, declared DAGs) and is converging on statistically predictable dynamism (PBKV, Pythia, SAGA, CacheSage — all 2026). The still-empty cell is a joint system in which the query creates a new team, edge, or round topology and the runtime participates: using cache locality, KV compatibility, queue state, and transfer cost while the structure is being chosen — or at minimum, being told the structure the moment it is chosen. Four recurring failure modes keep the cell empty: the future graph is assumed or bounded (KVFlow, TokenCake, PBKV's known inventory); KV compatibility is narrower than topology freedom (per-pair projectors and profiles); kernel gains require realized regularity (declared sharing groups, synchronized rounds); and the measurements omit the decisive fields (no trace ties graph evolution to cache keys and SLOs).

1 · Topology-conditioned cache planning

The orchestrator samples the graph milliseconds before dispatch. Export the sampled edge set — or the sampler's edge marginals — as prefetch, eviction, and placement hints. PBKV infers what GPTSwarm already knows; the missing artifact is the interface, not the information.

2 · Multi-parent KV merge with bounded error

A DAG-round agent consumes k predecessor caches computed under k different prefixes. No work characterizes error accumulation over multi-hop, multi-parent merges or gives a repair budget that bounds downstream drift — Kamera's low-rank residue is the closest primitive.

3 · Budgeted speculative prefill

Prefetch moves existing caches; nobody speculatively prefills the probable next agent's not-yet-materialized context while the current agent decodes, with wasted-FLOPs priced JITServe-style and cheap cancellation of losing branches — the MAS analogue of speculative decoding.

4 · Cache-economics-aware topology objectives

Make GPTSwarm/AgentPrune/MaAS objectives serving-aware: prune the edge whose removal saves real prefill, keep the edge that rides an existing cache. Needs a differentiable or bandit-style proxy of per-edge cache-hit value; no published attempt exists — and the parent survey's Section 9 reaches the same conclusion from the other side.

5 · A public dynamic-MAS serving trace + benchmark

TraceLab covers coding agents; ServeGen and BurstGPT cover production chat. Nothing captures per-query topology variation, team churn, cache keys, transfers, tool gaps, and SLOs together — so every dynamic claim, including PBKV's, is evaluated on synthetic workflows.

Opportunity 3 drawn as a timeline: the tool gap is long enough to stage and speculatively prefill the probable next branch, gated by confidence and free-memory-only rules, with losing branches cancelled before they touch HBM. Every ingredient has a measured static-side number; only the composition is unbuilt.
