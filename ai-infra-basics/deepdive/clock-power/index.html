The Clock Is Not a Constant

A preliminary measurement note: under sustained projection-GEMM load, the SM clock droops with token count by 20–44% across data-centre GPUs.

n=5 randomised-order, 4 data-centre SKUs Controlled cross-cap causal probe Isolated GEMM telemetry — not end-to-end serving

Llama-3.1-8B projection-GEMM shapes · bf16 · sustained 2.5 s per shape · NVML telemetry

0. Why a hidden clock breaks performance models

Almost every analytical GPU performance model — the roofline, its learned descendants, and the cost functions inside LLM serving simulators — prices a kernel by dividing work by a peak rate, with peak FLOP/s quoted at the boost clock. The unstated assumption is that a compute-bound kernel runs near boost throughout. For short kernels this is harmless; for the sustained matrix multiplications that dominate large-batch prefill, this note shows it is not.

A concrete trace

A single Llama-3.1-8B qkv multiplication, run for 2.5 s on an idle H100 as input tokens $M$ grow from 64 to 16{,}384: at $M=64$ the clock holds at the 1980 MHz boost; as board power climbs to the sustained limit the clock falls, settling near 1380 MHz at $M=16{,}384$. Same kernel, same card, same thermal envelope — only the problem size changed — and the clock dropped roughly 30%.

How much the status quo mis-prices

The error is not a rounding detail: across the four data-centre GPUs measured here, a datasheet-boost roofline over-predicts achieved compute-bound throughput by a mean absolute percentage error of 29.0%, rising to 36.7% in the saturated large-$M$ regime — large enough to invert the capacity and scheduling decisions the model informs.

The obvious fix, and why it is not enough

The standard remedy multiplies the peak rate by one fitted efficiency ceiling $\bar{\eta}$ per GPU. It works numerically but absorbs the droop into an opaque constant that conflates two unrelated effects — the kernel's geometric efficiency and the involuntary frequency the power controller granted. A ceiling fitted on one card's conditions does not transfer to another card, cap, or duty cycle, because the clock component inside it does not transfer.

The fundamental obstacle

The sustained clock is not published by the silicon. It is an involuntary, workload-dependent quantity — a function of token count $M$ (via arithmetic intensity and power draw), board power limit, and thermal state, none on a datasheet. A transferable model must treat the clock as a first-class measured input, not a number folded into a fitted constant. This note does not predict $\fclk$ from first principles (left as future work) but shows that exposing and cheaply measuring it recovers most of the hidden error.

Where this sits relative to prior work

Power and frequency in GPU serving are not new, but prior treatments leave the clock outside the throughput model. POLCA (ASPLOS 2024) caps GPUs to fit a power budget but treats the cap as an external knob; throttLL'eM uses the clock as an energy-saving scheduling knob; Maliakel et al. characterise power-capped inference end-to-end without separating the clock from the clock-independent efficiency; the 'illusion of power capping' line observes clock effects in the memory-bound decode phase. None exposes the clock as an explicit, transferable factor inside a compute-bound GEMM/roofline model — the one thing this note isolates.

1. The decomposition: pull the clock out

The contribution is a single change of variables. Instead of pricing a sustained GEMM by a peak rate times one fitted ceiling, we write the achieved throughput as the datasheet boost rate times two distinct factors: an explicit clock factor carrying the droop, and a clock-independent efficiency carrying everything else.

(1) $$\text{achieved TFLOP/s} \;=\; \Pboost \,\cdot\, \fclk \,\cdot\, \etanoclk, \qquad \fclk \;=\; \frac{f_{\mathrm{sustained}}}{f_{\mathrm{boost}}}$$
$\fclk$
The explicit clock factor — sustained clock ÷ boost clock, a measured input every datasheet-boost model implicitly sets to 1.
×
$\etanoclk$
The clock-independent efficiency — tile/wave quantisation, tensor-pipe duty, memory effects: everything that remains if the clock were pinned.

The point is transferability. $\etanoclk$ is a property of kernel geometry and architecture, largely stable across caps and duty cycles because it describes how work maps onto the machine, not how fast it is allowed to run. $\fclk$ carries the part that changes with the cap and workload. Kept separate, a controlled-condition $\etanoclk$ can be reused while only the cheap clock reading is refreshed; folding both into one ceiling destroys that reuse.

An honest boundary, stated up front. Here $\fclk$ is measured from the sustained run and $\etanoclk$ from a separate pinned-clock pass. Equation (1) is therefore a decomposition and reconstruction that explains where the error goes — not yet a predictor that forecasts the clock from the cap. Section 4 reports how well it closes; Section 5 states what a predictor would need.

2. Measuring the droop: n=5, randomised order, with intervals

A clock that depends on token count, power, and temperature is exactly what a single ordered sweep can fake. To rule out heat accumulation, the full clock-versus-$M$ curve for each GPU was measured five times in randomised $(\text{projection}, M)$ order, with cooldown between cells, aggregated into a median with a 95% confidence interval on clock, power, temperature, and throughput at every point. The droop is thus order-robust, not a sweep-order artefact.

The shape of the curve

Every data-centre card shows the same qualitative shape — a piecewise-decreasing curve with quantised controller fluctuations, not one clean step. At small $M$ the kernel draws little power and the clock sits at boost; as $M$ grows, board power rises to the sustained limit, and the clock must fall to keep power within the cap; at large $M$ it settles at a drooped sustained level.

Why some cards decline and others do not

The droop is universal, but its throughput consequence is not. Achieved throughput is clock × clock-independent efficiency, and as $M$ grows the clock falls while $\etanoclk$ rises. On well-provisioned cards (H100, A100) the rising efficiency outpaces the falling clock and throughput keeps climbing — the droop is real but masked. On a tight-cap card (L40S) the clock falls far enough to overwhelm the efficiency gain and throughput declines ~15% at large $M$, with per-projection droop reaching 54% on the widest projection. The decline is a tight-cap phenomenon; per-projection droop spans ~20–54%.

3. A controlled probe: does the cap set the clock?

The sweeps establish a strong power–clock correlation, but correlation across one sweep cannot prove the cap drives the clock, since token count, power, and temperature move together. To probe causation, the one directly manipulable variable — the power cap — was swept on a fully controlled local consumer RTX 5090, where both the power limit and clock lock are settable. The shared cluster does not grant this control, so this is a controllable-card illustration of the mechanism, deliberately distinct from the data-centre SKUs.

Lowering the cap lowers the sustained clock, monotonically

With the kernel, thermal solution, and duty cycle held fixed, the cap was set to 575, 500, 450, and 400 W in turn and the sustained clock at large $M$ recorded. It tracks the cap monotonically: each cap reduction lowers the sustained clock.

The cap is the manipulated variable with kernel and thermal solution fixed; a sequential cap sweep still shifts temperature and controller history, so this is a controlled illustration, not a fully isolated single-variable manipulation.

An honest null result. On this consumer card throughput did not decline at any cap (peak throughput rose from 199 to 223 TFLOP/s as the cap rose). The RTX 5090 at 575 W is simply not tight-capped enough for the droop to overwhelm the efficiency gain — consistent with, and reinforcing, the finding that the decline is a tight-cap phenomenon (seen on the L40S), not a universal one. With no decline to remove, the clock-lock mediation test was inconclusive on this card; it would need a tight-cap card under direct power control, which the cluster does not provide.

The probe supports a bounded claim: the power cap causally sets the sustained clock. It does not, alone, prove the full cap → clock → throughput-decline chain on the data-centre cards, because the decline could not be exercised on the only directly controllable card. Closing that chain is exactly what a tight-cap controllable card would provide.

4. Does the decomposition close?

A decomposition is useful only if its factors multiply back to the measured throughput. The closure test substitutes the measured $\fclk$ and pinned-clock $\etanoclk$ into Equation (1) and compares the product against independently measured throughput. Two questions: does making the clock explicit reduce error versus the datasheet-boost baseline, and does the reconstruction close exactly?

The explicit clock cuts the error substantially

Against achieved throughput, the datasheet-boost roofline carries 29.0% MAPE; supplying the measured clock cuts it to 9.6%. In the saturated large-$M$ regime, where the boost roofline is worst (36.7%), the explicit-clock reconstruction falls to 3.5%. The clock term is the dominant missing factor in this regime.

But the reconstruction does not close perfectly

Closure is honest about its limits. The two factors come from different measurement contexts — $\fclk$ from the live sustained loop, $\etanoclk$ from a separate Nsight Compute pass at a pinned base clock — and the mismatch leaves a residual. On the H200 it closes to within ~4.8% at large $M$, but on the H100 it is off by as much as 14% even at large $M$ (per-cell closure MAE 15.6%), with residuals up to 26% at small-to-mid $M$. The decomposition is a strong diagnostic — it localises and removes most of the error — but it is not yet an exact predictive model, and is not presented as one.

Reconstruction, not prediction. Every closure number above uses the clock measured on the same run. Turning this into a model that forecasts throughput without first measuring the clock requires predicting $\fclk$ from the cap, the kernel's power profile, and the operating point — motivated by the cross-cap probe but not yet delivered. That is the central future work.

5. Scope, honesty, and what is left

This is a preliminary measurement note, and its boundaries are stated as deliberately as its results. The measurements are isolated, sustained projection-GEMM telemetry on single GPUs — not an end-to-end serving study. The relevance to serving latency (large-batch prefill is exactly this sustained compute-bound regime) is the motivation, not a measured serving result; propagating the droop into a full per-step serving model is future work.

What is solid

  • 20–44% qkv droop on four SKUs, order-robust with tight 95% intervals (n=5 randomised).
  • The L40S −15% throughput decline at large M, a tight-cap phenomenon.
  • Making the clock explicit cuts the boost-roofline MAPE from 29.0% to 9.6%.
  • The cap → clock causal link, shown by a controlled cross-cap sweep.

Stated limitations

  • Eq. (1) is reconstruction with a measured clock, not a predictor; closure is imperfect (H100 off ~14% at large M).
  • Decline mediation is untested: needs a tight-cap card under direct power control, unavailable on the shared cluster.
  • One device per SKU; the clock is an NVML median over a 2.5 s window, an approximation to the throughput-exact time-weighted clock.
  • The Max-Q RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell is an exploratory single-pass observation only, not a co-equal n=5 result.

Taken together, the note makes one defensible claim and is careful not to exceed it: under sustained projection-GEMM load the SM clock droops materially and measurably with token count; a datasheet-boost model hides the droop inside a fitted ceiling; and exposing the clock as an explicit factor recovers most of the error while remaining honest that the clock is, for now, measured rather than predicted.
